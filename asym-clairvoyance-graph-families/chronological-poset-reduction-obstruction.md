# Why a chronological online chain lower bound does not reduce to adaptive TSP

## Verdict

The proposed reduction is invalid unless chronology is proved inside the
metric.  In adaptive stochastic TSP the policy chooses the next client to
call; there is no exogenous arrival order.  Permanent guards, stochastic
gates, and ordinary algebraic lifts do not supply the missing chronology.
The obstruction is that a client has one service event but remains reusable
as a transit vertex.  Hence one guard can constrain at most the one
active-call chain containing its service event.

This note makes the reduction and the guard obstruction exact.  The argument
does not use small examples.

## 1. The reachability metric really is chain cover

Let \(P\) be a finite graded poset of height \(L\), with rank function
\(\rho\).  Fix \(0<\varepsilon<1/L\).  On the clients of \(P\), put
\[
 d_\varepsilon(x,y)=
 \begin{cases}
   \varepsilon(\rho(y)-\rho(x)),&x<y,\\
   1,&x\not<y,\ x\ne y,\\
   0,&x=y.
 \end{cases}
\tag{1.1}
\]
Add a depot \(r\) with
\[
             d(r,x)=d(x,r)=1.
\tag{1.2}
\]
Equivalently, use length-\(\varepsilon\) cover arcs, unit arcs between all
ordered pairs, and take directed shortest-path closure.  Equations
(1.1)--(1.2) are the resulting directed metric.

For an ordering \(x_1,\ldots,x_N\) of an active set, split the order into
its \(K\) maximal increasing runs.  If run \(j\) starts at rank \(s_j\) and
ends at rank \(t_j\), its closed-depot cost is
\[
       2+(K-1)+\varepsilon\sum_{j=1}^K(t_j-s_j).
\tag{1.3}
\]
Therefore, uniformly over all realizations,
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \operatorname {OPT}_{\rm post}(A)
   &=1+\operatorname {width}(A)+O(\varepsilon L|A|),\\
 \operatorname {cost}_\pi(A)
   &=1+K_\pi(A)+O(\varepsilon L|A|),
\end{aligned}
\tag{1.4}
\]
where \(K_\pi(A)\) is the number of increasing runs in the active-call
order generated by policy \(\pi\).  Thus the metric part of the reduction is
sound.

What is not sound is replacing \(K_\pi\) by the chain count of an online
algorithm under a prescribed chronological arrival order.

## 2. Controlled revelation is not online arrival

In an online chain-partition problem, an external process reveals the next
element.  In adaptive TSP, at every state \((u,S)\), the policy itself
chooses any \(v\in S\) to call.  If \(v\) is inactive, the policy learns
that fact without moving.  If it is active, \(v\) becomes the next element
of the active-call order.

Consequently a chronological online lower bound applies only after proving
an exchange lemma of the following strength:

> every call outside the next chronological bank either can be moved back
> into chronological order without helping the policy, or incurs a new
> reset that cannot be shared with any other out-of-order call.

Neither assertion follows from reachability.  A policy may query an entire
descendant bank from its current active vertex.  All inactive answers are
free, and the first active descendant continues the same increasing run.
The high-girth descendant calculation in
`multilayer-path-cover-audit.md` shows that this continuation can succeed
with probability \(1-\exp(-\Theta(d))\).

There is an even more elementary diagnostic.  For an ordinal sum of
antichains
\[
             A_1\oplus\cdots\oplus A_L,
\]
an arbitrary externally imposed order can have a very poor online chain
count.  The adaptive policy is nevertheless realization-wise optimal: use
repeated increasing sweeps, taking the first active uncalled element in
each layer.  The number of sweeps is exactly
\(\max_i|A_i\cap A|\), the posterior width.  Hence an online lower bound
for a chosen presentation order says nothing about the adaptive value even
when the poset has many stages.

## 3. A guard has only one unit of chronological capacity

Call an active client \(g\) a *service guard* for a pass if the claimed
chronological proof uses the service event at \(g\) between calls from the
earlier and later sides of a stage cut.

### Lemma 1 (one-use guard)

One client \(g\) can be a service guard for at most one increasing run of
an execution.

### Proof

Every client is called exactly once.  If \(g\) is active, its unique service
event occurs in one active-call run.  Every later metric move may pass
through \(g\), but transit through a called or uncalled client is not a
second call and creates no service event.  Thus no other run contains a
service event at \(g\). \(\square\)

This is precisely where a switching-network analogy breaks: a permanent
vertex is not a consumable token.  Shortest paths may reuse it arbitrarily
often after its one call.

### Theorem 2 (guard-capacity lower bound)

Suppose a proposed chronological reduction claims that \(k\) simultaneous
passes across a stage cut are each protected by a service guard.  Then it
requires at least \(k\) distinct active guard clients at that cut.

If those guards are pairwise incomparable, every realization containing
them has posterior width at least \(k\).  If they are comparable and can be
put in fewer than \(k\) chains, then they cannot protect \(k\) independent
passes: after serving their fewer guard chains, all subsequent passes may
reuse the guard vertices only as transit.

### Proof

The first statement is Lemma 1 applied to the \(k\) runs.  A chain contains
at most one member of an antichain, proving the width bound.  In the
comparable case, serve each chain of guards in increasing order.  Once their
service events have occurred, later paths can traverse the same vertices
without producing a guarded chronological event.  Hence the number of
guarded passes is at most the chain-cover number of the active guards, not
their number of later transit occurrences. \(\square\)

Thus permanent gate antichains buy chronological capacity only by adding
the same capacity to the posterior chain baseline.  They cannot be treated
as zero-cost resources that every causal pass must consume.

## 4. Stochastic gates do not repair the theorem

Replace a permanent guard \(g\) by an independently active stochastic gate.
If it is inactive, calling it causes no movement and it cannot impose a
position in the active-call order.  If it is active, Lemma 1 applies and the
posterior must also serve it.  For a gate bank \(G\), the total service-guard
capacity is therefore at most
\[
                      |G\cap A|.
\tag{4.1}
\]
When the bank is an antichain, its contribution to posterior width is also
at least \(|G\cap A|\).

Correlating all copies of a gate so that exactly one chosen lane is active
would mimic a consumable switch, but that is outside the independent-client
model.  With independent copies, every active copy is a required client,
including copies on lanes the posterior does not use.

## 5. Algebraic and voltage lifts have the same obstruction

Consider an \(h\)-sheet lift of a guarded stage.

* If the \(h\) lifted guards are permanent and incomparable across sheets,
  their antichain gives posterior width at least \(h\).
* If lift arcs make the guards comparable, they can be served in fewer guard
  chains; after that, all sheets reuse them as transit and the claimed
  \(h\)-fold chronological capacity disappears.
* If only the guard on a hidden sheet is intended to be active, the lift
  needs correlated state-dependent activation.  Independent activations
  instead require service of active guards on every sheet.
* A voltage label carried by the current walk is not hidden state.  The
  metric is fixed and known, and a call names a concrete lifted client.
  Shortest paths can change sheets through all deterministic lift arcs
  regardless of which clients are active.

Therefore an ordinary lift changes neither the one-use lemma nor the
capacity/width tradeoff.

## 6. Why local stage deficits cannot simply be summed

Suppose a causal matching decision at stage \(i\) leaves \(b_i\) unmatched
active vertices.  Transitive reachability lets these vertices connect to
later layers, possibly after skipping stage \(i+1\).  A single later chain
can therefore repair deficits attributed to several interfaces.  Charging
\(\sum_i b_i\) counts the same eventual chain start more than once.

Permanent intermediate gates block a skip only for the guard chains that
still have uncalled guard clients.  By Theorem 2, maintaining this protection
for \(k\) passes at every stage already installs width \(k\) at every cut.
Extra causal chains beyond that protected capacity are unguarded and can
batch their repairs through transitive descendants.

This explains simultaneously:

1. why the height-two matching reduction is exact but too weak;
2. why repeating it across layers does not multiply the deficit; and
3. why a high-girth lift makes descendant probing easier rather than harder.

## 7. Exact status of the proposed reduction

The following implication is valid:
\[
\boxed{\text{metric cost}\ \longleftrightarrow\
       \text{number of chains in the policy's own active-call order}.}
\tag{7.1}
\]
The following implication is false without a new metric lemma:
\[
\boxed{\text{policy's active-call order}\ \longleftrightarrow\
       \text{a prescribed chronological online arrival order}.}
\tag{7.2}
\]

Service guards, stochastic gates, and ordinary algebraic lifts do not prove
(7.2).  They either have insufficient one-use capacity, raise posterior
width by the same amount, or require forbidden correlated activations.

A repair would need a genuinely consumable geometric resource whose use in
one chain changes the future metric available to other chains.  A fixed
directed metric has no such state: vertices and arcs remain available for
transit after service.  Consequently the chronological-online-chain
reduction, in the stated fixed-poset form, is refuted rather than completed.
