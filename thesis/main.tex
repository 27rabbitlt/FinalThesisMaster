\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

We study the stochastic traveling salesperson problem, where each client is active
independently according to a given probability and all active clients must be
served at minimum expected travel cost.

There are three common benchmarks that differ in when the active clients are revealed
and when the decisions are  made: an a~posteriori solution knows the complete
active set in advance and decide the tour after active clients are revealed,
an adaptive solution decide the next client to visit based on past information,
and an a~priori solution commits to a fixed tour ordering before any activations are known.

We compare the three benchmarks and give lower bounds for the gap between them.
For clients with symmetric distances, we construct instances with an adaptivity gap approaching~$2$ and
instances with a clairvoyance gap of~$11/10$. For asymmetric distances, we
give a layered-poset construction showing that the clairvoyance gap is at
least~$3/2$, strengthening a recursive family whose ratio approaches~$4/3$.
We also
establish a partial result for asymmetric metrics, whose current construction
is an offline low-cost circulation rather than an adaptive policy.
Finally, for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$, we give a deterministic
$(1{+}\varepsilon)$-approximation algorithm for a~priori TSP with running time
$c_\varepsilon^n\poly(n)$ based on dynamic programming.

\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

Many real-world routing and scheduling problems are subject to uncertainty.
In a delivery service, for example, a courier must plan a route through several customer locations, yet some customers may have no packages to receive or their requests may be canceled.
A classical abstraction is the \emph{stochastic traveling salesperson problem} (stochastic TSP), where each vertex~$v$ comes with a specified probability~$p_v$ and becomes \emph{active} (requires service) independently with probability~$p_v$.
The goal is to find a tour that visits all active vertices and minimizes the expected travel cost, where the cost comes from the underlying metric on vertices.

An important modeling choice is whether the salesperson is allowed to adapt future decisions based on information revealed during the execution of the tour.
In the \emph{nonadaptive} (a~priori) setting, the salesperson commits to a fixed tour before observing the realization; during execution the salesperson follows this tour while shortcutting inactive vertices.
In the \emph{adaptive} setting, decisions may depend on observations revealed along the way, allowing the tour to be adjusted on the fly.
In the \emph{a~posteriori} setting, the salesperson has full knowledge of the realization.

A fundamental question is how much adaptivity can improve performance relative to an optimal nonadaptive tour, and how much full clairvoyance improves over the best adaptive policy.
Note that computing an optimal adaptive policy (or an optimal a~priori tour) for stochastic TSP is computationally hard, since the setting with $p_v=1$ for all~$v$ corresponds to the classical TSP, which is known to be APX-hard~\cite{Karpinski2015}.
Also, describing an optimal adaptive policy might require exponential space.
Thus, we are interested in understanding the separations between the three benchmarks, and in designing policies or algorithms whose expected cost can be provably bounded.

\section{Our contributions}\label{sec:contributions}

We obtain the following results.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Adaptivity gap lower bound for symmetric metrics} (Chapter~\ref{ch:sym-adapt}).
    We first construct an explicit family of cycle-subdivided-clique instances
    for which $\optprior/\optadapt$ tends to~$5/4$
    (Theorem~\ref{thm:cycle-subdivision}).  Motivated by the role of the random
    subdivision vertices in this construction, we then transfer the randomness
    to the metric and use the probabilistic method to obtain deterministic
    symmetric instances whose adaptivity ratio tends to~$2$
    (Theorem~\ref{thm:random-metric-gap}).  Since
    $\optpost\le\optadapt$, the same factor-two lower bound applies to the
    obliviousness gap.

    \item \textbf{Clairvoyance gap lower bound for symmetric metrics} (Chapter~\ref{ch:sym-clair}).
    We exhibit a family of symmetric instances, the \emph{three-chain construction}, achieving $\optadapt/\optpost = 11/10$ for infinitely many~$n$
    (Theorem~\ref{thm:sym-clair}).
    For context, we also record an elementary upper bound of~$1/(1-p+p^2)$ for the restricted setting with a single stochastic vertex of activation probability~$p$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:general-p}).

    \item \textbf{Clairvoyance gap lower bound for asymmetric metrics} (Chapter~\ref{ch:asym-clair}).
    We first define a recursive family of directed instances for which
    $\optadapt/\optpost$ tends to~$4/3$
    (Theorem~\ref{thm:recursive-gap}).  We then give a four-star layered-poset
    construction for which
    \[
        \liminf\frac{\optadapt}{\optpost}\ge\frac32
    \]
    (Theorem~\ref{thm:layered-poset-gap}).  The construction uses independent
    activations and a strictly positive directed metric.  We also give an
    explicit finite member whose ratio is strictly larger than~$4/3$.

    \item \textbf{Partial backbone-circulation framework} (Chapter~\ref{ch:circulation}).
    For asymmetric metrics, we prove the existence of a backbone whose tour
    and associated fractional circulation have total cost
    $O(\log(n+1))\optpost$.  We then round every outside client to a single
    directed chord of the backbone without increasing this cost, while
    incurring only $O(\log(n+1))$ weighted imbalance on every interval of
    the backbone order (Theorem~\ref{thm:backbone-circulation-rounding}).
    For all but an inverse-polynomial fraction of activation sets, the rounded
    chords admit an offline closed walk that traverses every active outside
    chord between one and four times and every available backbone arc at most
    $O(\log(n+1))$ times
    (Theorem~\ref{thm:offline-backbone-walk}).
    This is a partial structural result: the rounded object has not yet been
    converted into an adaptive policy.

    \item \textbf{Approximate a~priori TSP} (Chapter~\ref{ch:dp}).
    We give a $(1{+}\varepsilon)$-approximation algorithm for a~priori TSP on directed metrics, running in time $c_\varepsilon^n\poly(n)$
    (Theorem~\ref{thm:directed-apriori-ptas}).
    The algorithm discards pairs for which the probability that all intervening clients are inactive is tiny, losing only a negligible amount, and then uses a dynamic program whose state stores just the relevant suffix of the partial order.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Classical TSP.}
The traveling salesperson problem is one of the most studied problems in combinatorial optimization.
For symmetric metrics, the classical $3/2$-approximation of Christofides~\cite{Christofides1973} stood for decades until Karlin, Klein, and Oveis~Gharan~\cite{Karlin2021} obtained a slightly improved factor.
For asymmetric metrics, Svensson, Tarnawski, and V\'egh~\cite{Svensson2020} gave the first constant-factor approximation.

\paragraph{A~priori TSP.}
The a~priori model was introduced by Jaillet~\cite{Jaillet1985}.
Shmoys and Talwar~\cite{Shmoys2008} proved that the obliviousness gap $\optprior/\optpost$ is at most~$3$ for symmetric metrics, yielding a constant-factor approximation via a master tour.
Schalekamp and Shmoys~\cite{SchalekampShmoys2008} studied the connection between universal and a~priori TSP.
Blauth, Neuwohner, Puhlmann, and Vygen~\cite{Blauth2024} recently obtained improved approximation guarantees for a~priori TSP.
For asymmetric metrics, Christalla, Puhlmann, and Traub~\cite{Christalla2025} showed that the obliviousness gap is $O(\sqrt{n})$ and $\Omega(n^{1/4}/\log n)$; moreover, their construction also implies an adaptivity gap of $\Omega(n^{1/4}/\log n)$.

\paragraph{Adaptive and stochastic TSP.}
Gupta, Nagarajan, and Ravi~\cite{GuptaNagarajanRavi2017} studied adaptive TSP and gave an $O(\log^2 n)$-approximation for the stochastic version.
Ene, Nagarajan, and Saket~\cite{EneNagarajanSaket2017} studied approximation algorithms for stochastic $k$-TSP.
Jiang, Li, Liu, and Singla~\cite{JiangLiLiuSingla2020} obtained improved algorithms and adaptivity gaps for stochastic $k$-TSP.
Fu, Li, and Xu~\cite{FLX18} gave a PTAS for a class of stochastic dynamic programs.

\paragraph{Adaptivity gaps in stochastic optimization.}
The study of adaptivity gaps---how much adaptivity helps in stochastic optimization---has a rich history beyond TSP.
Dean, Goemans, and Vondr\'ak~\cite{DeanGoemansVondrak2008} studied the benefit of adaptivity for stochastic knapsack.
Gupta and Nagarajan~\cite{GuptaNagarajan2013} introduced the stochastic probing framework and proved constant adaptivity gaps for matching.
Gupta, Nagarajan, and Singla~\cite{GuptaNagarajanSingla2016,GuptaNagarajanSingla2017} extended these results to submodular and XOS objectives.
Brada\v{c}, Singla, and \v{Z}u\v{z}i\'c~\cite{BradacSinglaZuzic2019} obtained near-optimal adaptivity gaps for stochastic multi-value probing.
Levin and Vainer~\cite{LV14} studied adaptivity in the stochastic blackjack knapsack problem.
Barak and Talgam-Cohen~\cite{BarakTalgamCohen2026} recently studied semi-adaptivity gaps for stochastic knapsack.

\section{Model and benchmarks}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Metric spaces and tours}

Let $(\{r\}\cup V,d)$ be a finite metric space on $n+1$ vertices (a depot vertex~$r$ and $n$~clients) equipped with a distance function~$d$.
That is, $d$ satisfies the triangle inequality: for any three vertices $u,v,w$, $d(u,v) \leq d(u,w) + d(w,v)$.
A metric is \emph{symmetric} if $d(x,y)=d(y,x)$ for all $x,y$; otherwise it
may be asymmetric, in which case distances are directed.

A \emph{tour} on a subset $S \subseteq \{r\}\cup V$ containing the depot is a cyclic sequence $T=(r,u_1,u_2,\ldots,u_{|S|-1},r)$ that visits every vertex in~$S$ exactly once. Its \emph{cost} is
\[
\cost(T) := d(r,u_1) + d(u_1,u_2) + \cdots + d(u_{|S|-2},u_{|S|-1}) + d(u_{|S|-1},r).
\]
For a subset $A\subseteq V$, we write $\opt(A)$ for the minimum cost of a tour on $\{r\}\cup A$.

\subsection{Three benchmarks for stochastic TSP}

In the stochastic setting, the set of active vertices is a subset $A \subseteq V$ generated by independent activations: each vertex $v \in V$ is active (i.e., $v \in A$) independently with probability~$p_v$. We denote by~$q$ the resulting product distribution on~$2^V$, so $q(A) = \prod_{v \in A}p_v \cdot \prod_{v \notin A}(1 - p_v)$.

We consider three benchmarks for stochastic TSP, distinguished by how much the solution may depend on the realization of the active set. In each case we define the cost of a feasible solution via an overloaded function $\cost(\cdot)$.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A posteriori TSP.}
        An \emph{a~posteriori solution} is a family $\mathcal{S}=(T_A)_{A\subseteq V}$, where $T_A$ is a tour on $\{r\}\cup A$ for each activation set~$A$.
        We define
        \[
        \cost(\mathcal{S}) := \E_{A\sim q}\bigl[\cost(T_A)\bigr].
        \]
        Since each $T_A$ can be chosen independently, the optimum is
        \[
        \optpost := \min_{\mathcal{S}}\,\cost(\mathcal{S}) = \E_{A\sim q}\bigl[\opt(A)\bigr].
        \]

    \item \textbf{A priori (nonadaptive) TSP.}
        An \emph{a~priori tour} is a tour $T=(r,v_1,\ldots,v_n,r)$ on the full vertex set $\{r\}\cup V$.
        For a realization~$A$, the \emph{shortcut} $T\vert_A$ is obtained by retaining only the vertices in $\{r\}\cup A$ in the cyclic order prescribed by~$T$.
        More precisely, if $1 \leq i_1 < i_2 < \cdots < i_{|A|} \leq n$ are such that $\{v_{i_k} : 1 \leq k \leq |A|\} = A$, then $T\vert_A = (r,v_{i_1},v_{i_2},\ldots,v_{i_{|A|}},r)$.
        We overload:
        \[
        \cost(T) := \E_{A\sim q}\bigl[\cost(T\vert_A)\bigr].
        \]
        The optimum is $\optprior := \min_{T}\,\cost(T)$, where the minimum is over all a~priori tours.

    \item \textbf{Adaptive TSP.}
        The salesperson knows all the activation probabilities but not the realization~$A$.
        During execution, the salesperson calls clients one by one:
        if the called client~$v$ is in~$A$, the salesperson \emph{must} travel to~$v$ immediately; otherwise the salesperson remains in place.
        The order of calls may depend on the outcomes observed so far.

        Formally, an adaptive policy is a function $\pi: (\{r\} \cup V) \times 2^V \rightarrow \{r\} \cup V \cup \{\bot\}$, where $\pi(u,S)$ denotes the next vertex to call when the salesperson is at~$u$ and the set of uncalled vertices is~$S$.
        We require: (a)~$\pi(u,S) := \bot$ whenever $u \in S$ (invalid input); (b)~$\pi(u,S) = r$ iff $S = \emptyset$ (return to depot only when done); (c)~$\pi(u,S) \in S$ iff $S \neq \emptyset$ (must call an uncalled vertex).

        Restricting the policy to the state $(u,S)$ is without loss of
        generality.  Indeed, independence implies that, conditional on the
        calls made so far, the vertices in~$S$ retain their original
        independent activation probabilities.  If $J(u,S)$ denotes the
        minimum expected remaining cost, then backward induction gives
        \[
            J(u,\emptyset)=d(u,r)
        \]
        and, for $S\neq\emptyset$,
        \[
            J(u,S)
            =
            \min_{v\in S}
            \left\{
                (1-p_v)J(u,S\setminus\{v\})
                +p_v\bigl(d(u,v)+J(v,S\setminus\{v\})\bigr)
            \right\}.
        \]
        Thus a deterministic Markov policy attains the optimum; allowing
        dependence on the full history or private randomization does not
        decrease the optimal expected cost.

        Given a realization~$A$, the policy induces a realized tour $T(A;\pi)$ on $\{r\}\cup A$ as follows.
        Initialize $u \leftarrow r$, $S \leftarrow V$.
        While $S \neq \emptyset$: compute $v = \pi(u,S)$; if $v \in A$ then travel to~$v$ and set $u\leftarrow v$; set $S \leftarrow S \setminus \{v\}$.
        Finally, return to~$r$.
        We overload:
        \[
        \cost(\pi) := \E_{A\sim q}\bigl[\cost(T(A;\pi))\bigr].
        \]
        The optimum is $\optadapt := \min_{\pi}\,\cost(\pi)$, where the minimum is over all adaptive policies.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Gap measures}\label{sec:gaps}

Among the three benchmarks, a~posteriori TSP has the most information (the full realization~$A$), a~priori TSP has the least (only the metric and probabilities), and adaptive TSP lies in between.

We define an \emph{instance} $\mathcal{I} := (V \cup \{r\}, d, \{p_v\})$ of stochastic TSP. For any instance~$\mathcal{I}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optineq}
\optpost \le \optadapt \le \optprior
\end{equation}
since from left to right the solution has less information about the realization, resulting in higher optimal cost.

We define the following gap measures:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Adaptivity gap}: $\sup_{\mathcal{I}} \f{\optprior}{\optadapt}$ --- the worst-case benefit of adaptivity over a fixed a~priori tour.
    \item \emph{Clairvoyance gap}: $\sup_{\mathcal{I}} \f{\optadapt}{\optpost}$ --- the benefit of full clairvoyance (knowing $A$ upfront) over the best adaptive policy.
    \item \emph{Obliviousness gap}: $\sup_{\mathcal{I}} \f{\optprior}{\optpost}$ --- combines both effects.
\end{itemize}
We remark that the literature on a~priori TSP uses the term adaptivity gap for what we call the obliviousness gap; we deviate from this convention following \cite{Christalla2025} for the sake of clarity.

For symmetric metrics, the obliviousness gap is known to be at most $O(1)$; in particular, $\optprior/\optpost \le 3$ is shown in~\cite{Shmoys2008}.
The same upper bound carries over to the clairvoyance gap and the adaptivity gap by~\eqref{eq:optineq}.
For asymmetric metrics, Christalla, Puhlmann, and Traub~\cite{Christalla2025} show that the obliviousness gap is $O(\sqrt{n})$ and $\Omega(n^{1/4} \cdot \log^{-1}n)$.
Moreover, the construction in Section~3.1 of~\cite{Christalla2025} also implies an adaptivity gap of $\Omega(n^{1/4} \cdot \log^{-1}n)$.

\chapter{Adaptivity Gap for Symmetric Metrics}\label{ch:sym-adapt}

We prove that the adaptivity gap for symmetric stochastic TSP is at least~$2$.
We begin with an explicit family obtained by subdividing the edges of a
Hamiltonian cycle in a complete graph; for this family,
$\optprior/\optadapt$ tends to~$5/4$.  The mechanism behind that construction
then motivates a probabilistic construction in which the randomness is moved
from the subdivision vertices to the metric.  This strengthens the ratio
to~$2-o(1)$.

\section{The cycle-subdivided-clique construction}
\label{sec:cycle-subdivided-construction}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:cycle-subdivision}
The adaptivity gap for symmetric stochastic TSP is at least $\frac54$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:cycle-oblivious}
The obliviousness gap for symmetric stochastic TSP is at least~$\frac54$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
This follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:cycle-subdivision} and
$\optpost\le\optadapt$.
\end{proof}

We prove Theorem~\ref{thm:cycle-subdivision} by exhibiting a family of instances and establishing a matching pair of bounds. Consider the following construction.

\begin{definition}[Cycle-subdivided clique]\label{def:cycle-sub-graph}
Fix $n\ge 7$, and let $v_1,\dots,v_n$ be the vertices of the complete graph
$K_n$, with indices taken modulo~$n$. For every $i\in[n]$, subdivide the edge
$v_iv_{i+1}$ by introducing a new vertex~$v_i'$. Denote the resulting
unweighted graph by~$G_n$.
\end{definition}

The \emph{cycle-subdivided-clique instance} $\mathcal I_n$ has depot $r:=v_1$ and client set
\[
V_n:=\{v_2,\dots,v_n\}\cup\{v_1',\dots,v_n'\}.
\]
The underlying distance function is the shortest-path metric induced by~$G_n$. Every
original client $v_2,\dots,v_n$ is active with probability~$1$, while every
subdivision vertex~$v_i'$ is active independently with probability~$1/2$.

Theorem~\ref{thm:cycle-subdivision} follows from the next two lemmas.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:cycle-prior}
For every $n\ge 7$, the instance $\mathcal I_n$ satisfies $\optprior=\frac{15}{8}n$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:cycle-adapt}
For every $n\ge 7$, the instance $\mathcal I_n$ satisfies $\optadapt\le \frac32 n+6$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:cycle-subdivision}]
Lemma~\ref{lem:cycle-prior} gives $\optprior=\frac{15}{8}n$
and Lemma~\ref{lem:cycle-adapt} gives $\optadapt\le \frac32 n+6$.
Hence $\optprior/\optadapt$ converges to $\frac{5}{4}$ as $n$ grows.
\end{proof}

\section{The a priori optimum}
\label{sec:cycle-prior}

We prove Lemma~\ref{lem:cycle-prior} in two steps.  First, we bound the
expected contribution of every segment of an arbitrary a~priori tour between
two original vertices.  Summing these local bounds gives a global lower
bound.  We then construct tours attaining it.

We begin by recording the relevant distances in the metric completion.  For
original vertices,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cycle-original-distances}
    d(v_i,v_{i+1})=2,
    \qquad
    d(v_i,v_j)=1
    \quad\text{if }j\not\equiv i\pm1\pmod n.
\end{equation}
For a subdivision vertex and an original vertex,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cycle-mixed-distances}
    d(v_i',v_i)=d(v_i',v_{i+1})=1,
    \qquad
    d(v_i',v_j)=2
    \quad\text{if }j\notin\{i,i+1\}.
\end{equation}
Finally, for distinct subdivision vertices,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cycle-subdivision-distances}
    d(v_i',v_j')
    =
    \begin{cases}
        2, & \text{if the cycle edges }v_iv_{i+1}
             \text{ and }v_jv_{j+1}\text{ are adjacent},\\
        3, & \text{otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Given original vertices $u,v$ and an ordered sequence
$W=(w_1,\dots,w_k)$ of distinct subdivision vertices, let
$\Phi(u;W;v)$ be the expected length of the realized path obtained from
\[
    (u,w_1,\dots,w_k,v)
\]
after deleting inactive subdivision vertices.

\begin{lemma}[Segment lower bound]\label{lem:cycle-segment-bound}
Every such segment satisfies
\[
    \Phi(u;W;v)
    \ge
    \begin{cases}
        1, & k=0,\\
        1+\frac{7k}{8}, & k\ge1.
    \end{cases}
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We first treat the cases $k\le3$.

\begin{claim}\label{clm:short-seg}
For every segment $(u;W;v)$ with $k=|W|\le3$,
\[
    \Phi(u;W;v)
    \ge
    \begin{cases}
        1, & k=0,\\
        2, & k=1,\\
        \frac{11}{4}, & k=2,\\
        \frac{29}{8}, & k=3.
    \end{cases}
\]
Moreover, equality is attained for $k=2$ by
$(v_i,v_i',v_{i+1}',v_{i+1})$, and for $k=3$ by
$(v_{i+1},v_{i+1}',v_i',v_{i-1}',v_i)$.
\end{claim}

\begin{proof}
If $k=0$, then the segment contributes the single edge $uv$, so
$\Phi(u;\varnothing;v)=d(u,v)\ge1$.

If $k=1$, say $W=(w)$, then
\[
    \Phi(u;w;v)
    =
    \tfrac12d(u,v)
    +\tfrac12\bigl(d(u,w)+d(w,v)\bigr).
\]
If $u$ and $v$ are consecutive on the original cycle, then $d(u,v)=2$
and also $d(u,w)+d(w,v)\ge2$.  If they are not consecutive, then
$d(u,v)=1$, and no subdivision vertex is incident with both $u$ and $v$,
so $d(u,w)+d(w,v)\ge3$.  In either case
$\Phi(u;w;v)\ge2$.

Now let $k=2$, say $W=(w_1,w_2)$.  There are four realizations.  If
$d(u,v)=1$, then neither $w_1$ nor $w_2$ is incident with both $u$ and
$v$, so each singleton realization has cost at least~$3$, while the
realization with both active has cost at least~$4$.  Hence
\[
    \Phi(u;W;v)
    \ge\frac{1+3+3+4}{4}
    =\frac{11}{4}.
\]
If $d(u,v)=2$, then exactly one subdivision vertex is incident with both
$u$ and~$v$, so at most one singleton realization has cost~$2$; the two
singleton costs therefore sum to at least~$5$, and the realization with
both active has cost at least~$4$.  Thus
\[
    \Phi(u;W;v)
    \ge\frac{2+5+4}{4}
    =\frac{11}{4}.
\]
Equality is attained, for example, by
$(v_i,v_i',v_{i+1}',v_{i+1})$, whose four realization costs are
$2,2,3,4$.

Finally, let $k=3$, say $W=(w_1,w_2,w_3)$.  Suppose first that
$d(u,v)=1$.  Then each singleton realization has cost at least~$3$.
A two-vertex realization can have cost~$4$ only if one active subdivider is
incident with~$u$, the other is incident with~$v$, and the corresponding
cycle edges are adjacent.  For fixed nonconsecutive $u$ and $v$, there is
at most one unordered pair of subdivision vertices with this property.
Hence among the three two-vertex realizations, at most one has cost~$4$,
and the other two have cost at least~$5$.  The realization with all three
active has cost at least~$6$.  Therefore
\[
    \Phi(u;W;v)
    \ge\frac{1+9+14+6}{8}
    =\frac{15}{4}
    >\frac{29}{8}.
\]

Now suppose that $d(u,v)=2$.  Then exactly one subdivision vertex is
incident with both $u$ and~$v$, so among the three singleton realizations
at most one has cost~$2$.  Hence the sum of the singleton costs is at least
$2+3+3=8$.  A two-vertex realization can have cost~$4$ only if one active
subdivider is the unique subdivider of the edge $uv$ and the other comes
from one of the two cycle edges adjacent to $uv$.  Thus among the three
two-vertex realizations, at most two have cost~$4$, and the remaining one
has cost at least~$5$; the sum of these three costs is therefore at
least~$13$.  The realization with all three active has cost at least~$6$.
Consequently,
\[
    \Phi(u;W;v)
    \ge\frac{2+8+13+6}{8}
    =\frac{29}{8}.
\]
Equality is attained, for example, by
$(v_{i+1},v_{i+1}',v_i',v_{i-1}',v_i)$, whose eight realization costs are
\[
    2,\ 3,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 5,\ 4,\ 6.
\]
\end{proof}

It remains to consider $k\ge4$.  Set
\[
    z_0:=u,\qquad
    z_t:=w_t\ (1\le t\le k),\qquad
    z_{k+1}:=v.
\]
Two retained vertices $z_i,z_j$ become consecutive after shortcutting
precisely when every vertex strictly between them is deleted.  Since each
$w_t$ is retained independently with probability~$1/2$, we obtain
\begin{align*}
    \Phi(u;W;v)
    &=
    2^{-k}d(u,v)
    +\sum_{t=1}^k2^{-t}d(u,w_t)
    +\sum_{t=1}^k2^{-(k-t+1)}d(w_t,v)\\
    &\qquad
    +\sum_{1\le i<j\le k}2^{-(j-i+1)}d(w_i,w_j).
\end{align*}
Introduce the indicators
\[
    \alpha_t:=\1_{\{w_t\text{ is incident with }u\}},
    \qquad
    \beta_t:=\1_{\{w_t\text{ is incident with }v\}},
\]
and, for $1\le i<j\le k$,
\[
    \gamma_{ij}
    :=
    \1_{\{w_i,w_j\text{ correspond to adjacent cycle edges}\}}.
\]
By~\eqref{eq:cycle-original-distances}--%
\eqref{eq:cycle-subdivision-distances},
\begin{align*}
    d(u,v)
    &=1+\1_{\{u,v\text{ are consecutive on the original cycle}\}},\\
    d(u,w_t)&=2-\alpha_t,\\
    d(w_t,v)&=2-\beta_t,\\
    d(w_i,w_j)&=3-\gamma_{ij}.
\end{align*}
Substituting these identities gives
\[
    \Phi(u;W;v)
    =
    1+\frac{3k}{2}
    +2^{-k}\1_{\{u,v\text{ consecutive}\}}
    -D,
\]
where
\[
    D
    :=
    \sum_{t=1}^k2^{-t}\alpha_t
    +\sum_{t=1}^k2^{-(k-t+1)}\beta_t
    +\sum_{1\le i<j\le k}2^{-(j-i+1)}\gamma_{ij}.
\]
Since an original vertex is incident with exactly two subdivision vertices,
\[
    \sum_{t=1}^k2^{-t}\alpha_t
    \le\frac34,
    \qquad
    \sum_{t=1}^k2^{-(k-t+1)}\beta_t
    \le\frac34.
\]
For the last sum, consider the graph on
$\{w_1,\dots,w_k\}$ in which two vertices are adjacent when the
corresponding subdivision vertices arise from adjacent edges of the original
cycle.  This graph is a subgraph of a cycle, hence has maximum degree at
most~$2$ and at most $k$ edges.  Every edge contributes weight at
most~$1/4$, so
\[
    \sum_{1\le i<j\le k}
    2^{-(j-i+1)}\gamma_{ij}
    \le\frac{k}{4}.
\]
Therefore
\[
    D\le\frac32+\frac{k}{4},
\]
and hence
\[
    \Phi(u;W;v)
    \ge
    1+\frac{3k}{2}
    -\left(\frac32+\frac{k}{4}\right)
    =
    \frac54k-\frac12.
\]
Since $k\ge4$,
\[
    \frac54k-\frac12
    \ge1+\frac{7k}{8}.
\]
Together with Claim~\ref{clm:short-seg}, this proves the lemma.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:cycle-prior}]
Fix an arbitrary a~priori tour
\[
    T=(v_1,s_1,s_2,\dots,s_{2n-1},v_1)
\]
on $\{v_1\}\cup V_n$, where $s_1,\dots,s_{2n-1}$ is a permutation of
$\{v_2,\dots,v_n,v_1',\dots,v_n'\}$.  Deleting all subdivision vertices
from~$T$ leaves a cyclic ordering
\[
    (v_1=u_1,u_2,\dots,u_n,v_1)
\]
of the original vertices.  For each $j\in[n]$, let
\[
    W_j=(w_{j,1},\dots,w_{j,k_j})
\]
be the ordered list, possibly empty, of subdivision vertices appearing
between $u_j$ and $u_{j+1}$, where $u_{n+1}:=v_1$.  Every subdivision
vertex belongs to exactly one such segment, so
\[
    \sum_{j=1}^n k_j=n.
\]
Because all original vertices are active with probability~$1$, the realized
tour decomposes into these segment contributions.  Applying
Lemma~\ref{lem:cycle-segment-bound}, we obtain
\begin{align*}
    \E[\cost(T|_A)]
    &=
    \sum_{j=1}^n\Phi(u_j;W_j;u_{j+1})\\
    &\ge
    \sum_{j:k_j=0}1
    +\sum_{j:k_j\ge1}\left(1+\frac{7k_j}{8}\right)\\
    &=
    n+\frac78\sum_{j=1}^nk_j
    =
    \frac{15}{8}n.
\end{align*}
Since $T$ was arbitrary,
\[
    \optprior\ge\frac{15}{8}n.
\]

For the reverse inequality, we construct a tour attaining this bound.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Even \(n\).}
Write $n=2m$ and consider
\begin{align*}
T^{\mathrm{even}}
:=(&v_1,v_1',v_2',v_2,
   v_4,v_4',v_3',v_3,
   v_6,v_6',v_5',v_5,
   \dots,\\
  &v_{2m},v_{2m}',v_{2m-1}',v_{2m-1},v_1).
\end{align*}
This tour has $m$ nonempty segments, namely
\[
    (v_1,v_1',v_2',v_2)
    \quad\text{and}\quad
    (v_{2t},v_{2t}',v_{2t-1}',v_{2t-1})
    \qquad(2\le t\le m),
\]
each with expected contribution $11/4$, together with $m$ direct edges
between original vertices, each of length~$1$.  Hence
\[
    \E[\cost(T^{\mathrm{even}}|_A)]
    =
    m\left(\frac{11}{4}+1\right)
    =
    \frac{15}{8}n.
\]

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Odd \(n\).}
Write $n=2m+1$ with $m\ge2$ and consider
\begin{align*}
T^{\mathrm{odd}}
:=(&v_n,v_2,v_2',v_1',v_n',v_1,
   v_4,v_4',v_3',v_3,
   v_6,v_6',v_5',v_5,
   \dots,\\
  &v_{2m},v_{2m}',v_{2m-1}',v_{2m-1},v_n).
\end{align*}
This tour has one segment
\[
    (v_2,v_2',v_1',v_n',v_1)
\]
with expected contribution $29/8$, together with $m-1$ segments
\[
    (v_{2t},v_{2t}',v_{2t-1}',v_{2t-1})
    \qquad(2\le t\le m),
\]
each contributing $11/4$, and $m+1$ direct edges between original vertices,
each of length~$1$.  Therefore
\begin{align*}
    \E[\cost(T^{\mathrm{odd}}|_A)]
    &=
    \frac{29}{8}
    +(m-1)\frac{11}{4}
    +(m+1)\\
    &=
    \frac{15}{8}(2m+1)
    =
    \frac{15}{8}n.
\end{align*}

In both parity cases we have constructed an a~priori tour of expected cost
$15n/8$.  Together with the lower bound, this proves
\[
    \optprior=\frac{15}{8}n.
\]
\end{proof}

\section{Randomizing the metric}
\label{sec:random-metric-gap}

The cycle-subdivided-clique construction suggests a more general mechanism
for separating adaptive and a~priori solutions.  Its subdivision vertices are
random, and deleting the inactive ones creates shortcut edges that a fixed
master tour must pay for.  Although the master tour knows the metric, it must
choose one cyclic order that works for every activation pattern.  The adaptive
policy, by contrast, can use the outcomes of calls to decide which cheap
continuation to take.

We now amplify this mechanism by moving the randomness from the subdivision
vertices to the metric.  The metric will contain a sparse random collection of
cheap edges.  A fixed a~priori order typically obtains little benefit from
them, because the consecutive active clients in its shortcut are determined by
independent activation randomness.  An adaptive policy can instead call all
cheap neighbors of its current location before calling a client at distance~$2$.

The random metric is only a device for the probabilistic method.  After it is
sampled, we fix one deterministic realization.  That complete metric is known
to both benchmarks, and both the optimal master tour and the adaptive policy
may depend on it.  Consequently, the a~priori lower bound must hold
simultaneously for all master tours, rather than merely for each fixed tour in
expectation over the sampled metric.

\begin{theorem}[Probabilistic factor-two construction]
\label{thm:random-metric-gap}
There exists a sequence of deterministic symmetric stochastic TSP instances
for which
\[
    \frac{\optprior}{\optadapt}\longrightarrow 2.
\]
In particular, the adaptivity gap for symmetric stochastic TSP is at
least~$2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:random-metric-oblivious}
The obliviousness gap for symmetric stochastic TSP is at least~$2$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For every instance, $\optpost\le\optadapt$.  Hence
\[
    \frac{\optprior}{\optpost}
    \ge
    \frac{\optprior}{\optadapt},
\]
and the claim follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:random-metric-gap}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The random metric}

All logarithms in this section are natural.  Fix a sufficiently large
integer~$n$, let $V=[n]$, and set
\[
    p:=\frac{1}{(\log n)^4},
    \qquad
    \theta:=\frac{1}{\log n},
    \qquad
    q:=1-p.
\]
Every client is active independently with probability~$p$.

Independently for every unordered pair
$e=\{x,y\}\subseteq\{r\}\cup V$, sample a Bernoulli random variable
$X_e$ with mean~$\theta$, and define
\[
    D(x,y):=2-X_{\{x,y\}}
    \quad (x\neq y),
    \qquad
    D(x,x):=0.
\]
Thus a pair has distance~$1$ with probability~$\theta$ and distance~$2$
otherwise.  We call a pair of distance~$1$ a \emph{cheap edge}.
Every realization of~$D$ is a symmetric metric: for distinct $x,y,z$,
\[
    D(x,z)\le2\le D(x,y)+D(y,z).
\]

Let $A$ be the random active set and let
$K:=|A|\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p)$.  Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:random-metric-baseline}
    W
    :=
    \E\bigl[(K+1)\1_{\{K\ge1\}}\bigr]
    =
    np+1-q^n.
\end{equation}
For a nonempty realization with $K=k$, every feasible tour has exactly
$k+1$ traveled edges.  Since all distances are at least~$1$, $W$ is a
lower bound on the expected cost of every benchmark.  Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:random-metric-growth}
    W\ge np=\frac{n}{(\log n)^4},
    \qquad
    \theta W\ge\frac{n}{(\log n)^5}\longrightarrow\infty.
\end{equation}

\subsection{A uniform lower bound for a priori tours}

Fix an a~priori tour
\[
    T=(r,v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n,r)
\]
and a deterministic realization~$d$ of the metric.  Write
\[
    C_d(T):=\E_A[\cost_d(T|_A)].
\]

\begin{lemma}[Expected-cost expansion]\label{lem:random-metric-expansion}
For $i\in[n]$, define
\[
    \alpha_i:=p\bigl(q^{i-1}+q^{n-i}\bigr),
\]
and, for $1\le i<j\le n$, define
\[
    \beta_{ij}:=p^2q^{j-i-1}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:random-metric-expansion}
    C_d(T)
    =
    \sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i d(r,v_i)
    +
    \sum_{1\le i<j\le n}\beta_{ij}d(v_i,v_j).
\end{equation}
The coefficients in~\eqref{eq:random-metric-expansion} sum to~$W$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The edge from $r$ to $v_i$ is used when $v_i$ is active and
$v_1,\ldots,v_{i-1}$ are inactive, which has probability $pq^{i-1}$.
Similarly, the edge from $v_i$ to~$r$ is used with probability
$pq^{n-i}$.  For $i<j$, the clients $v_i$ and $v_j$ are consecutive
active clients in the shortcut precisely when both are active and all clients
strictly between them are inactive.  This event has probability
$p^2q^{j-i-1}$, proving~\eqref{eq:random-metric-expansion}.

If every distance in the expansion is replaced by~$1$, the right-hand side
is the expected number of edges in the shortcut tour.  This number is
$(K+1)\1_{\{K\ge1\}}$, whose expectation is~$W$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Coefficient-square bound]
\label{lem:random-metric-coefficients}
For every a~priori order and all sufficiently large~$n$,
\[
    \sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i^2
    +
    \sum_{1\le i<j\le n}\beta_{ij}^2
    \le 2np^3.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Using $(a+b)^2\le2a^2+2b^2$,
\begin{align*}
    \sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i^2
    &\le
    2p^2\sum_{i=1}^n
        \bigl(q^{2(i-1)}+q^{2(n-i)}\bigr)\\
    &\le
    \frac{4p^2}{1-q^2}
    =
    \frac{4p}{2-p}
    \le4p.
\end{align*}
Also,
\begin{align*}
    \sum_{1\le i<j\le n}\beta_{ij}^2
    &=
    p^4\sum_{g=0}^{n-2}(n-g-1)q^{2g}\\
    &\le
    \frac{np^4}{1-q^2}
    =
    \frac{np^3}{2-p}
    \le np^3.
\end{align*}
Since $np^2\to\infty$, we have $4p\le np^3$ for all sufficiently
large~$n$.  Adding the two estimates proves the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[All master tours are expensive]
\label{lem:random-metric-prior}
With probability $1-o(1)$ over the random metric~$D$,
\[
    \optprior(D)
    \ge
    \left(2-\frac{2}{\log n}\right)W.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix a tour~$T$, and let $c_e$ be the coefficient of the unordered pair~$e$
in~\eqref{eq:random-metric-expansion}.  Since $D(e)=2-X_e$ and the
coefficients sum to~$W$,
\[
    C_D(T)=2W-\sum_e c_eX_e,
    \qquad
    \E_D[C_D(T)]=(2-\theta)W.
\]
Set $\varepsilon:=1/\log n$.  The event
\[
    C_D(T)\le(2-\theta-\varepsilon)W
\]
is equivalent to
\[
    \sum_e c_e(X_e-\theta)\ge\varepsilon W.
\]
The weighted form of Hoeffding's inequality therefore gives
\[
    \P_D\left[
        C_D(T)\le(2-\theta-\varepsilon)W
    \right]
    \le
    \exp\left(
        -\frac{2\varepsilon^2W^2}{\sum_ec_e^2}
    \right).
\]
Using Lemma~\ref{lem:random-metric-coefficients} and $W\ge np$, we obtain
\begin{align*}
    \P_D\left[
        C_D(T)\le(2-\theta-\varepsilon)W
    \right]
    &\le
    \exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^2W^2}{np^3}\right)\\
    &\le
    \exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^2n}{p}\right)\\
    &=
    \exp\bigl(-n(\log n)^2\bigr).
\end{align*}

There are at most $n!$ master tours with the depot fixed.  A union bound
therefore shows that the probability that some tour violates the claimed
lower bound is at most
\[
    n!\exp\bigl(-n(\log n)^2\bigr)
    \le
    \exp\bigl(n\log n-n(\log n)^2\bigr)
    =
    o(1).
\]
The conclusion follows because
$\theta=\varepsilon=1/\log n$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A cheap adaptive policy}

For each deterministic metric realization~$d$, define a policy~$\pi_d$ as
follows.  Suppose the salesperson is at~$u$ and the set of uncalled clients
is~$S$.  If $S=\varnothing$, return to~$r$.  If $u$ has a cheap neighbor
in~$S$, call the least-labeled such client; otherwise call the least-labeled
client in~$S$.  Thus all uncalled cheap neighbors of the current location
are called before any client at distance~$2$.

This policy may depend on~$d$, because the metric is fixed as part of the
instance and is known before any activation outcomes are observed.

\begin{lemma}[Cost of the adaptive policy]
\label{lem:random-metric-adaptive-average}
Let
\[
    U(d):=\E_A[\cost_d(\pi_d,A)].
\]
Then $U(d)\ge W$ for every~$d$, and
\[
    \E_D[U(D)]
    \le
    np+\left(\frac1\theta+1\right)(1-q^n)
    \le
    W+\frac1\theta.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix $k\ge1$ and condition on $K=k$.  Consider the execution jointly over
the random metric and the active set.  Immediately after the salesperson
arrives at an active client~$u$, let $S$ be the set of uncalled clients and
suppose that exactly $s$ clients in~$S$ are active.

We expose the random metric lazily for the purpose of the analysis.  Before
this arrival, none of the edges $\{u,v\}$ with $v\in S$ has affected a
decision of the policy: previous decisions inspected only edges incident to
the then-current location, while neither endpoint of $\{u,v\}$ had yet been
the current location.  The incoming edge to~$u$ has its other endpoint
outside~$S$.  Consequently, conditional on the entire previous execution,
the edges $\{u,v\}$ for $v\in S$ remain independent, each being cheap with
probability~$\theta$, and they remain independent of the activation states
in~$S$.  This deferred exposure is only an analysis device; the policy
itself knows the complete fixed metric.
At the initial state $u=r$, the edges from the depot to all uncalled clients
are likewise unexposed in this analysis.

The next traveled edge has length~$2$ exactly when none of the $s$ remaining
active clients is a cheap neighbor of~$u$.  Its conditional expected length
is therefore
\[
    1+(1-\theta)^s.
\]
As the policy visits the $k$ active clients, the value of~$s$ before the
successive traveled edges is $k,k-1,\ldots,1$.  The final return to the depot
costs at most~$2$.  Hence
\begin{align*}
    \E_{D,A}[\cost_D(\pi_D,A)\mid K=k]
    &\le
    \sum_{s=1}^k\bigl(1+(1-\theta)^s\bigr)+2\\
    &\le
    k+\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}+2\\
    &=
    k+\frac1\theta+1.
\end{align*}
For $K=0$ the cost is~$0$.  Averaging over~$K$ gives
\begin{align*}
    \E_D[U(D)]
    &\le
    \E[K]
    +\left(\frac1\theta+1\right)\P[K\ge1]\\
    &=
    np+\left(\frac1\theta+1\right)(1-q^n)\\
    &=
    W+\frac{1-q^n}{\theta}
    \le
    W+\frac1\theta.
\end{align*}

For the pointwise lower bound, if $K=k\ge1$, the policy travels along exactly
$k+1$ edges, each of length at least~$1$.  Taking expectation over the active
set gives $U(d)\ge W$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[A good policy for most sampled metrics]
\label{lem:random-metric-adaptive-whp}
Let
\[
    \delta_n:=\frac1{\sqrt{\theta W}}.
\]
Then $\delta_n=o(1)$ and
\[
    \P_D\bigl[\optadapt(D)\le(1+\delta_n)W\bigr]
    \ge1-\delta_n.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By~\eqref{eq:random-metric-growth}, $\delta_n=o(1)$.  The nonnegative random
variable $U(D)-W$ satisfies
\[
    \E_D[U(D)-W]\le\frac1\theta
\]
by Lemma~\ref{lem:random-metric-adaptive-average}.  Markov's inequality gives
\begin{align*}
    \P_D[U(D)>(1+\delta_n)W]
    &=
    \P_D[U(D)-W>\delta_nW]\\
    &\le
    \frac{1/\theta}{\delta_nW}
    =
    \frac1{\sqrt{\theta W}}
    =
    \delta_n.
\end{align*}
Since $\optadapt(d)\le U(d)$ for every~$d$, the lemma follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:random-metric-gap}]
By Lemmas~\ref{lem:random-metric-prior}
and~\ref{lem:random-metric-adaptive-whp}, with probability $1-o(1)$ the
sampled metric satisfies both
\[
    \optprior(D)
    \ge
    \left(2-\frac{2}{\log n}\right)W
\]
and
\[
    \optadapt(D)\le(1+\delta_n)W.
\]
The two events therefore have a nonempty intersection for all sufficiently
large~$n$.  Choose a deterministic realization~$d_n$ in this intersection.
Then
\[
    \frac{\optprior(d_n)}{\optadapt(d_n)}
    \ge
    \frac{2-2/\log n}{1+\delta_n}
    =
    2-o(1).
\]

It remains to verify the stated convergence, rather than only a lower limit.
For every metric in the support of the construction, every shortcut tour on a
nonempty active set of size~$k$ uses $k+1$ edges of length at most~$2$.
Consequently, $\optprior(d_n)\le2W$.  On the other hand, every adaptive policy
has expected cost at least~$W$.  Thus
\[
    \frac{\optprior(d_n)}{\optadapt(d_n)}\le2.
\]
Combining the two bounds proves that the ratio converges to~$2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Choice of parameters]
The particular powers of $\log n$ are chosen for clarity rather than
optimality.  The three relevant requirements are that $\theta\to0$, that
$\theta W\to\infty$, and that the concentration exponent for a fixed master
tour dominate $\log(n!)$.  The choices above make these conditions immediate.
\end{remark}

\chapter{Clairvoyance Gap for Symmetric Metrics}\label{ch:sym-clair}

In this chapter we study the symmetric clairvoyance gap $\optadapt/\optpost$.
We construct an explicit family of symmetric instances achieving ratio~$11/10$.

\section{The three-chain lower bound}\label{sec:3chain}

\begin{theorem}[Symmetric clairvoyance gap lower bound]\label{thm:sym-clair}
The clairvoyance gap for symmetric stochastic TSP is at least~$11/10$.
\end{theorem}

We prove the theorem by constructing a family of three-chain instances whose
adaptive and a~posteriori optimum values differ by precisely this factor.

\begin{construction}[Three-chain graph $H_k$]\label{con:3chain}
Let $k \ge 3$ be an integer.
Define a symmetric weighted graph with vertex set
\[
V(H_k) \;=\; A\;\cup\; B\;\cup\; C\;\cup\; \{s\},
\qquad |V(H_k)| = 3k+1,
\]
where the \emph{chains}
\begin{align*}
A &= (a_0, a_1, \ldots, a_{k-1}), &
B &= (b_0, b_1, \ldots, b_{k-1}), &
C &= (c_0, c_1, \ldots, c_{k-1})
\end{align*}
are vertex-disjoint paths. There are two types of edges in this graph:

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Internal edges} (weight~$1$): for each chain $X \in \{A,B,C\}$
and each $0\le i\le k-2$, include the edge $\{x_i, x_{i+1}\}$ of weight~$1$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Inter-chain edges} (weight~$2(k-1)$):
\begin{equation*}\label{eq:interchain}
\begin{gathered}
\{a_0, b_{k-1}\},\quad
\{a_0, c_{k-1}\},\quad
\{b_0, a_{k-1}\},\quad
\{c_0, a_{k-1}\},\\
\{c_0, b_{k-1}\},\quad
\{b_0, s\},\quad
\{c_{k-1}, s\}.
\end{gathered}
\end{equation*}

\end{construction}

The instance has depot $r=a_0$ and $3k$ client 
$V(H_k)\setminus\{r\}$. Its distance function is the shortest-path metric
induced by $H_k$. The client $s$ is active with probability~$1/2$, and

\[

The inter-chain weight $2(k-1)$ balances the cost of a crossing against
the cost of traversing the chains and makes the active and inactive optimal
tour skeletons compete at the thresholds used below.

Theorem \ref{thm:sym-clair} can be directly derived from the following lemmas:

\begin{lemma}[Optimal a posteriori cost]\label{lem:sym-apost-cost}
    For $H_k$, $\optpost=10(k-1)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{lemma}[Optimal adaptive cost]\label{lem:sym-adapt-cost}
    For $H_k$, $\optadapt=11(k-1)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}[Proof to Theorem~\ref{thm:sym-clair}]
    Follows immediately from definition.
\end{proof}

The proof of these identities has two structural ingredients.  First we identify the only
near-optimal crossing patterns in the two realizations.  We then show that an
adaptive policy cannot realize both patterns because its calls before learning
the status of~$s$ must be the same in both realizations.

\begin{lemma}[Realization costs and near-optimal routes]\label{lem:near-optimal-routes}
Let $k\ge3$ and $h=k-1$.
In the instance $G_k$, the following hold.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}]
Every tour on $\{r\}\cup D$ has cost at least $9h$.
This bound is attained by
\begin{multline*}
T_D:\;
a_0 \to a_1 \to \cdots \to a_{k-1}
\xrightarrow{\;2h\;} b_0 \to b_1 \to \cdots \to b_{k-1}\\
\xrightarrow{\;2h\;} c_0 \to c_1 \to \cdots \to c_{k-1}
\xrightarrow{\;2h\;} a_0,
\end{multline*}
which has cost $9h$.
Moreover, any tour on $\{r\}\cup D$ of cost strictly less than $11h$
has the following property: every expansion of its metric edges into shortest
paths in $H_k$ uses each of these three inter-chain crossings exactly once
and uses no other crossing:
\[
    \{a_0,c_{k-1}\},\qquad
    \{c_0,b_{k-1}\},\qquad
    \{b_0,a_{k-1}\}.
\]

\item[\textup{(ii)}]
Every tour on $\{r\}\cup D\cup\{s\}$ has cost at least $11h$.
This bound is attained by
\begin{multline*}
T_{\mathrm{all}}:\;
a_0 \to a_1 \to \cdots \to a_{k-1}
\xrightarrow{\;2h\;} c_0 \to c_1 \to \cdots \to c_{k-1}\\
\xrightarrow{\;2h\;} s
\xrightarrow{\;2h\;} b_0 \to b_1 \to \cdots \to b_{k-1}
\xrightarrow{\;2h\;} a_0,
\end{multline*}
which has cost $11h$.
Moreover, any tour on the full vertex set of cost strictly less than $13h$
has the following property: every shortest-path expansion uses each of these
four crossings exactly once and uses no other crossing:
\[
    \{a_0,b_{k-1}\},\qquad
    \{b_0,s\},\qquad
    \{s,c_{k-1}\},\qquad
    \{c_0,a_{k-1}\}.
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Replace every metric edge of a tour by a shortest path in the underlying
weighted graph.  The resulting closed walk has the same length, and its
support---the subgraph formed by the edges it uses---is connected.

We first show that the internal edges of each chain contribute at least~$h$.
Fix one chain.  If all its $h$ internal edges occur in the walk, the claim is
immediate.  Otherwise, at most one internal edge can be absent: two absent
edges would separate the required vertices between them from the support of
the walk, since only the two endpoints of the chain have edges leaving the
chain.  If one internal edge is absent, each of the remaining $h-1$ internal
edges is a bridge in the support and is therefore traversed at least twice by
the closed walk.  Its internal-edge contribution is then at least
$2(h-1)\ge h$, where the last inequality uses $h\ge2$.  Summing over the three
chains gives a contribution of at least~$3h$.

Call a traversal of one of the seven edges in~\eqref{eq:interchain} a
\emph{crossing}.  Each crossing has cost~$2h$.
For a tour on $\{r\}\cup D$, the expanded walk starts and ends in chain~$A$
and must visit chains~$B$ and~$C$, so it uses at least three crossings.  Hence
its cost is at least $3h+6h=9h$.
For a tour on $\{r\}\cup D\cup\{s\}$, the expanded walk must also visit~$s$,
so it uses at least four crossings.  Hence its cost is at least
$3h+8h=11h$.

It remains to identify the tours that can be close to these lower bounds.
First consider a tour on $\{r\}\cup D$ of cost strictly less than
$11h=3h+8h$. It must use exactly three crossings.  After contracting each
chain, these crossings form the cycle $A$--$B$--$C$--$A$; in particular, the
walk does not use~$s$.  The $B$--$C$ crossing is necessarily
$\{b_{k-1},c_0\}$.  There are two choices for the $A$--$B$ crossing and two
for the $A$--$C$ crossing.  Choosing $\{a_0,b_{k-1}\}$ together with
$\{a_0,c_{k-1}\}$ repeats the crossing endpoint on chains $A$ and~$B$;
choosing $\{a_0,b_{k-1}\}$ together with $\{a_{k-1},c_0\}$ repeats it on
$B$ and~$C$; and choosing $\{a_{k-1},b_0\}$ together with
$\{a_{k-1},c_0\}$ repeats it on $A$ and~$C$.  The remaining combination is
the unique one using distinct endpoints on every chain, and its crossing set
is
\[
E_D:=\bigl\{\{a_0,c_{k-1}\},\{c_0,b_{k-1}\},
              \{b_0,a_{k-1}\}\bigr\}.
\]
If the two crossings incident with a chain use the same endpoint, the walk
must travel from that endpoint to the other end of the chain and back, at
cost at least~$2h$.  Hence each of the other three combinations pays at least
$2h+2h+h=5h$ on chain edges and at least $5h+6h=11h$ in total, a
contradiction.  Thus the three crossings of every tour below~$11h$ are
exactly those in~$E_D$.

Now consider a full tour of cost strictly less than
$13h=3h+10h$.  It uses exactly four crossings, which form a cycle through
$A,B,s,C$.  The two crossings incident with~$s$ must be $\{b_0,s\}$ and
$\{c_{k-1},s\}$.  Again there are two choices for the $A$--$B$ crossing and
two for the $A$--$C$ crossing.  The three choices other than
$\{a_0,b_{k-1}\}$ together with $\{a_{k-1},c_0\}$ are as follows:
$\{a_0,b_{k-1}\}$ with $\{a_0,c_{k-1}\}$ repeats endpoints on $A$ and~$C$;
$\{a_{k-1},b_0\}$ with $\{a_{k-1},c_0\}$ repeats them on $A$ and~$B$; and
$\{a_{k-1},b_0\}$ with $\{a_0,c_{k-1}\}$ repeats them on $B$ and~$C$.
Hence the unique combination using distinct endpoints on every chain is
\[
E_{\mathrm{all}}:=
\bigl\{\{a_0,b_{k-1}\},\{b_0,s\},\{s,c_{k-1}\},
        \{c_0,a_{k-1}\}\bigr\}.
\]
Each of the other three combinations repeats an endpoint on two chains, so
the same $5h$ chain-edge lower bound gives total cost at least
$5h+8h=13h$.  Thus every full tour below~$13h$ uses exactly the four
crossings in~$E_{\mathrm{all}}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Incompatibility of the two near-optimal skeletons]
\label{lem:incompatible-skeletons}
Let $C_0$ and $C_1$ be the realized costs of an adaptive policy when $s$ is
inactive and active, respectively. Then it is impossible to have both
$C_0<11h$ and $C_1<13h$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The two realizations make exactly the same calls until the policy calls~$s$,
because every other client is deterministic. Write their common called prefix
as
\[
P=(r=x_0,x_1,\dots,x_t=u).
\]
Thus the inactive realized tour begins with~$P$, whereas the active realized
tour begins with $(P,s)$.

Assume for contradiction that $C_0<11h$ and $C_1<13h$. Expand every metric
edge into a shortest path in $H_k$, choosing the same expansion for the
edges of the common prefix. Lemma~\ref{lem:near-optimal-routes} forces the
inactive and active expanded tours to use the crossing sets $E_D$ and
$E_{\mathrm{all}}$, respectively. These sets are disjoint. Consequently, the
common prefix contains no crossing. Since it starts at $a_0$, every client
called before~$s$ lies on chain~$A$, and $u\in A$.

Only $b_0$ and $c_{k-1}$ are adjacent to~$s$ in the underlying graph. The
expanded path from $u$ to~$s$ in the active realization must therefore reach
$s$ through one of them. Because its crossings must belong to
$E_{\mathrm{all}}$, there are only two possibilities:
\begin{itemize}
  \item it goes from $A$ through $\{a_0,b_{k-1}\}$, traverses chain~$B$ from
  $b_{k-1}$ to~$b_0$, and then uses $\{b_0,s\}$; or
  \item it goes from $A$ through $\{a_{k-1},c_0\}$, traverses chain~$C$ from
  $c_0$ to~$c_{k-1}$, and then uses $\{c_{k-1},s\}$.
\end{itemize}
In the first case no client of~$B$ has yet been called. Passing through its
vertices in the shortest-path expansion does not call them, so all of~$B$
must still be served after reaching~$s$. But the two crossings just used are
the only crossings of $E_{\mathrm{all}}$ incident with~$B$; the remaining
skeleton proceeds from $s$ through $C$ and $A$ and never returns to~$B$.
Serving~$B$ would therefore require repeating a crossing or adding another
one, contradicting the forced four-crossing skeleton. The second case gives
the same contradiction with chain~$C$. Hence the two inequalities cannot
hold simultaneously.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:sym-clair}]
Since $s$ is active with probability~$1/2$, there are exactly two
realizations.  Lemma~\ref{lem:near-optimal-routes} gives optimum costs $9h$
when $s$ is inactive and $11h$ when it is active.  Therefore
\[
    \optpost=\frac12\cdot9h+\frac12\cdot11h=10h.
\]

For an adaptive upper bound, follow the order of $T_{\mathrm{all}}$ and
call~$s$ after $c_{k-1}$.  If $s$ is active, continue through~$s$ and then
chain~$B$.  If it is inactive, call $b_0$ next; the metric edge from
$c_{k-1}$ to~$b_0$ costs at most $4h$, by the path
$c_{k-1}$--$s$--$b_0$.  Thus either realization costs at most
$\cost(T_{\mathrm{all}})=11h$, and $\optadapt\le11h$.

For the reverse inequality, consider any adaptive policy and let $C_0$ and
$C_1$ be its realized costs when $s$ is inactive and active.  If
$(C_0+C_1)/2<11h$, the realization lower bounds $C_0\ge9h$ and $C_1\ge11h$
force $C_0<11h$ and $C_1<13h$.  This contradicts
Lemma~\ref{lem:incompatible-skeletons}.  Hence $\optadapt\ge11h$.

Substituting $h=k-1$ gives the displayed optimum values above.  Their ratio is
$11/10$, which proves the claimed lower bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Tour restructuring]\label{rem:restructuring}
The two optimal realized tours visit the chains in opposite orders:
$T_{\mathrm{all}}$ routes $A \to C \to s \to B$,
while $T_D$ routes $A \to B \to C$.
The cost reduction from removing~$s$ is not a local vertex deletion
but a \emph{global tour restructuring} that saves one inter-chain crossing.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Equality of adaptive and a~priori]
The order $T_{\mathrm{all}}$ is also an a~priori tour of expected cost
$11(k-1)$ or less, since shortcutting an inactive~$s$ replaces the two edges
$c_{k-1}s$ and $sb_0$ by a metric edge of no greater length.  Therefore
$\optprior\le 11(k-1)$.  Together with
$\optadapt\le\optprior$ from~\eqref{eq:optineq}, this gives
$\optprior=\optadapt=11(k-1)$ for this family.  All of the gap comes from
clairvoyance, not from adaptivity.
\end{remark}

\section{An auxiliary single-stochastic-vertex bound}\label{sec:single-vertex-upper}

\sharat{You don't have to call it an auxiliary bound. It's mostly a thing we say while talking but not necessarily write it.}

This section records an elementary bound for the restricted case of a single
stochastic vertex. It is independent of the three-chain argument and is not
claimed to be tight. Consider a symmetric instance with a single stochastic
vertex~$s$ of activation probability $p\in(0,1)$ and deterministic
customers~$D$.
If $D=\emptyset$, the adaptive and a~posteriori benchmarks coincide, so for
the notation and proof below we may assume $D\ne\emptyset$.
Write $T_{\mathrm{all}}$ for the optimal tour on $D\cup\{s\}\cup\{r\}$ and $T_D$ for the optimal tour on $D\cup\{r\}$, with costs
\[
    \optall:=\cost(T_{\mathrm{all}}),
    \qquad
    \opt_D:=\cost(T_D).
\]
Let
\[
    \rho:=\frac{\optall}{\opt_D},
    \qquad
    d_{\min}:=\min_{v\in D\cup\{r\}}d(v,s),
    \qquad
    \beta:=\frac{d_{\min}}{\opt_D}.
\]

\begin{proposition}[Single-stochastic-vertex bound]\label{prop:general-p}
For any symmetric metric instance with a single stochastic vertex~$s$
of probability~$p \in (0,1)$,
\[
\frac{\optadapt}{\optpost} \;\le\; \frac{1}{1 - p + p^2}.
\]
The right-hand side is maximized at $p = 1/2$, where it equals~$4/3$;
we do not claim that this value is attained by an instance.
By continuity, at $p = 0$ and $p = 1$ the bound equals~$1$ (no gap).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For general~$p$, the a~posteriori expected cost is
\[
\optpost = (1-p)\,\opt_D + p\,\optall = \opt_D\bigl((1-p) + p\rho\bigr).
\]
One adaptive policy simply follows $T_{\mathrm{all}}$.  Another follows
$T_D$ to a closest vertex $v$ to~$s$ and
calls~$s$ there.  If $s$ is inactive, it continues along $T_D$.  If $s$ is
active, it calls the next vertex of $T_D$ after visiting~$s$; by the triangle
inequality, this changes the next leg by at most $2d(v,s)=2d_{\min}$.
The two policies have expected costs at most $\optall$ and
$\opt_D+2p\,d_{\min}=\opt_D(1+2p\beta)$, respectively.  Taking the better
one gives
\[
\frac{\optadapt}{\optpost}
\;\le\; \frac{\min(\rho,\; 1 + 2p\beta)}{(1-p) + p\rho}.
\]

We use three elementary metric constraints.  Shortcutting~$s$ from
$T_{\mathrm{all}}$ gives $\rho\ge1$; inserting~$s$ at a closest vertex of
$T_D$ gives $\rho\le1+2\beta$; and the two edges of $T_{\mathrm{all}}$
incident with~$s$ each have length at least~$d_{\min}$, so
\[
    1\le\rho\le1+2\beta,
    \qquad
    2\beta\le\rho.
\]

For fixed~$\rho$, the numerator $\min(\rho,1+2p\beta)$ is nondecreasing in~$\beta$, and the largest feasible value is $\beta=\rho/2$.
Thus it remains to maximize
\[
    \frac{\min(\rho,1+p\rho)}{(1-p)+p\rho}
    \qquad(\rho\ge1).
\]
If $\rho\le1/(1-p)$, the minimum is~$\rho$ and the expression is increasing in~$\rho$.
If $\rho\ge1/(1-p)$, the minimum is~$1+p\rho$ and the expression is decreasing in~$\rho$.
Therefore the maximum occurs at $\rho=1/(1-p)$, where
\[
\frac{1/(1-p)}{(1-p) + p/(1-p)}
= \frac{1}{(1-p)^2 + p}
= \frac{1}{1 - p + p^2}.
\]

Finally, $1-p+p^2$ is minimized at $p=1/2$.
Hence the largest value of the bound over $p\in[0,1]$ is
\[
    \frac{1}{1-1/2+1/4}=\frac43.
\]
\end{proof}

\chapter{Clairvoyance Gap for Asymmetric Metrics}\label{ch:asym-clair}

We now turn to asymmetric (directed) metrics.  We give two constructions.
The first is a recursive family for which the clairvoyance gap
$\optadapt/\optpost$ tends to~$4/3$.  At each recursive level, a clairvoyant
tour chooses the cheaper orientation after seeing whether a new midpoint is
active, whereas an adaptive policy must make part of this choice before
learning that activation bit.  The second construction strengthens the lower
bound to~$3/2$.  It embeds a layered poset into a directed metric: a
clairvoyant tour needs essentially two increasing chains, while every causal
policy needs at least three increasing runs with high probability.  Both
proofs allow a policy to interleave calls across different recursive blocks or
poset layers.

\section{The construction}

For a copy~$C$ of a recursive block, write $\mathcal P(C)$ for its
\emph{permanent vertices}, which will also serve as its \emph{ports}, and
write $\mathcal M(C)$ for its midpoint vertices.  For the canonical block
$B_k$, abbreviate these sets by $\mathcal P_k$ and $\mathcal M_k$.

The block $B_0$ consists of one permanent vertex and has no midpoint.  For
$k\ge1$, let
\[
    w_k:=2^{k-1}.
\]
Construct $B_k$ from disjoint copies $B_{k-1}^{\mathrm L}$ and
$B_{k-1}^{\mathrm R}$ and a new midpoint~$m_k$.  Add the following arcs,
all of length~$w_k$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $x\to y$ for every
    $x\in\mathcal P(B_{k-1}^{\mathrm L})$ and
    $y\in\mathcal P(B_{k-1}^{\mathrm R})$;
    \item $y\to m_k$ for every
    $y\in\mathcal P(B_{k-1}^{\mathrm R})$; and
    \item $m_k\to x$ for every
    $x\in\mathcal P(B_{k-1}^{\mathrm L})$.
\end{itemize}
Thus, after contracting the two children, the top level is the directed
triangle
\[
    B_{k-1}^{\mathrm L}
    \longrightarrow
    B_{k-1}^{\mathrm R}
    \longrightarrow
    m_k
    \longrightarrow
    B_{k-1}^{\mathrm L}.
\]
Set
\[
    \mathcal P_k
    :=
    \mathcal P(B_{k-1}^{\mathrm L})
    \cup
    \mathcal P(B_{k-1}^{\mathrm R}),
    \qquad
    \mathcal M_k
    :=
    \mathcal M(B_{k-1}^{\mathrm L})
    \cup
    \mathcal M(B_{k-1}^{\mathrm R})
    \cup\{m_k\}.
\]
Every permanent vertex is active with probability~$1$, and every midpoint is
active independently with probability~$1/2$.

For $l\ge1$, the full instance~$I_l$ is obtained from $B_l$ by adding the
depot~$r$ and, for every $x\in\mathcal P_l$, the two arcs $r\to x$ and
$x\to r$, each of length~$w_l$.  Distances in~$I_l$ are the directed
shortest-path distances in this generating graph.

The generating graph of every block is strongly connected.  This follows by
induction: each child is strongly connected, and the three top-level arc
bundles connect the two children and the new midpoint in a directed cycle.
The depot arcs preserve strong connectivity.  Hence all shortest-path
distances are finite and define a directed metric.  Notice also that every arc
crossing the boundary of a child block is incident with one of its ports.

The block $B_l$ has $|\mathcal P_l|=2^l$ permanent vertices and
$|\mathcal M_l|=2^l-1$ midpoints.  For $1\le k\le l$, it contains
$2^{l-k}$ midpoint vertices introduced at level~$k$, each associated with
the length scale~$w_k$.

\section{Statement}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:recursive-gap}
For every $l\ge1$, the instance $I_l$ satisfies
\[
    \frac{\optadapt(I_l)}{\optpost(I_l)}
    =
    \frac{4(l+1)}{3l+4}.
\]
As $l\to\infty$, these ratios approach~$4/3$, so the asymmetric
clairvoyance gap is at least~$4/3$.
\end{theorem}

The theorem follows from two exact cost evaluations, which we prove after
developing the required decomposition tools.

\begin{lemma}[A posteriori cost]\label{lem:post-cost}
For every $l\ge1$,
\[
    \optpost(I_l)=(3l+4)2^{l-2}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Adaptive cost]\label{lem:adapt-cost}
For every $l\ge1$,
\[
    \optadapt(I_l)=(l+1)2^l.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:recursive-gap}]
Lemmas~\ref{lem:post-cost} and~\ref{lem:adapt-cost} give
\[
    \frac{\optadapt(I_l)}{\optpost(I_l)}
    =
    \frac{(l+1)2^l}{(3l+4)2^{l-2}}
    =
    \frac{4(l+1)}{3l+4}.
\]
The right-hand side tends to~$4/3$.
\end{proof}

\section{Decomposition tools}

If the two children of every block were served in two uninterrupted visits,
the desired recurrences would follow immediately.  A general tour or policy
may instead leave a child, do work elsewhere, and later return.  We therefore
define local open-service values and show how to charge every additional
piece of a child service to an arc at its parent's level.

\subsection{Open services and expanded executions}

For a copy~$C$ of $B_k$, let $d_C$ be the directed shortest-path metric of
the generating subgraph induced by~$C$.  For a fixed active-midpoint set
$A\subseteq\mathcal M(C)$, a \emph{fixed open service} is a directed walk
inside~$C$ that starts and ends at ports and visits every vertex in
$\mathcal P(C)\cup A$.  Its two endpoint ports are free choices, and repeated
vertices are allowed.  Let $F_k(A)$ be its minimum cost.  Shortcutting repeated
visits in the metric shows that allowing walks does not change this optimum.

For adaptive services we use the following boundary relaxation.  Initially
the policy is in an abstract exterior state.  It must call every client of~$C$
exactly once.  Inactive calls made before the first movement leave the policy
in the exterior state.  When the first active client~$v$ is called, the policy
chooses an entry port $a\in\mathcal P(C)$ and is charged $d_C(a,v)$; the
external movement to~$a$ is not charged.  Thereafter the usual adaptive rules
apply with metric~$d_C$.  Once all clients have been called, the policy chooses
an exit port $b\in\mathcal P(C)$ and, if its current position is~$x$, is
charged $d_C(x,b)$ for the final internal movement.  Let $G_k$ be the minimum
expected cost of such an
\emph{adaptive open service}, where the expectation is over the independent
midpoint activations in~$C$.  The same backward-induction argument as in
Chapter~\ref{ch:intro} shows that history dependence and private randomization
cannot improve this finite boundary-state problem.  For the single-port block,
\[
    F_0(\emptyset)=G_0=0.
\]

For cost accounting, fix once and for all a shortest generating-graph walk for
each ordered pair of vertices.  Whenever a metric move is made, \emph{expand}
it into this walk.  Passing through a client in an expanded walk neither calls
nor serves that client.  Every non-depot arc occurrence in an expanded walk
belongs to a unique recursive level.

Fix a child block~$C$.  Cut an expanded execution at every crossing of the
boundary of~$C$, including multiple crossings made during a single metric
move.  A \emph{$C$-piece} is a maximal internal portion containing at least
one service event in~$C$; it enters and leaves at ports.  Initial and terminal
port endpoints are treated as boundary crossings.  Internal portions that
only transit through~$C$ may be omitted from the list of pieces, but their
arcs remain included in the total internal cost~$Z$.  Omitting such transit
can only decrease the cost of the local service constructed below.  Calls to
inactive vertices made outside~$C$ cause no movement and do not create pieces.

\begin{lemma}[Ports and interrupted services]\label{lem:block-excursions}
Let $C$ be a copy of~$B_k$.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Any two ports of~$C$ can be joined inside~$C$ by a directed walk of
    length at most $w_{k+1}=2w_k$ when $k\ge1$; for $k=0$, the block has only
    one port and the corresponding bound is~$0\le w_1$.
    \item Suppose $C$ occurs as a child of a copy of~$B_{k+1}$.  If a
    fixed-realization service is split into $N\ge1$ $C$-pieces, $Z$ is the
    total cost of all arc occurrences internal to~$C$, and
    $A_C\subseteq\mathcal M(C)$ is the active-midpoint set, then
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:post-excursion}
        Z+w_{k+1}(N-1)\ge F_k(A_C).
    \end{equation}
    \item Under the same embedding, suppose an adaptive service is split into
    a random number $N\ge1$ of $C$-pieces, possibly according to observations
    outside~$C$.  Then
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:adapt-excursion}
        \E[Z]+w_{k+1}\E[N-1]\ge G_k.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We first prove the port bound by induction on~$k$.  It is immediate for
$k=0$.  Let $k\ge1$ and let $x,y$ be ports of~$C$.  If they lie in the same
child, induction gives an internal walk of length at most~$w_k$.  If $x$ lies
in the left child and $y$ in the right, the direct level-$k$ arc has length
$w_k$.  In the reverse direction, the two-arc walk through~$m_k$ has length
$2w_k=w_{k+1}$.  This proves the first statement.

For a fixed realization, retain the $N$ pieces in their original order.
Between consecutive pieces, join the preceding exit port to the next entry
port by an internal walk of length at most~$w_{k+1}$.  Together with all
service-containing internal portions, these connectors form a fixed open
service of~$C$.  Its cost is at most $Z+w_{k+1}(N-1)$, proving
\eqref{eq:post-excursion}.

For the adaptive statement, let $\xi$ consist of the complete activation
vector outside~$C$, together with any private random seed used by the original
policy.  Sample $\xi$ at time zero.  It is independent of all activations
inside~$C$.  We run a virtual copy of the original execution: calls outside
$C$ receive the corresponding pre-sampled coordinate of~$\xi$ only when that
vertex is virtually called, and their movements update only the virtual
position in the ambient execution; calls inside~$C$ use the actual local
outcomes.  Thus every decision remains causal with respect to the observations
in~$C$, even though the virtual exterior behavior may depend on them.

Consider two consecutive active calls in~$C$.  If they belong to the same
piece, the internal portion of the original expanded movement connects them.
If they belong to different pieces, concatenate three internal walks: the
suffix leading from the first client to the preceding exit port, a
port-to-port connector of length at most~$w_{k+1}$, and the prefix from the
next entry port to the second client.  The local metric distance between the
two clients is no larger than this concatenated walk.  The first piece uses
the free-entry convention, and the final internal suffix supplies the terminal
movement to an exit port.  Inactive local calls cause no movement.  This
construction also covers arbitrarily many boundary crossings within one
expanded metric move.

For every joint realization, the resulting local strategy has cost at most
$Z+w_{k+1}(N-1)$.  Averaging over the independent seed~$\xi$ and the
activations in~$C$ gives a randomized, possibly history-dependent open service
with expected cost at most the left-hand side of
\eqref{eq:adapt-excursion}.  By backward induction for the finite local state
space, a deterministic Markov open service is no more expensive.  Its cost is
at least~$G_k$, proving \eqref{eq:adapt-excursion}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Top-level accounting}

At one recursive node, contract the two children to symbols $L$ and $R$ and
write $M$ for the new midpoint.  The level arcs form
\[
    L\longrightarrow R\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow L,
\]
each of unit length after scaling by the common factor~$w$.  In the full
instance add the depot symbol~$D$ and the unit arcs
$D\leftrightarrow L$ and $D\leftrightarrow R$.

Let $N_L$ and $N_R$ be the numbers of service pieces in the two children, and
let $H$ be the number of top-level arc \emph{occurrences} in the expanded
execution, counted with multiplicity.  In the closed case, $H$ also counts all
depot-arc occurrences, including intermediate passages through~$D$.  Define
\[
    \widehat H:=H-(N_L-1)-(N_R-1).
\]

\begin{lemma}[Top-level accounting]\label{lem:top-accounting}
The following bounds hold.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item For a fixed realization, an open service satisfies
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:open-top-fixed}
        \widehat H\ge1+\1_{\{M\text{ active}\}},
    \end{equation}
    while a service starting and ending at~$D$ satisfies
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:closed-top-fixed}
        \widehat H\ge3+\1_{\{M\text{ active}\}}.
    \end{equation}
    \item For an adaptive service, when $M$ is independently active with
    probability~$1/2$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:open-top-adapt}
        \E[\widehat H]\ge2
    \end{equation}
    in the open case, and
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:closed-top-adapt}
        \E[\widehat H]\ge4
    \end{equation}
    in the case starting and ending at~$D$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For every piece of $L$ except its first, charge the last top-level arc
occurrence by which the execution enters $L$ before that piece.  Charge pieces
of~$R$ analogously.  These charged occurrences are distinct, one for each
extra piece.  All other top-level arc occurrences are uncharged, so their
number is exactly~$\widehat H$.

In an open fixed-realization service with $M$ inactive, the first entry into
whichever child is not the starting child is uncharged, giving
$\widehat H\ge1$.  If $M$ is active, its service requires another uncharged
entry, giving $\widehat H\ge2$.  In the closed case, the first entries used to
serve $L$ and $R$ and the final return to~$D$ are three distinct uncharged
occurrences.  An active~$M$ requires a fourth.  This proves
\eqref{eq:open-top-fixed} and \eqref{eq:closed-top-fixed}.

We next record the equality cases when $M$ is inactive.  Contract every child
service piece to its letter in $\{L,R\}$.  Without the depot, the minimum
numbers of top-level arcs between consecutive letters are
\[
    c(L,R)=1,\qquad c(R,L)=2,\qquad
    c(L,L),c(R,R)\ge3.
\]
The piece word contains both letters, and a word with $t$ letters has exactly
$t-2$ charged entries.  If $t=2$, the adjusted costs of the words $LR$ and
$RL$ are respectively~$1$ and~$2$.  If $t\ge3$, the transition table shows
that the sum of transition costs minus $(t-2)$ is at least~$2$: an additional
equal letter costs at least three before its one-unit charge, while any
additional alternation that includes a return $R\to L$ costs two.  Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:open-equality-trace}
    \widehat H=1
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \text{there is one piece per child and the trace is }L\to R.
\end{equation}

In the closed quotient, entering the first child and returning from the last
each cost one arc.  Intermediate use of the depot gives
$c(L,L),c(R,R)\ge2$, while $c(L,R)=1$ and $c(R,L)=2$ remain valid.  For two
pieces, $D\to L\to R\to D$ has adjusted cost~$3$, whereas the reverse order
has cost~$4$.  For $t\ge3$, the transition sum is at least~$t$, so after
adding the two boundary arcs and subtracting $t-2$ charged entries the adjusted
cost is at least~$4$.  Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:closed-equality-trace}
    \widehat H=3
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \text{there is one piece per child and the trace is }
    D\to L\to R\to D.
\end{equation}
These classifications include pure transit, repeated pieces, and intermediate
depot passages, since all their top-level arc occurrences were retained in
$H$.

For the adaptive bounds, condition on all activation bits except that of~$M$
and, if present, on the policy's private seed.  Couple the executions with
$M$ inactive and active, and denote their adjusted counts by
$\widehat H_0$ and~$\widehat H_1$.

We will repeatedly use the following elementary counting observation.  If
$t$ specified arc occurrences are unavoidable and at most $s$ of those
occurrences can be charged to extra child pieces, then
$\widehat H\ge t-s$.  Indeed, every other charged occurrence must be one of
the remaining $H-t$ occurrences, so subtracting all charges can cancel at
most $s$ of the specified occurrences.  This observation allows the active
execution to create arbitrarily many additional pieces without weakening the
counts below.

In the open case, the fixed bounds give $\widehat H_0\ge1$ and
$\widehat H_1\ge2$.  If $\widehat H_0\ge2$, their sum is at least~$4$.
Otherwise \eqref{eq:open-equality-trace} applies, and the two executions agree
until $M$ is called.  There are three possible phases.
\begin{itemize}
    \item If $M$ is called in the exterior state, an active execution needs at
    least the three occurrences $R\to M\to L\to R$.  The entry into~$M$ and
    the first service entries into $L$ and $R$ are all uncharged, so
    $\widehat H_1\ge3$.
    \item If it is called during the $L$-service, reaching $M$ requires
    $L\to R\to M$, and subsequently serving $R$ requires
    $M\to L\to R$.  Of these four occurrences, only $M\to L$ can be charged,
    namely when an unfinished $L$-service is resumed there.  Hence
    $\widehat H_1\ge4-1=3$; completing~$L$ before the call cannot reduce the
    count.
    \item If it is called during or after the $R$-service, the common prefix
    already contains the uncharged first entry $L\to R$.  Serving $M$ and
    reaching a terminal port requires $R\to M\to L$.  All service in~$L$ was
    completed in the common prefix, so $M\to L$ is pure transit or terminal
    movement and cannot be charged.  These three specified occurrences are
    therefore uncharged.  If an unfinished $R$-service is later re-entered,
    its charged entry is an additional occurrence and does not reduce this
    bound.
\end{itemize}
Thus $\widehat H_1\ge3$ whenever $\widehat H_0=1$, and in all cases
\[
    \widehat H_0+\widehat H_1\ge4.
\]
Averaging the equiprobable values of~$M$ proves
\eqref{eq:open-top-adapt}.

In the closed case, the fixed bounds give $\widehat H_0\ge3$ and
$\widehat H_1\ge4$.  The desired pair sum is immediate when
$\widehat H_0\ge4$.  If $\widehat H_0=3$, then
\eqref{eq:closed-equality-trace} applies.  Again the executions agree until
$M$ is called.
\begin{itemize}
    \item If $M$ is called at the initial depot, an active execution needs at
    least the five occurrences $D\to R\to M\to L\to R\to D$.  They consist
    of the entry into~$M$, the first service entries into both children, the
    final return, and the necessary transit before~$M$; none is chargeable.
    Thus $\widehat H_1\ge5$.
    \item If it is called during the $L$-service, the necessary occurrences
    are
    \[
        D\to L\to R\to M\to L\to R\to D.
    \]
    There are six of them, and only $M\to L$ can be charged, when an
    unfinished $L$-service is resumed.  Hence
    $\widehat H_1\ge6-1=5$; if $L$ is already complete, the count is no
    smaller.
    \item If it is called during or after the $R$-service, the common prefix is
    $D\to L\to R$.  If the $R$-service is complete, the five occurrences in
    $D\to L\to R\to M\to L\to D$ are all uncharged: in particular,
    $M\to L$ is pure transit because the $L$-service is complete.  If the
    $R$-service is unfinished, returning to it and then to~$D$ produces six
    specified occurrences, of which only the re-entry $L\to R$ can be
    charged.  Both subcases give $\widehat H_1\ge5$.
\end{itemize}
There is no case in which $M$ is called after a final return to~$D$, because
the policy may return only after all clients have been called.  Consequently,
$\widehat H_0+\widehat H_1\ge8$, and averaging proves
\eqref{eq:closed-top-adapt}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Gluing relaxed block policies}

The open-service definition allows a terminal movement to a chosen port,
whereas the original adaptive model permits movement only after an active call
or on the final return to the depot.  The following observation justifies all
recursive policies used below.

\begin{lemma}[Policy gluing]\label{lem:block-policy-gluing}
Consider a causal recursive plan obtained by concatenating adaptive open
services of child blocks with generating-graph walks between their chosen
ports.  The plan may make inactive calls before entering a child, and its
entry and exit ports may depend on the observed history.  If every terminal
child movement is included in the stated cost of the plan, then the plan can
be implemented by a policy in the original metric model with no larger cost.
The same conclusion holds for recursively nested concatenations.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Do not execute a child's terminal movement when its local service ends.
Instead, keep the internal walk from the last active client to the chosen exit
port as a pending walk.  Inactive calls made before the next entry cause no
movement, so the pending walk remains unchanged.  When the next active
client~$v$ is called, concatenate the pending exit walk, the prescribed
parent-level connection to the next entry port, and the next child's internal
entry walk to~$v$.  This is a directed generating-graph walk from the current
active client to~$v$; the metric distance charged by the original model is no
larger than its length.  At the end of the full execution, concatenate the
remaining pending walk with the prescribed return to the depot.

All ports and connections are chosen from information available when the
corresponding active call is made.  The complete arc bundles between child
ports, and between top-level ports and the depot, realize every such choice.
The same pending-walk invariant applies at each recursive level, so nested
terminal movements are handled by concatenation rather than by voluntary
repositioning.  Each arc of the recursive plan is charged at most once, which
proves the claim for the resulting causal, possibly finite-memory policy.
The Markovization argument in Chapter~\ref{ch:intro} replaces this policy,
without increasing expected cost, by a deterministic policy of the form
$\pi(u,S)$ used in the original model.
\end{proof}

\section{Proof of the a posteriori cost lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Fixed open recurrence]\label{lem:post-rec}
For every $k\ge1$ and every $A\subseteq\mathcal M_k$, let
\[
    A_{\mathrm L}:=A\cap\mathcal M(B_{k-1}^{\mathrm L}),
    \qquad
    A_{\mathrm R}:=A\cap\mathcal M(B_{k-1}^{\mathrm R}).
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:post-rec}
    F_k(A)
    =
    F_{k-1}(A_{\mathrm L})+F_{k-1}(A_{\mathrm R})
    +w_k\bigl(1+\1_{\{m_k\in A\}}\bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Expand an arbitrary fixed open service.  Let $Z_{\mathrm L}$ and
$Z_{\mathrm R}$ be the total costs of arcs internal to the children,
$N_{\mathrm L}$ and $N_{\mathrm R}$ their numbers of pieces, and $H$ the
number of top-level arc occurrences.  Lemmas~\ref{lem:block-excursions}
and~\ref{lem:top-accounting} give
\begin{align*}
    \cost
    &=Z_{\mathrm L}+Z_{\mathrm R}+w_kH\\
    &\ge F_{k-1}(A_{\mathrm L})+F_{k-1}(A_{\mathrm R})
       +w_k\bigl(H-(N_{\mathrm L}-1)-(N_{\mathrm R}-1)\bigr)\\
    &\ge F_{k-1}(A_{\mathrm L})+F_{k-1}(A_{\mathrm R})
       +w_k\bigl(1+\1_{\{m_k\in A\}}\bigr).
\end{align*}

For the reverse inequality, choose optimal child services together with their
initial and terminal ports.  If $m_k$ is inactive, serve the left child and
use the direct length-$w_k$ port arc to enter and serve the right child.  If
$m_k$ is active, serve the right child, use the two arcs
$R\to m_k\to L$ of total length~$2w_k$, and then serve the left child.  The
complete port bundles allow the required endpoint choices, so both
constructions attain the lower bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Depot normalization]\label{lem:recursive-depot-normalization}
For every $l\ge1$ and every $A\subseteq\mathcal M_l$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recursive-depot-normalization}
    \opt(\mathcal P_l\cup A)=2w_l+F_l(A).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Expand a tour on $\{r\}\cup\mathcal P_l\cup A$.  Every internal occurrence of
the depot in a client-to-client expanded path has the form
$x\to r\to y$ for ports $x,y\in\mathcal P_l$ and costs~$2w_l$.  By the port
bound in Lemma~\ref{lem:block-excursions}, it can be replaced by an internal
$x$-to-$y$ walk of length at most $w_{l+1}=2w_l$.  Replace every such
occurrence.  Only the first departure from and the final return to the depot
remain.  Each contributes one depot arc of length~$w_l$; this remains true
when the first or last served client is a midpoint, because its expanded path
enters or leaves $B_l$ through a port.  Removing these two depot arcs leaves a
fixed open service, so every tour costs at least $2w_l+F_l(A)$.

Conversely, add a depot arc before and after an optimal fixed open service.
The resulting closed walk costs $2w_l+F_l(A)$ and serves every active client.
Shortcutting repeated client visits in the directed metric produces a tour of
no greater cost.  This proves equality.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:post-cost}]
Let $\overline F_k:=\E[F_k(A)]$, where the expectation is over the midpoint
activations in~$B_k$.  Since $m_k$ is active with probability~$1/2$,
\[
    \overline F_k
    =
    2\overline F_{k-1}+\frac32w_k,
    \qquad
    \overline F_0=0.
\]
Equivalently, summing over the $2^{k-j}$ level-$j$ midpoints gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expected-open-post}
    \overline F_k
    =
    \frac32\sum_{j=1}^k2^{k-j}w_j
    =
    \frac32\sum_{j=1}^k2^{k-1}
    =
    3k2^{k-2}.
\end{equation}
Taking expectations in \eqref{eq:recursive-depot-normalization} and using
$2w_l=2^l$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:post-value-recursive}
    \optpost(I_l)
    =
    2^l+3l2^{l-2}
    =
    (3l+4)2^{l-2}.
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\section{Proof of the adaptive cost lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Adaptive open recurrence]\label{lem:adapt-rec}
For every $k\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:adapt-rec}
    G_k=2G_{k-1}+2w_k.
\end{equation}
Consequently, $G_k=k2^k$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Consider an arbitrary adaptive open service and use the decomposition from the
proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:post-rec}.  The adaptive part of
Lemma~\ref{lem:block-excursions} and the open part of
Lemma~\ref{lem:top-accounting} give
\begin{align*}
    \E[\cost]
    &=\E[Z_{\mathrm L}+Z_{\mathrm R}+w_kH]\\
    &\ge2G_{k-1}
      +w_k\E\bigl[H-(N_{\mathrm L}-1)-(N_{\mathrm R}-1)\bigr]\\
    &\ge2G_{k-1}+2w_k.
\end{align*}

For the reverse inequality, use optimal adaptive open services in the two
children.  First serve the left child, then cross to and serve the right
child, and finally call~$m_k$.  If $m_k$ is inactive, terminate at the chosen
right-child exit port.  If it is active, use $R\to m_k\to L$ and terminate at
a left-child port.  Lemma~\ref{lem:block-policy-gluing} makes this recursive
description a legal adaptive open service.  Its top-level cost is $w_k$ when
$m_k$ is inactive and $3w_k$ when it is active, hence its expected top-level
cost is~$2w_k$.  Equality follows.  Induction from $G_0=0$ and
$w_k=2^{k-1}$ gives $G_k=k2^k$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:adapt-cost}]
Expand an arbitrary adaptive policy for~$I_l$ and decompose its work in the
two top-level children.  Lemmas~\ref{lem:block-excursions}
and~\ref{lem:top-accounting}, now using the closed quotient, give
\begin{align*}
    \E[\cost]
    &=\E[Z_{\mathrm L}+Z_{\mathrm R}+w_lH]\\
    &\ge2G_{l-1}
      +w_l\E\bigl[H-(N_{\mathrm L}-1)-(N_{\mathrm R}-1)\bigr]\\
    &\ge2G_{l-1}+4w_l.
\end{align*}

For a matching policy, start at the depot, recursively serve the left child
and then the right child, and finally call~$m_l$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:block-policy-gluing} realizes the relaxed child interfaces as legal
metric movements.  If $m_l$ is inactive, the top-level trace is
$r\to L\to R\to r$ and costs~$3w_l$.  If it is active, the trace is
$r\to L\to R\to m_l\to L\to r$ and costs~$5w_l$.  Its expected top-level
cost is therefore~$4w_l$, so the lower bound is attained.  Using
$G_{l-1}=(l-1)2^{l-1}$ and $w_l=2^{l-1}$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:adapt-value-recursive}
    \optadapt(I_l)
    =
    2G_{l-1}+4w_l
    =
    (l+1)2^l.
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\section{A layered-poset lower bound}
\label{sec:layered-poset-gap}

We next strengthen the preceding lower bound.  The construction separates two
different events at each layer.  A clairvoyant chain cover pays extra only
when at least three selectors in one layer are active, an event of order
$q^3$.  A causal policy, however, can be forced to make an incompatible
routing decision on an exact two-selector realization, an event of order
$q^2$.  Choosing the number of layers between these two scales makes the
posterior cost approach~$2$ while the adaptive cost is at least~$3-o(1)$.

\begin{theorem}[Layered-poset lower bound]
\label{thm:layered-poset-gap}
The clairvoyance gap for asymmetric stochastic TSP is at least~$3/2$.
More precisely, there is a sequence of independently activated instances
$(J_t)_{t\ge2}$ on strictly positive directed metrics such that
\[
    \liminf_{t\to\infty}
    \frac{\optadapt(J_t)}{\optpost(J_t)}
    \ge\frac32.
\]
\end{theorem}

\subsection{The four-star layered poset}

Fix an integer $L\ge1$.  For each $0\le i\le L$, introduce two
\emph{gate clients}
\[
    G_i:=\{g_i^1,g_i^2\}.
\]
All gates are permanent.  For each stage $1\le i\le L$, introduce four
\emph{selector clients} $A_i,B_i,C_i,D_i$.  Every selector is active
independently with probability~$q$.

We define a poset~$P_L$ by the following generating comparabilities:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:four-star-comparabilities}
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{selector}
    &\text{predecessor gates}
    &\text{successor gates}\\ \hline
A_i&\{g_{i-1}^1\}&\{g_i^1,g_i^2\}\\
B_i&\{g_{i-1}^2\}&\{g_i^1,g_i^2\}\\
C_i&\{g_{i-1}^1,g_{i-1}^2\}&\{g_i^1\}\\
D_i&\{g_{i-1}^1,g_{i-1}^2\}&\{g_i^2\}.
\end{array}
\end{equation}
Every gate in the middle column is below the selector, and the selector is
below every gate in the last column.  We take the transitive closure and add
no other comparabilities.

It is useful to identify a transition from $G_{i-1}$ to~$G_i$ with a cell of
a $2\times2$ matrix.  A chain entering through $g_{i-1}^r$ and leaving through
$g_i^c$ uses cell~$(r,c)$.  The selector stars are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:four-star-matrix}
\begin{array}{c|cc}
&c=1&c=2\\ \hline
r=1&A_i,C_i&A_i,D_i\\
r=2&B_i,C_i&B_i,D_i.
\end{array}
\end{equation}
Thus $A_i,B_i$ are the two row stars and $C_i,D_i$ are the two column stars.
The four selectors in one stage are pairwise incomparable, as are the two
gates in one layer.  On the other hand, every gate in $G_{i-1}$ is below every
gate in~$G_i$.

\subsection{The posterior chain cover}

Let $Z_i$ be the number of active selectors in stage~$i$, so
$Z_i\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(4,q)$.  For a realization~$A$, write
$P_L[A]$ for the subposet induced by its active clients.

\begin{lemma}[Width of a realization]
\label{lem:layered-poset-width}
Every realization satisfies
\[
    2
    \le\operatorname{width}(P_L[A])
    \le2+\sum_{i=1}^L(Z_i-2)_+.
\]
Consequently,
\[
    2
    \le\E[\operatorname{width}(P_L[A])]
    \le2+L(4q^3-2q^4)
    \le2+4Lq^3.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Every gate layer is a permanent two-element antichain, which proves the lower
bound.

For the upper bound, first observe that every subset of at most two stars in
\eqref{eq:four-star-matrix} can be assigned cells in different rows and
different columns.  For the two row stars use cells $(1,1)$ and~$(2,2)$, and
do the same for the two column stars.  For a mixed row-column pair, assign
the row star to the column opposite the column star, and assign the column
star to the row opposite the row star.

Start two chains at $g_0^1$ and~$g_0^2$.  At stage~$i$, if $Z_i\le2$, put all
active selectors on the chain transitions supplied by the preceding cell
assignment.  If $Z_i>2$, put any two active selectors on those transitions
and give each remaining selector a singleton chain.  In both cases the two
spanning chains emerge at different gates of~$G_i$, so the construction
continues to the next stage.  This gives a chain cover of size at most
\[
    2+\sum_{i=1}^L(Z_i-2)_+.
\]
Dilworth's theorem proves the realization-wise upper bound.  Finally,
\begin{align*}
    \E[(Z_i-2)_+]
    &=\P(Z_i=3)+2\P(Z_i=4)\\
    &=4q^3(1-q)+2q^4\\
    &=4q^3-2q^4,
\end{align*}
and linearity of expectation proves the remaining claims.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The causal obstruction}

Fix an adaptive policy and one realization.  List the active clients in the
order in which the policy serves them.  An \emph{increasing run} is a maximal
contiguous part of this list in which every client is below the next client
in~$P_L$.  Let $K_\pi$ be the number of such runs.

Every gate layer forces $K_\pi\ge2$.  Moreover, on the event $K_\pi\le2$,
the two runs form a two-chain cover of all permanent gates.  Each run
therefore contains exactly one gate from every layer and passes through the
layers in increasing order.  In each stage the first run can contain at most
one selector.  If it enters the stage in row~$r$ and leaves in column~$c$,
any other active selector must fit the complementary cell $(3-r,3-c)$ on the
second run.

This observation also explains why remote calls do not evade the
construction.  If a policy calls a selector in a future stage before the
first run has served the intervening gates, an active answer jumps past one
gate in each intervening layer.  The second run cannot contain both gates of
such a layer, so $K_\pi\le2$ is then impossible.  Inactive remote calls cause
no movement and will be allowed in the product argument below.

Put
\[
    h:=q^2(1-q)^2.
\]

\begin{lemma}[One-stage obstruction]
\label{lem:four-star-local}
Fix the input row of the first run at one stage.  Every deterministic causal
strategy fails to preserve a two-run routing on at least one realization
having exactly two active selectors.  Its one-stage success probability is
therefore at most~$1-h$.  The same statement holds if the strategy is given
an arbitrary advice string independent of the four local activation bits.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Follow the strategy on the branch on which every selector queried before the
output gate is inactive.  If the strategy chooses an output gate without
querying any selector, every exact two-active realization is fatal.  Calling
one of the two selectors before the second run reaches its predecessor gate
skips an unserved gate and forces a third run; waiting leaves two incomparable
selectors for the single second run.

Otherwise, let $X$ be the first queried selector.  Follow the branch on which
$X$ is active and every later selector queried before the output gate is
inactive.  If $X$ is incompatible with the input row, this branch already
cannot belong to a two-run routing, and any exact pair containing~$X$ is
fatal.  We may therefore assume that the strategy assigns~$X$ to a cell
$(r,c)$ compatible with its input row and chosen output column.

Exactly two of the four stars contain $(r,c)$: its row star and its column
star.  Let $Y\ne X$ be the other one.  Consider the realization in which
exactly $X$ and~$Y$ are active.  If the strategy queries~$Y$ before taking
the output gate, then it sees two incomparable active selectors before the
first run leaves the stage, so the two-run event has already failed.  Suppose
instead that it queries~$Y$ later.  If the second run has not yet reached its
predecessor gate in stage~$i$, an active call to~$Y$ jumps past an unserved
gate of that run and forces a third run.  Once the second run has reached
that predecessor, it must use the complementary cell $(3-r,3-c)$, but a row
or column star through $(r,c)$ does not contain this cell.  Calling~$Y$ then
or after leaving the stage also forces a third run.  Since every selector
must eventually be called, the exact $\{X,Y\}$ realization is fatal in all
cases.

Thus one exact-pair atom is always excluded from the success event.  Every
such atom has probability $q^2(1-q)^2=h$.  Conditioning on independent advice
merely selects a deterministic local strategy, so it does not change the
argument.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Interleaved product bound]
\label{lem:four-star-product}
Every adaptive policy, including a randomized policy making arbitrary remote
calls, satisfies
\[
    \P(K_\pi\le2)\le(1-h)^L.
\]
Consequently,
\[
    \E[K_\pi]\ge3-(1-h)^L.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We first fix a deterministic policy.  View the preservation of two runs as a
finite reachability problem with one independent four-bit component per
stage.  A component becomes \emph{ready} when the first run reaches its input
gate and remains unresolved until all its selectors have been called and the
second-run routing, if needed, has been completed.  For a ready unresolved
stage, let $V(s)$ be the optimal conditional probability of completing a
valid local routing from its current local state~$s$.  The state records the
input row, all local answers seen so far, any selector placed on the first
run, its output column, whether the first run has left the stage, and whether
the second run has reached its complementary input gate.  In particular, an
active selector call after the first-run output but before that complementary
input gate is reached is a failed state, by the argument in the proof of
Lemma~\ref{lem:four-star-local}.  The Bellman definition gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:four-star-bellman}
    \E[V(s_{\mathrm{next}})\mid s]\le V(s)
\end{equation}
for every local action.  At an untouched stage,
Lemma~\ref{lem:four-star-local} gives $V(s)\le1-h$, for either input row and
conditional on any observations outside the stage.

For a future stage that is not yet ready, assign an upper potential as
follows.  Its potential is $1-h$ before any premature local query, it is~$1$
after one or more such queries have all answered inactive, and it is~$0$ if
one answers active.  The last case makes $K_\pi\le2$ impossible by the gate
argument preceding Lemma~\ref{lem:four-star-local}.  The first premature query
changes the expected potential by at most
\[
    q\cdot0+(1-q)\cdot1
    =1-q
    \le1-h,
\]
where $h\le q$.  Further premature queries start from potential~$1$ and have
expected next potential at most~$1$.

Take the product of the $L$ local potentials, using factor~$1$ only after all
local selectors have been resolved and a valid two-chain routing has been
completed, and factor~$0$ for a failed stage.  After the first run leaves a
stage, its exact Bellman factor remains in the product until the second-run
part of that stage is resolved.  A selector call changes only one factor.
When the first run reaches a new input gate, an untouched factor becomes an
exact Bellman value at most~$1-h$, while a prematurely queried factor becomes
an exact conditional value at most~$1$.  Gate calls that violate the forced
layer order make the two-run event impossible and may also set the product
to zero.  Hence
\eqref{eq:four-star-bellman} and the preceding premature-query estimate make
the product a nonnegative supermartingale under arbitrary interleaving.

Its initial value is at most $(1-h)^L$.  At termination it is the indicator
that every stage admits the required local two-chain routing.  The event
$K_\pi\le2$ implies this terminal event, so taking expectations proves
\[
    \P(K_\pi\le2)\le(1-h)^L.
\]
For a randomized policy, condition on its private seed and average the same
bound.  Finally, $K_\pi\ge2$ implies
\[
    \E[K_\pi]
    \ge2+\P(K_\pi\ge3)
    =3-\P(K_\pi\le2)
    \ge3-(1-h)^L.
\]
\end{proof}

\subsection{Embedding the poset into a directed metric}

Fix $0<\varepsilon<1$.  For distinct clients $x,y$ of~$P_L$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:layered-poset-metric}
    d_\varepsilon(x,y)
    :=
    \begin{cases}
        \varepsilon,&x<y\text{ in }P_L,\\
        1,&x\not<y\text{ in }P_L,
    \end{cases}
    \qquad
    d_\varepsilon(r,x)=d_\varepsilon(x,r):=\frac12,
\end{equation}
and put $d_\varepsilon(x,x)=0$.

\begin{lemma}[Poset metric and run cost]
\label{lem:layered-poset-metric}
Equation~\eqref{eq:layered-poset-metric} defines a strictly positive directed
metric.  If a realization has $N$ active clients and a service order has
$K$ maximal increasing runs, then its closed-tour cost is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:layered-poset-run-cost}
    \varepsilon N+(1-\varepsilon)K.
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:layered-poset-posterior-identity}
    \opt(A)
    =
    \varepsilon N(A)
    +(1-\varepsilon)\operatorname{width}(P_L[A]).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For the triangle inequality, consider first three clients.  If two
consecutive arcs have length~$\varepsilon$, then $x<y<z$, so transitivity
gives $x<z$ and the direct arc also has length~$\varepsilon$.  If a direct
client arc has length~$1$, a two-arc client path with an intermediate client
distinct from both endpoints cannot consist of two $\varepsilon$-arcs and
therefore has length at least $1+\varepsilon$.  A degenerate intermediate
equal to an endpoint, or a detour through the depot, has length exactly~$1$.
Similarly, a two-leg path competing with a depot arc has length at least
$1/2+\varepsilon$ when its intermediate client is distinct and exactly
$1/2$ in the degenerate case.  Thus all directed triangle inequalities hold,
and all distances between distinct vertices are positive.

The two depot legs contribute~$1$.  Within the service order, the $N-K$
transitions inside increasing runs cost~$\varepsilon$, and the $K-1$ breaks
between runs cost~$1$.  The total is
\[
    1+\varepsilon(N-K)+(K-1)
    =\varepsilon N+(1-\varepsilon)K.
\]
The minimum number of increasing chains covering a finite poset equals its
width by Dilworth's theorem.  Concatenating such chains gives a service order
with that many runs, while the runs of every service order form a chain
cover.  Minimizing~\eqref{eq:layered-poset-run-cost} therefore gives
\eqref{eq:layered-poset-posterior-identity}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:layered-poset-gap}]
For an integer $t\ge2$, choose
\[
    L:=t^5,
    \qquad
    q:=t^{-2},
    \qquad
    \varepsilon:=t^{-10}=L^{-2},
\]
and let $J_t$ be the resulting instance.  Its expected number of active
clients is
\[
    \E[N]=2(L+1)+4Lq=O(L),
\]
so $\varepsilon\E[N]=o(1)$.  Lemma~\ref{lem:layered-poset-width} and
$Lq^3=t^{-1}$ give
\[
    \optpost(J_t)
    \le
    \varepsilon\E[N]
    +(1-\varepsilon)(2+4Lq^3)
    =2+o(1).
\]

On the adaptive side,
\[
    Lh
    =Lq^2(1-q)^2
    =t(1-t^{-2})^2
    \longrightarrow\infty.
\]
Hence $(1-h)^L\le\e^{-Lh}=o(1)$.  Lemmas~\ref{lem:four-star-product}
and~\ref{lem:layered-poset-metric} imply, for every policy~$\pi$,
\[
    \E[\cost(\pi)]
    \ge
    \varepsilon\E[N]
    +(1-\varepsilon)\bigl(3-(1-h)^L\bigr)
    =3-o(1).
\]
Taking the infimum over policies and combining the two estimates yields
\[
    \liminf_{t\to\infty}
    \frac{\optadapt(J_t)}{\optpost(J_t)}
    \ge\frac32.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[An explicit finite separation]
\label{cor:finite-layered-poset-gap}
There is a $120{,}002$-client asymmetric stochastic TSP instance satisfying
\[
    \frac{\optadapt}{\optpost}>\frac43.
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Take
\[
    q:=\frac1{100},
    \qquad
    L:=20{,}000,
    \qquad
    \varepsilon:=10^{-7}.
\]
Then $h=q^2(1-q)^2$ and
\[
    Lh=\frac{9801}{5000}>\frac{49}{25}.
\]
The exponential series gives the rational estimate
\[
    \e^{Lh}
    >
    \sum_{j=0}^{6}\frac{(49/25)^j}{j!}
    =
    \frac{1242847593301}{175781250000}
    >7.
\]
It follows that $(1-h)^L\le\e^{-Lh}<1/7$, and therefore
\[
    \inf_\pi\E[K_\pi]>\frac{20}{7}.
\]
On the other hand, Lemma~\ref{lem:layered-poset-width} gives
\[
    \E[\operatorname{width}(P_L[A])]
    \le2+L(4q^3-2q^4)
    =\frac{5199}{2500}.
\]
There are $2(L+1)+4L=120{,}002$ potential clients, and
\[
    \E[N]=2(L+1)+4Lq=40{,}802.
\]
Using Lemma~\ref{lem:layered-poset-metric}, three times the adaptive lower
bound minus four times the posterior upper bound is strictly larger than
\begin{align*}
    &(1-10^{-7})
      \left(
        3\cdot\frac{20}{7}
        -4\cdot\frac{5199}{2500}
      \right)
      -10^{-7}(40{,}802)\\
    &=
      (1-10^{-7})\frac{1107}{4375}
      -0.0040802
      >0.24.
\end{align*}
Thus $3\optadapt>4\optpost$, which proves the claim.
\end{proof}

\chapter{Backbone Circulations and Endpoint Rounding}
\label{ch:circulation}

This chapter develops a partial approach toward constructing an efficient
adaptive policy for asymmetric stochastic TSP.  The starting point is the
backbone method used for symmetric a~priori TSP~\cite{Shmoys2008}: sample a
set of clients according to their activation probabilities, route the sampled
clients, and attach the remaining clients to this backbone.  In a directed
metric, attaching a client to its nearest backbone vertex is not adequate,
because the cost of entering and leaving that client may be very different.
We therefore replace undirected detours by a directed circulation.

There are three results.  First, some backbone admits a fractional circulation
whose cost, together with the cost of a tour on the backbone, is at most
$O(\log(n+1))\optpost$.  Second, the part of this circulation passing through
clients outside the backbone can be rounded so that each such client chooses
one ordered pair of backbone vertices.  The rounding does not increase cost
and leaves only $O(\log(n+1))$ weighted imbalance across every interval of
the backbone order.  Third, after the activation set is known, Hoffman's
circulation theorem converts the active rounded chords into a closed walk
whose edge multiplicities are $O(\log(n+1))$, with high probability over the
realization.

These statements do \emph{not} yet give an adaptive policy.  In particular,
the last construction is offline: both its feasibility proof and the walk
itself depend on the complete activation set.  It therefore does not prescribe
which client an adaptive salesperson should call next.

\section{The backbone circulation relaxation}
\label{sec:circulation-lp}

Independently include each client $v\in V$ in a random set~$S$ with
probability~$p_v$.  We call
\[
    R:=S\cup\{r\}
    \qquad\text{and}\qquad
    U:=V\setminus S
\]
the \emph{backbone} and the set of \emph{outside clients}, respectively.
For a nonnegative vector~$z$ on directed pairs, use the notation
\[
    z(\delta^+(w)):=\sum_v z_{wv},
    \qquad
    z(\delta^-(w)):=\sum_u z_{uw}.
\]
For a fixed choice of~$S$, consider the following circulation relaxation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:backbone-circulation-lp}
\begin{aligned}
\min\quad
    &\sum_{u,v\in\{r\}\cup V}d(u,v)z_{uv}\\
\text{subject to}\quad
    &z(\delta^+(w))=z(\delta^-(w))
        &&\forall w\in\{r\}\cup V,\\
    &z(\delta^+(i))=z(\delta^-(i))=p_i
        &&\forall i\in U,\\
    &z_{ij}=0
        &&\forall i,j\in U,\\
    &z_{uv}\ge0
        &&\forall u,v\in\{r\}\cup V.
\end{aligned}
\tag{$\mathrm{CIRC}(S)$}
\end{equation}
For a feasible vector~$z$, write
\[
    \cost(z):=\sum_{u,v\in\{r\}\cup V}d(u,v)z_{uv},
\]
and let $c(S)$ denote the optimum value of
$\mathrm{CIRC}(S)$.  The prohibition on arcs between two
outside clients makes the intended structure explicit: flow that visits an
outside client enters from the backbone and immediately returns to the
backbone.  Thus the relaxation is a fractional directed analogue of assigning
a detour to each outside client.

\section{A low-cost fractional circulation}
\label{sec:low-cost-circulation}

We next show that the backbone tour and the circulation can be chosen to have
small total cost.  The logarithmic factor comes from the longest consecutive
run of outside clients on an optimal tour through two independent
realizations.

Let $A\subseteq V$ be an activation set sampled independently of~$S$, using
the same probabilities~$(p_v)_{v\in V}$, and put $C:=A\cup S$.  Fix an
optimal tour~$T_C$ on $\{r\}\cup C$.  Cutting this tour at the vertices
of~$R$ produces directed segments
\[
    P_j=(s_j,u_{j,1},\ldots,u_{j,k_j},s_{j+1}),
\]
where $s_j,s_{j+1}\in R$ and every internal vertex belongs to
$A\setminus S$.  Indices on the vertices of~$R$ are cyclic.  Define
\[
    M(A,S):=\max_j k_j.
\]

For every segment~$P_j$, send one unit of flow along
$s_j\to u_{j,\ell}\to s_{j+1}$ for each $1\le\ell\le k_j$, and send
$M(A,S)-k_j$ units directly from $s_j$ to~$s_{j+1}$.  Denote the resulting
flow by~$z^{A,S}$.  Every outside client in $A\setminus S$ has one unit of
incoming and outgoing flow.  Moreover, every backbone vertex has
$M(A,S)$ units entering from the preceding segment and the same amount
leaving along the next segment, so $z^{A,S}$ is a circulation.

By the directed triangle inequality, the cost of each two-arc detour
$s_j\to u_{j,\ell}\to s_{j+1}$, as well as the direct arc
$s_j\to s_{j+1}$, is at most the length of~$P_j$.  Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:realization-circulation-cost}
    \cost(z^{A,S})
    \le M(A,S)\cost(T_C)
    =M(A,S)\opt(C).
\end{equation}

For a fixed backbone~$S$, average this construction over~$A$:
\[
    z^S:=\E_A[z^{A,S}].
\]
If $i\in U$, then $i$ receives one unit of flow exactly when $i\in A$.
Hence its expected incoming and outgoing flow is~$p_i$.  All other
constraints in~\eqref{eq:backbone-circulation-lp} are preserved under
averaging, so $z^S$ is feasible for $\mathrm{CIRC}(S)$.
The fixed-pair construction also gives the same degree at every backbone
vertex:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ordinary-backbone-degree}
    z^S(\delta^+(s))=z^S(\delta^-(s))
    =Z(S)
    \quad\forall s\in R,
    \qquad
    Z(S):=\E_A[M(A,S)\mid S].
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Longest outside run]\label{lem:circulation-longest-run}
For every integer $t\ge1$ and every fixed set~$C$ with
$\P(A\cup S=C)>0$,
\[
    \P\bigl(M(A,S)\ge t\mid A\cup S=C\bigr)
    \le n2^{-t}.
\]
In particular,
\[
    \E\bigl[M(A,S)\mid A\cup S=C\bigr]
    =O(\log(n+1)).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Condition on $A\cup S=C$.  The tour~$T_C$ is then fixed.  For each
$v\in C$, the conditional probability that it is active but outside the
backbone is
\[
    \P(v\in A\setminus S\mid v\in A\cup S)
    =
    \frac{p_v(1-p_v)}{1-(1-p_v)^2}
    =
    \frac{1-p_v}{2-p_v}
    \le\frac12.
\]
The conditioning factors over clients, so these classifications remain
independent.  A run of length~$t$ can start in at most~$n$ positions of the
fixed cyclic tour.  A union bound therefore gives the claimed tail estimate.

Let $L:=\lceil\log_2(\max\{n,2\})\rceil$.  The tail-sum formula yields
\[
    \E[M(A,S)\mid A\cup S=C]
    \le
    L+\sum_{t>L}n2^{-t}
    =O(\log(n+1)).
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Low-cost backbone and circulation]
\label{prop:low-cost-backbone-circulation}
If $S$ is sampled independently with $\P(v\in S)=p_v$, then
\[
    \E_S\bigl[\opt(S)+c(S)\bigr]
    \le O(\log(n+1))\optpost.
\]
Consequently, there exists a fixed backbone~$S$ for which
\[
    \opt(S)+c(S)
    \le O(\log(n+1))\optpost.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The random set~$S$ has the same distribution as an activation set, and hence
\[
    \E_S[\opt(S)]=\optpost.
\]
Using the feasible solution~$z^S$, linearity of cost,
\eqref{eq:realization-circulation-cost}, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:circulation-longest-run}, we obtain
\begin{align*}
    \E_S[c(S)]
    &\le \E_S[\cost(z^S)]\\
    &=\E_{A,S}[\cost(z^{A,S})]\\
    &\le\E_{A,S}[M(A,S)\opt(A\cup S)]\\
    &\le O(\log(n+1))\,\E_{A,S}[\opt(A\cup S)].
\end{align*}
For every pair~$A,S$, concatenate optimal tours on
$\{r\}\cup A$ and $\{r\}\cup S$ and shortcut repeated vertices.  The directed
triangle inequality gives
\[
    \opt(A\cup S)\le\opt(A)+\opt(S).
\]
Both terms on the right have expectation~$\optpost$, so
\[
    \E_S[c(S)]\le O(\log(n+1))\optpost.
\]
Adding the expected backbone-tour cost proves the first statement.  The
existence statement follows by averaging.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A boosted backbone}
\label{sec:boosted-backbone}

For the sharper rounding and the offline construction below, we need two
additional properties: bounded circulation degree at backbone vertices and
small activation probabilities outside the backbone.  Both can be obtained
by boosting the sampling probabilities.

\begin{proposition}[Boosted backbone]\label{prop:boosted-backbone}
There are absolute constants $c,D_0,K_0>0$ such that the following holds.
Put
\[
    \lambda:=\left\lceil c\log(n+1)\right\rceil
    \qquad\text{and}\qquad
    q_v:=\min\{1,\lambda p_v\}.
\]
There exist a set~$S\subseteq V$, a tour~$T_R$ on
$R:=S\cup\{r\}$, and a feasible solution~$z$ to
$\mathrm{CIRC}(S)$ such that
\begin{align}
    \cost(T_R)+\cost(z)
    &\le K_0\log(n+1)\optpost,
    \label{eq:boosted-cost}\\
    z(\delta^+(s))=z(\delta^-(s))
    &\le D_0\log(n+1)
    \qquad\forall s\in R,
    \label{eq:boosted-degree}\\
    p_i&<\frac1\lambda
    \qquad\forall i\in V\setminus S.
    \label{eq:boosted-outside-probability}
\end{align}
The set~$S$ may be chosen from the product distribution with inclusion
probabilities~$(q_v)_{v\in V}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
First consider one ordinary sample~$B$, in which each client~$v$ is included
with probability~$p_v$, and the averaged witness~$z^B$ constructed above.
Equation~\eqref{eq:ordinary-backbone-degree} and
Lemma~\ref{lem:circulation-longest-run} give
\[
    \E_B[Z(B)]=O(\log(n+1)).
\]
The calculation in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:low-cost-backbone-circulation} also gives
\[
    \E_B[\cost(z^B)]
    =O(\log(n+1))\optpost.
\]
By Markov's inequality, after choosing absolute constants $D_0$ and~$K_0$
large enough, both
\[
    Z(B)\le D_0\log(n+1)
    \quad\text{and}\quad
    \cost(z^B)\le K_0\log(n+1)\optpost
\]
hold with probability at least~$3/4$.  Call such a sample \emph{good}.

Take $\lambda$ independent ordinary samples
$B^1,\ldots,B^\lambda$ and let $S_0:=\bigcup_jB^j$.  The probability that
none is good is at most $4^{-\lambda}$.  Moreover,
\[
    \P(v\in S_0)=1-(1-p_v)^\lambda
    \le\min\{1,\lambda p_v\}=q_v.
\]
Thus a product sample~$S$ with marginals~$q_v$ can be coupled with~$S_0$ so
that $S_0\subseteq S$.

Suppose $B^j$ is good.  The same vector~$z^{B^j}$ remains feasible when the
backbone is enlarged from~$B^j$ to~$S$.  Indeed, every client still outside
$S$ was outside $B^j$ and retains incoming and outgoing degree~$p_v$.
Every new backbone client $s\in S\setminus B^j$ previously had degree
$p_s\le1$, while the old backbone vertices have degree at most
$D_0\log(n+1)$.  The support restriction can only become weaker when the
outside set shrinks.  Hence~\eqref{eq:boosted-degree} holds and the circulation
cost is unchanged.

It remains to control the backbone tour.  After sampling~$S$, independently
give each $v\in S$ a uniform label in~$[\lambda]$, and let $S_j$ contain the
vertices carrying label~$j$.  The sets~$S_j$ partition~$S$, and, for each
fixed~$j$, client memberships are independent with
\[
    \P(v\in S_j)=\frac{q_v}{\lambda}
    =\min\left\{\frac1\lambda,p_v\right\}
    \le p_v.
\]
By a monotone coupling, $\E[\opt(S_j)]\le\optpost$.  Concatenating tours for
the sets~$S_j$ at the depot and shortcutting gives
\[
    \opt(S)\le\sum_{j=1}^{\lambda}\opt(S_j),
    \qquad
    \E[\opt(S)]\le\lambda\optpost.
\]
Markov's inequality therefore gives
$\opt(S)\le4\lambda\optpost$ with probability at least~$3/4$.
For a sufficiently large choice of~$c$, this event and the existence of a
good~$B^j$ have positive joint probability.  Fix an outcome in their
intersection and let~$T_R$ be an optimal tour on~$R$.  Enlarging~$K_0$ absorbs
the term $4\lambda\optpost$ and proves~\eqref{eq:boosted-cost}.

Finally, if $i\notin S$, then $q_i<1$.  By the definition of~$q_i$, this
implies $\lambda p_i<1$, which is
\eqref{eq:boosted-outside-probability}.
\end{proof}

\section{Endpoint rounding with interval discrepancy}
\label{sec:circulation-rounding}

Fix a backbone~$R=S\cup\{r\}$ and a feasible circulation~$z$.  Since there
are no arcs between outside clients, the flow through each $i\in U$ can be
coupled into paths with both endpoints in~$R$.  More precisely, there are
nonnegative variables
\[
    x_{i,ab}
    \qquad(i\in U,\ a,b\in R)
\]
such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coupled-marginals}
    \sum_{b\in R}x_{i,ab}=z_{ai},
    \qquad
    \sum_{a\in R}x_{i,ab}=z_{ib}.
\end{equation}
Such a coupling exists because both marginal sums equal~$p_i$; it can be
obtained, for example, by greedily matching the incoming and outgoing flow
at~$i$.  Put $y_{ab}:=z_{ab}$ for $a,b\in R$.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coupled-outside-constraint}
    \sum_{a,b\in R}x_{i,ab}=p_i
    \qquad\forall i\in U,
\end{equation}
and the coupling preserves cost:
\begin{align}
    &\sum_{i\in U}\sum_{a,b\in R}
       \bigl(d(a,i)+d(i,b)\bigr)x_{i,ab}
      +\sum_{a,b\in R}d(a,b)y_{ab}
      \notag\\
    &\hspace{35mm}=
      \sum_{u,v\in\{r\}\cup V}d(u,v)z_{uv}.
    \label{eq:coupled-cost}
\end{align}

Let
\[
    T_R=(s_0,s_1,\ldots,s_{m-1},s_0)
\]
be a tour on the backbone.  A \emph{cyclic interval} is a consecutive set of
vertices in this cyclic order.  For $I\subseteq R$ and $a,b\in R$, define
\[
    \chi_I(a,b):=\1_{\{a\in I\}}-\1_{\{b\in I\}},
\]
and define the weighted outgoing-minus-incoming imbalance of~$I$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:interval-imbalance}
    \Delta_{x,y}(I)
    :=
    \sum_{i\in U}\sum_{a,b\in R}\chi_I(a,b)x_{i,ab}
    +\sum_{a,b\in R}\chi_I(a,b)y_{ab}.
\end{equation}
Summing the original flow-conservation equations over the backbone vertices
in~$I$ shows that
\[
    \Delta_{x,y}(I)=0
    \qquad\forall I\subseteq R.
\]

The goal is to replace every $x_{i,ab}$ by either~$0$ or~$p_i$.  By
\eqref{eq:coupled-outside-constraint}, this assigns every outside client of
positive probability to exactly one ordered pair~$(a,b)$ of backbone
vertices.  Preserving all subset-balance equations while doing this need not
leave a direction in which to move.  We instead preserve only a laminar
family of interval equations and discard an equation once few fractional
variables still touch it.

\begin{lemma}[Endpoint rounding]\label{lem:circulation-endpoint-rounding}
Let $p_{\max}:=\max_{i\in U}p_i$, with $p_{\max}:=0$ when $U=\emptyset$.
There is a vector~$\widehat x$ satisfying
\[
    \widehat x_{i,ab}\in\{0,p_i\}
    \qquad\text{and}\qquad
    \sum_{a,b\in R}\widehat x_{i,ab}=p_i
    \quad\forall i\in U
\]
such that
\begin{align}
    \sum_{i\in U}\sum_{a,b\in R}
       \bigl(d(a,i)+d(i,b)\bigr)\widehat x_{i,ab}
    &\le
    \sum_{i\in U}\sum_{a,b\in R}
       \bigl(d(a,i)+d(i,b)\bigr)x_{i,ab},
    \label{eq:rounding-cost}\\
    |\Delta_{\widehat x,y}(I)|
    &\le O\bigl(p_{\max}\log^2(n+1)\bigr).
    \label{eq:rounding-interval-balance}
\end{align}
The second inequality holds for every cyclic interval~$I$ of~$T_R$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The claim is immediate when $|R|=1$, so assume $m:=|R|\ge2$.  Pad the linear
order $s_0,\ldots,s_{m-1}$ with empty positions to the next power of two and
let $\mathcal D$ be the standard dyadic interval family represented by the
nodes of the resulting binary interval tree.  Put
\[
    h:=\lceil\log_2 m\rceil,
    \qquad
    L:=8(h+1).
\]
Each backbone position belongs to at most $h+1$ members of~$\mathcal D$.
Consequently, a variable~$x_{i,ab}$ has nonzero coefficient in at most
$2(h+1)$ of the equations
\[
    \Delta_{x,y}(D)=0,
    \qquad D\in\mathcal D.
\]

Call a variable \emph{live} when $0<x_{i,ab}<p_i$, and let~$F$ be the set of
live variables.  Throughout the procedure we preserve every outside equation
\eqref{eq:coupled-outside-constraint}.  We also preserve a dyadic interval
equation as long as it touches at least~$L$ live variables; once fewer than
$L$ live variables touch it, we discard it permanently.

Let $c_1$ be the number of outside equations that contain a live variable.
No such equation can contain exactly one live variable: after all other
variables in the equation have values in~$\{0,p_i\}$, the equality with
right-hand side~$p_i$ would force the remaining variable to an endpoint as
well.  Hence
\[
    c_1\le\frac{|F|}{2}.
\]
Let $c_2$ be the number of currently preserved dyadic equations.  Counting
incidences between these equations and the live variables gives
\[
    c_2L
    \le 2(h+1)|F|,
\]
and therefore $c_2\le |F|/4$.  Thus
\[
    c_1+c_2<|F|.
\]
The homogeneous system associated with the preserved equalities has a
nonzero direction supported on~$F$.

Move in both signs of this direction until a live variable first reaches
either~$0$ or its upper bound~$p_i$.  The current point is a convex
combination of the two resulting endpoints.  Since the objective in
\eqref{eq:rounding-cost} is linear, at least one endpoint has cost no larger
than the current point; choose such an endpoint.  This preserves all current
equalities, never increases cost, and makes at least one additional variable
nonlive.  Repeating the step terminates with every variable in
$\{0,p_i\}$, while all outside equations still hold.  This proves
\eqref{eq:rounding-cost} and the first displayed properties of~$\widehat x$.

Consider a dyadic equation at the moment it is discarded.  Its imbalance is
still zero, and fewer than~$L$ of its variables are live.  Only those
variables can subsequently change.  Each changes in absolute value by at
most $p_i\le p_{\max}$ and has coefficient in~$\{-1,0,1\}$.  It follows that
at the end of the procedure,
\[
    |\Delta_{\widehat x,y}(D)|\le Lp_{\max}
    \qquad\forall D\in\mathcal D.
\]
Every linear interval has a canonical decomposition into $O(h+1)$ disjoint
dyadic intervals.  A cyclic interval is the union of at most two linear
intervals, so it has a decomposition into $O(h+1)$ members of~$\mathcal D$.
The imbalance is additive over disjoint unions.  Therefore
\[
    |\Delta_{\widehat x,y}(I)|
    \le O((h+1)Lp_{\max})
    =O\bigl(p_{\max}\log^2(n+1)\bigr)
\]
for every cyclic interval~$I$, proving
\eqref{eq:rounding-interval-balance}.
\end{proof}

Combining the low-cost construction and the endpoint rounding gives the main
partial result of this chapter.

\begin{theorem}[Backbone circulation and rounding]
\label{thm:backbone-circulation-rounding}
For every stochastic TSP instance on a directed metric, there exist a backbone
$R=S\cup\{r\}$, a tour
$T_R=(s_0,\ldots,s_{m-1},s_0)$, nonnegative backbone variables
$(y_{ab})_{a,b\in R}$, and, for every $i\in V\setminus S$ with $p_i>0$, an
ordered pair $(a_i,b_i)\in R\times R$ such that
\begin{align}
    \cost(T_R)
    +\sum_{a,b\in R}d(a,b)y_{ab}
    +\sum_{\substack{i\in V\setminus S\\p_i>0}}
       p_i\bigl(d(a_i,i)+d(i,b_i)\bigr)
    \le O(\log(n+1))\optpost.
    \label{eq:partial-circulation-total-cost}
\end{align}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rounded-backbone-degree}
    \sum_{b\in R}y_{ab}\le O(\log(n+1)),
    \qquad
    \sum_{b\in R}y_{ba}\le O(\log(n+1))
    \qquad\forall a\in R,
\end{equation}
and every outside client satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rounded-small-probability}
    p_i\le O\left(\frac1{\log(n+1)}\right).
\end{equation}
If $\widehat x_{i,a_i b_i}:=p_i$ and all other $\widehat x$-variables are
zero, then for every cyclic interval~$I$ of~$T_R$,
\[
    |\Delta_{\widehat x,y}(I)|
    \le O(\log(n+1)).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Choose $S$ and a feasible circulation~$z$ as in
Proposition~\ref{prop:boosted-backbone}.  Couple the flow through each outside
client according to~\eqref{eq:coupled-marginals}, and apply
Lemma~\ref{lem:circulation-endpoint-rounding}.  The coupling preserves the
circulation cost by~\eqref{eq:coupled-cost}, and the rounding does not
increase it by~\eqref{eq:rounding-cost}.  Thus
\eqref{eq:partial-circulation-total-cost} follows from
\eqref{eq:boosted-cost}.  The backbone variables~$y$ form a subvector of~$z$,
so~\eqref{eq:rounded-backbone-degree} follows from
\eqref{eq:boosted-degree}; \eqref{eq:rounded-small-probability} is
\eqref{eq:boosted-outside-probability}.  Finally,
$p_{\max}=O(1/\log(n+1))$, and hence
\eqref{eq:rounding-interval-balance} improves to
$O(\log(n+1))$.
\end{proof}

\section{An offline bounded-multiplicity closed walk}
\label{sec:offline-backbone-walk}

We now use the rounded structure after the active set has been revealed.
The construction in this section is therefore offline.  Its main tool is the
following classical form of Hoffman's circulation theorem~\cite{Hoffman1960}.

\begin{theorem}[Hoffman's circulation theorem]\label{thm:hoffman-circulation}
Let $G=(W,E)$ be a directed multigraph and let
$\ell,u:E\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}$ satisfy $\ell_e\le u_e$ for every arc~$e$.
There is a circulation~$g$ with
\[
    \ell_e\le g_e\le u_e
    \qquad\forall e\in E
\]
if and only if, for every $X\subseteq W$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hoffman-condition}
    \ell(\delta^-(X))\le u(\delta^+(X)).
\end{equation}
If $\ell$ and~$u$ are integral, then~$g$ may be chosen integral.
\end{theorem}

Fix the objects supplied by
Theorem~\ref{thm:backbone-circulation-rounding}.  For an activation
set~$A$, every active outside client $i\in A\cap U$ supplies the directed
chord
\[
    e_i=(a_i,b_i)
\]
on~$R$; after constructing a circulation on these collapsed chords, we will
expand $e_i$ back to the path $a_i\to i\to b_i$.  Parallel chords are kept as
distinct arcs.

For $X\subseteq R$, write
\begin{align*}
    F_A^-(X)
    &:=
    \bigl|\{i\in A\cap U:a_i\notin X,\ b_i\in X\}\bigr|,\\
    F_A^+(X)
    &:=
    \bigl|\{i\in A\cap U:a_i\in X,\ b_i\notin X\}\bigr|,
\end{align*}
and let their expectations be
\begin{align*}
    \mu^-(X)
    &:=
    \sum_{\substack{i\in U\\a_i\notin X,\ b_i\in X}}p_i,\\
    \mu^+(X)
    &:=
    \sum_{\substack{i\in U\\a_i\in X,\ b_i\notin X}}p_i.
\end{align*}
Also put
\[
    y^+(X):=\sum_{\substack{a\in X\\b\notin X}}y_{ab},
    \qquad
    y^-(X):=\sum_{\substack{a\notin X\\b\in X}}y_{ab}.
\]

Let $C_R$ denote the directed backbone cycle given by~$T_R$, and define
\[
    q(X):=|\delta_{C_R}^+(X)|.
\]
For a nonempty proper subset~$X$, this is the number of cyclic intervals in
the decomposition of~$X$; it also equals
$|\delta_{C_R}^-(X)|$.  Set $q(\emptyset)=q(R)=0$.

\begin{lemma}[Uniform cut concentration]\label{lem:offline-cut-concentration}
There is an absolute constant $C_0$ such that, with probability at least
$1-(n+1)^{-5}$ over the activation set~$A$, every $X\subseteq R$ satisfies
\begin{align}
    F_A^-(X)
    &\le2\mu^-(X)+C_0q(X)\log(n+1),
    \label{eq:cut-concentration-in}\\
    \mu^+(X)
    &\le2F_A^+(X)+C_0q(X)\log(n+1).
    \label{eq:cut-concentration-out}
\end{align}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix $X$ with $q:=q(X)\ge1$.  The variable $F_A^-(X)$ is a sum of independent
Bernoulli variables with mean~$\mu^-(X)$.  A standard Chernoff bound gives,
for every $t\ge0$,
\[
    \P\bigl(F_A^-(X)>2\mu^-(X)+t\bigr)\le\e^{-t/3}.
\]
For the other direction, if $\mu^+(X)\le t$, then
\eqref{eq:cut-concentration-out} holds automatically with~$t$ in place of
its additive term.  If $\mu^+(X)>t$, a violation implies
$F_A^+(X)<\mu^+(X)/2$, whose probability is at most
\[
    \e^{-\mu^+(X)/8}\le\e^{-t/8}.
\]

There are at most $|R|^{2q}\le(n+1)^{2q}$ subsets of a cyclic order having
$q$ interval components: it is enough to choose their $2q$ boundary
positions.  Set $t=C_0q\log(n+1)$, choose $C_0$ sufficiently large, and take
a union bound over all $q\ge1$ and both inequalities.  The resulting failure
probability is at most $(n+1)^{-5}$.  The two sets with $q=0$ satisfy both
inequalities trivially.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Offline bounded-multiplicity walk]
\label{thm:offline-backbone-walk}
Let $A$ be an independent activation set.  With probability at least
$1-(n+1)^{-5}$, there is an offline closed walk~$W_A$ with the following
properties:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item for every active outside client $i\in A\cap U$, the walk traverses
    both arcs of $a_i\to i\to b_i$ between one and four times;
    \item every backbone-cycle edge is traversed at least once and at most
    $O(\log(n+1))$ times; and
    \item every auxiliary backbone arc $a\to b$ with $y_{ab}>0$ is traversed
    at most $O(\log(n+1))$ times.
\end{enumerate}
Thus every active outside chord is used at least once and every edge used by
the offline walk has multiplicity $O(\log(n+1))$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix a realization satisfying
Lemma~\ref{lem:offline-cut-concentration}.  By
Theorem~\ref{thm:backbone-circulation-rounding}, there is an absolute constant
$C_\Delta$ such that every cyclic interval~$I$ satisfies
\[
    |\Delta_{\widehat x,y}(I)|
    \le C_\Delta\log(n+1).
\]
If $X$ is the disjoint union of $q=q(X)$ cyclic intervals, additivity of the
imbalance gives
\[
    \bigl|
       \mu^+(X)-\mu^-(X)+y^+(X)-y^-(X)
    \bigr|
    \le C_\Delta q\log(n+1).
\]
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mean-cut-comparison}
    \mu^-(X)
    \le\mu^+(X)+y^+(X)+C_\Delta q\log(n+1).
\end{equation}
Combining~\eqref{eq:cut-concentration-in},
\eqref{eq:mean-cut-comparison}, and
\eqref{eq:cut-concentration-out} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:realized-cut-comparison}
    F_A^-(X)
    \le
    4F_A^+(X)+2y^+(X)+
    (2C_\Delta+3C_0)q\log(n+1)
    \qquad\forall X\subseteq R.
\end{equation}

We apply Theorem~\ref{thm:hoffman-circulation} to a directed multigraph on
vertex set~$R$.  It has three types of arcs, with the following lower and
upper bounds:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item each active outside chord~$e_i=(a_i,b_i)$ has
    $\ell_{e_i}=1$ and $u_{e_i}=4$;
    \item each auxiliary backbone arc $a\to b$ with $y_{ab}>0$ has
    $\ell_{ab}=0$ and $u_{ab}=\lceil2y_{ab}\rceil$; and
    \item each edge of the directed cycle~$C_R$ has lower bound~$1$ and
    upper bound
    \[
        K:=
        \left\lceil
        (2C_\Delta+3C_0)\log(n+1)
        \right\rceil+1.
    \]
\end{enumerate}
Coincident arcs of different types are treated as parallel arcs.

For every $X\subseteq R$, the total lower bound on arcs entering~$X$ is
\[
    \ell(\delta^-(X))=F_A^-(X)+q(X).
\]
The total upper bound on arcs leaving~$X$ satisfies
\[
    u(\delta^+(X))
    \ge4F_A^+(X)+2y^+(X)+Kq(X).
\]
Inequality~\eqref{eq:realized-cut-comparison} and the definition of~$K$
therefore imply Hoffman's condition~\eqref{eq:hoffman-condition}.  All bounds
are integral, so there is an integral circulation~$g$ within them.

Every auxiliary backbone arc has
\[
    g_{ab}\le\lceil2y_{ab}\rceil
    \le 2D_0\log(n+1)+1
\]
by~\eqref{eq:rounded-backbone-degree}.  The cycle-edge multiplicity is at
most~$K=O(\log(n+1))$, while every active outside chord has multiplicity
between one and four.

Finally, expand every chord~$e_i$ carrying $g_{e_i}$ units into
$g_{e_i}$ copies of the path $a_i\to i\to b_i$.  The resulting directed
multigraph is balanced.  Its support is strongly connected: it contains the
whole directed backbone cycle, and every active outside vertex has a path
from and back to that cycle.  It therefore has an Euler tour, which is the
claimed closed walk~$W_A$.
\end{proof}

\section{The remaining policy-construction gap}
\label{sec:circulation-gap}

Theorem~\ref{thm:backbone-circulation-rounding} turns every outside client
$i$ into a probability-weighted directed chord
\[
    a_i\longrightarrow i\longrightarrow b_i
\]
of the backbone order.  This is useful structural information: the total
weighted length of the chords is small, and their aggregate imbalance on
every backbone interval is logarithmically bounded.
Theorem~\ref{thm:offline-backbone-walk} shows that, for a typical realization,
there is no purely combinatorial congestion obstruction: after seeing the
whole active set, the chords can be joined into a closed walk with logarithmic
edge multiplicity.  Nevertheless, this does not specify how an adaptive
salesperson should execute the chords.  Three difficulties remain:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the Hoffman circulation, and hence its Euler tour, is chosen using
    the complete realization~$A$;
    \item an adaptive execution must choose its next call from its current
    location without knowing which uncalled chords will become active; and
    \item bounded multiplicity alone does not give a cost bound for the
    auxiliary backbone arcs, because an arc with very small fractional
    value~$y_{ab}$ may still receive one integral copy.
\end{itemize}

Thus the missing step is an \emph{online}, cost-sensitive version of the
offline construction: a policy must discover and schedule the realized
chords through its calls while keeping its expected travel cost comparable
to~\eqref{eq:partial-circulation-total-cost}.  Hoffman's theorem establishes
existence after all activations are known, but it does not provide this causal
implementation.

\chapter{Approximate A Priori TSP via Dynamic Programming}\label{ch:dp}

We give a deterministic $(1+\varepsilon)$-approximation algorithm for
a~priori TSP on directed metrics.  For every fixed $\varepsilon>0$, its
running time is $c_\varepsilon^n\poly(n)$.

The algorithm is based on the following view of an a~priori order.  Once an
order $\pi=(r,v_1,\dots,v_n,r)$ is fixed, an edge from $v_i$ to $v_j$ is paid
exactly when $v_i$ and $v_j$ are active and all clients between them in the
order are inactive.  Thus the expected cost is a sum over ordered pairs.  The
coefficient of a pair contains the product
\[
    Q(i,j)=\prod_{i<k<j}(1-p_k),
\]
the probability that the interval between the two endpoints is completely
inactive.

Pairs with very small $Q(i,j)$ correspond to rare long shortcuts.  The first
part of the chapter proves that, up to a factor $1+O(\varepsilon)$, such pairs
may be discarded.  The proof does not try to charge each discarded pair
separately.  Instead, it slightly increases all activation probabilities.  This
adds enough active clients to split long jumps, while the directed triangle
inequality ensures that the shortcut cost can only increase.  This gives a
uniform bound comparing the full cost with the truncated pairwise cost.

It remains to minimize this truncated objective.  Directly remembering the
entire prefix of a partial order would be too expensive, so we group clients
into blocks: high-probability clients are kept as singletons, while
low-probability clients are grouped into small-mass blocks.  For each block we
precompute the best internal order.  Between two blocks, the truncated objective
depends only on the complete blocks between them.  Consequently, when the
dynamic program builds the block order from left to right, blocks that are
separated from the current end by inactivity product at most~$\eta$ can never
interact with future blocks and can be forgotten.

The rest of the chapter follows this outline: first the pairwise objective,
then the truncation lemma, then the block objective and its comparison with the
original order.  After that we give the dynamic program and choose the
parameters that yield the $(1+\varepsilon)$ guarantee.

\section{The pairwise objective}

Let
\[
    \pi=(r,v_1,\dots,v_n,r)
\]
be an a~priori order.  Put $v_0=v_{n+1}=r$ and $p_0=p_{n+1}=1$.  For brevity,
write
\[
    p_i:=p_{v_i},
    \qquad
    d_{ij}:=d(v_i,v_j).
\]
For $0\le i<j\le n+1$, define
\[
    Q(i,j):=\prod_{i<k<j}(1-p_k).
\]
Thus $Q(i,j)$ is the probability that every client strictly between $v_i$ and
$v_j$ is inactive.  The expected cost of $\pi$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:apriori-pairwise}
    \cost(\pi)
    =
    \sum_{0\le i<j\le n+1}p_i p_j Q(i,j)d_{ij}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, the term indexed by $(i,j)$ is paid exactly when $v_i$ and $v_j$ are
consecutive active vertices in the shortcut of~$\pi$.

Fix $0<\eta<1$.  We retain a pair $(i,j)$ when
$Q(i,j)>\eta$, and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:local-pair-cost}
    \cost^{\loc}_{\eta}(\pi)
    :=
    \sum_{\substack{0\le i<j\le n+1\\Q(i,j)>\eta}}
    p_i p_j Q(i,j)d_{ij}.
\end{equation}

\section{\texorpdfstring{Truncating pairs with small \(Q\)}{Truncating pairs with small Q}}

We avoid choosing a splitting vertex separately for every discarded pair.
Instead, we slightly increase all activation probabilities.  The additional
active clients split all long jumps simultaneously, and the directed triangle
inequality shows that this can only increase the shortcut cost.

The parameter $\alpha$ measures the strength of this additional activation
round.  Write $q_v:=1-p_v$.  Besides the original active set, independently
activate $v$ with probability $1-q_v^\alpha$.  Then $v$ is absent from both
rounds with probability
\[
    q_v q_v^\alpha=q_v^{1+\alpha}.
\]
Consequently, a gap whose original inactivity probability is $Q(i,j)$ remains
empty after the additional round with probability
\[
    Q(i,j)^{1+\alpha}=Q(i,j)\,Q(i,j)^\alpha.
\]
Thus every discarded pair with $Q(i,j)\le\eta$ gains the uniform damping factor
$Q(i,j)^\alpha\le\eta^\alpha$.  The price is that each endpoint activation
probability grows by at most a factor $1+\alpha$.  The proof below formalizes
this tradeoff between the endpoint factor $(1+\alpha)^2$ and the damping factor
$\eta^\alpha$.

For an activation vector $s=(s_v)_{v\in V}$, let $\cost_s(\pi)$ denote the
expected cost of~$\pi$ under independent activation probabilities~$s_v$.
Thus $\cost_p(\pi)=\cost(\pi)$ for the original vector~$p$.

\begin{lemma}[Truncation by increasing activation probabilities]
\label{lem:long-gap-truncation}
Let $\alpha>0$, put $\kappa:=(1+\alpha)^2$, and suppose that
$0<\eta<1$ and $\kappa\eta^\alpha<1$.  Then every a~priori order~$\pi$
satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:long-gap-truncation}
    \cost(\pi)
    \le
    \frac{\kappa}{1-\kappa\eta^\alpha}\,
    \cost^{\loc}_{\eta}(\pi).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Define
\[
    \widetilde p_v:=1-(1-p_v)^{1+\alpha}.
\]
To couple the two activation vectors, first sample the original active set~$A$.
Independently, form a set~$B$ by including each client~$v$ with probability
$1-(1-p_v)^\alpha$.  Then $\widetilde A:=A\cup B$ has independent activation
probabilities~$\widetilde p_v$.

For every outcome, $A\subseteq\widetilde A$, and $\pi\vert_A$ is obtained from
$\pi\vert_{\widetilde A}$ by shortcutting vertices.  Hence the directed
triangle inequality gives
\[
    \cost(\pi\vert_A)\le \cost(\pi\vert_{\widetilde A}).
\]
Taking expectations,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:activation-monotonicity}
    \cost(\pi)\le \cost_{\widetilde p}(\pi).
\end{equation}

Convexity of $x\mapsto x^{1+\alpha}$ gives
\[
    \widetilde p_v
    =1-(1-p_v)^{1+\alpha}
    \le(1+\alpha)p_v.
\]
Moreover,
\[
    \widetilde Q(i,j)
    =\prod_{i<k<j}(1-\widetilde p_k)
    =Q(i,j)^{1+\alpha}.
\]
Thus the coefficient of $d_{ij}$ under~$\widetilde p$ is at most
\begin{align*}
    \widetilde p_i\widetilde p_j\widetilde Q(i,j)
    &\le
    \kappa p_ip_jQ(i,j)^{1+\alpha}\\
    &=\kappa Q(i,j)^\alpha\bigl(p_ip_jQ(i,j)\bigr).
\end{align*}

Set
\[
    T:=\cost(\pi),
    \qquad
    C:=\cost^{\loc}_{\eta}(\pi).
\]
On retained pairs, $Q(i,j)^\alpha\le1$; on discarded pairs,
$Q(i,j)^\alpha\le\eta^\alpha$.  Therefore
\[
    T
    \le \cost_{\widetilde p}(\pi)
    \le \kappa\bigl(C+\eta^\alpha(T-C)\bigr)
    \le \kappa C+\kappa\eta^\alpha T.
\]
Rearranging proves~\eqref{eq:long-gap-truncation}.
\end{proof}

\section{The block objective}

The dynamic program will order blocks rather than individual clients.  We
therefore define an objective on admissible ordered partitions.  For endpoints
in different blocks, this objective retains the exact inactivity factors of all
complete intervening blocks but replaces the factors contributed by vertices
inside the two endpoint blocks by~$1$.  This gives an upper bound on the
retained pair contribution and allows the contribution of a new block to be
evaluated from a bounded suffix of the preceding blocks.

Fix $0<\delta<1/4$.  A client is \emph{heavy} if
$p_v>\delta/2$ and \emph{light} otherwise.  For a set of clients~$B$, write
\[
    \mu(B):=\sum_{v\in B}p_v,
    \qquad
    q(B):=\prod_{v\in B}(1-p_v).
\]
For the depot block $\{r\}$, set $q(\{r\})=1$.

\begin{definition}[Admissible blocks and partitions]\label{def:admissible-partition}
A block is \emph{admissible} if it is either
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item a singleton containing one heavy client, or
    \item a nonempty set of light clients with total mass at most~$\delta$.
\end{enumerate}
An ordered partition $(B_1,\dots,B_m)$ is admissible if all its blocks are
admissible and, whenever $B_{t-1}$ and $B_t$ are both light blocks,
\[
    \mu(B_t)\ge\delta/2.
\]
Thus, in every consecutive run of light blocks, only the first block may have
mass below~$\delta/2$.
\end{definition}

For a light block~$B$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:internal-block-cost}
    I(B)
    :=
    \min_{\sigma}
    \sum_{x<_\sigma y}p_xp_y d(x,y),
\end{equation}
where the minimum is over all orders~$\sigma$ of~$B$.  For a heavy singleton
and for the depot block, set $I=0$.  The values $I(B)$, together with a
minimizing order, can be computed by the recurrence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:internal-block-recurrence}
    I(B)
    =
    \min_{y\in B}
    \left(
        I(B\setminus\{y\})
        +\sum_{x\in B\setminus\{y\}}p_xp_y d(x,y)
    \right),
    \qquad I(\emptyset)=0.
\end{equation}
Indeed, the second term is exactly the contribution of the pairs whose last
vertex is~$y$.

Given an admissible ordered partition
$\mathcal B=(B_1,\dots,B_m)$, order each light block according to a minimizer in
\eqref{eq:internal-block-cost}, and concatenate the block orders.  Denote the
resulting a~priori order by~$\pi(\mathcal B)$.  Put
$B_0=B_{m+1}=\{r\}$ and $p_r=1$, and define
\[
    C(B_i,B_j)
    :=
    \sum_{x\in B_i}\sum_{y\in B_j}p_xp_y d(x,y).
\]
Define the \emph{block objective} by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:block-objective}
    F_\eta(\mathcal B)
    :=
    \sum_{t=1}^m I(B_t)
    +
    \sum_{\substack{0\le i<j\le m+1\\
                     \prod_{i<t<j}q(B_t)>\eta}}
    \left(\prod_{i<t<j}q(B_t)\right)C(B_i,B_j).
\end{equation}
The first sum accounts for pairs whose endpoints lie in the same block.  The
second sum accounts for pairs in different blocks whenever the inactivity
product of the complete intervening blocks exceeds~$\eta$.

\begin{lemma}[Comparison with the exact objective]\label{lem:block-comparison}
The block objective satisfies the following two inequalities.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item For every admissible ordered partition~$\mathcal B$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:block-objective-dominates-local}
        \cost^{\loc}_{\eta}(\pi(\mathcal B))
        \le F_\eta(\mathcal B).
    \end{equation}

    \item For every a~priori order~$\pi$, there is an admissible ordered
    partition $\mathcal B_\pi$ into consecutive sets of~$\pi$ such that
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:block-objective-from-order}
        F_\eta(\mathcal B_\pi)
        \le(1-\delta)^{-2}\cost(\pi).
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For~(i), first consider endpoints $x\in B_i$ and $y\in B_j$ with $i<j$.
In the order $\pi(\mathcal B)$, their exact coefficient is
\[
    p_xp_y\,
    q(B_i^{>x})\,
    \left(\prod_{i<t<j}q(B_t)\right)\,
    q(B_j^{<y}),
\]
where $B_i^{>x}$ denotes the vertices of $B_i$ after~$x$, and
$B_j^{<y}$ the vertices of $B_j$ before~$y$.  If $(x,y)$ is retained in
$\cost^{\loc}_{\eta}$, then its exact inactivity probability exceeds
$\eta$.  Therefore the product over the complete intervening blocks also
exceeds $\eta$, so the corresponding block pair occurs in
\eqref{eq:block-objective}.  Dropping the two endpoint factors can only
increase its coefficient.  If both endpoints lie in the same light block~$B$,
then their exact coefficient is at most $p_xp_y$, and the sum of these upper
bounds in the chosen internal order is exactly~$I(B)$.  Summing over all
retained pairs proves~\eqref{eq:block-objective-dominates-local}.

For~(ii), keep every heavy client as a singleton.  In each maximal consecutive
run of light clients in~$\pi$, scan from right to left.  Close a block as soon
as its mass reaches~$\delta/2$, and continue with the remaining prefix of the
run.  Every closed block has mass in $[\delta/2,\delta]$, because every light
client has probability at most~$\delta/2$.  The only possible block of mass
below~$\delta/2$ is the leftmost block of the run.  The resulting partition
$\mathcal B_\pi$ is therefore admissible.

Consider a cross-block term of $F_\eta(\mathcal B_\pi)$ with endpoints
$x\in B_i$ and $y\in B_j$, where $i<j$.  Relative to the exact coefficient of
this pair in~$\pi$, the corresponding term of $F_\eta(\mathcal B_\pi)$ omits
only the factors corresponding to the vertices after~$x$ in~$B_i$ and
before~$y$ in~$B_j$.  For a heavy singleton or
a depot block, the corresponding factor is~$1$.  For a light block~$B$,
\[
    q(B')\ge1-\sum_{v\in B'}p_v\ge1-\delta
    \qquad\text{for every }B'\subseteq B.
\]
Thus every cross-block coefficient in $F_\eta(\mathcal B_\pi)$ is at most
$(1-\delta)^{-2}$ times the corresponding exact coefficient in~$\pi$.

Finally, let $B$ be a light block, and retain the order induced by~$\pi$ on~$B$.
If $T_B(\pi)$ denotes the exact contribution of pairs with both endpoints
in~$B$, then
\[
    I(B)
    \le
    \sum_{\substack{x,y\in B\\x<_\pi y}}p_xp_y d(x,y)
    \le
    \frac{1}{1-\delta}T_B(\pi).
\]
The second inequality holds because every inactivity factor between two
vertices of~$B$ is at least $q(B)\ge1-\delta$.  Summing the cross-block and
internal comparisons proves~\eqref{eq:block-objective-from-order}.
\end{proof}

\section{The dynamic program}

We now minimize $F_\eta$ exactly over all admissible ordered partitions.
Consider a partial partition
\[
    (B_0,B_1,\dots,B_t),
    \qquad B_0=\{r\}.
\]
If
\[
    \prod_{s=1}^t q(B_s)>\eta,
\]
its \emph{relevant suffix} is the entire prefix
$(B_0,B_1,\dots,B_t)$.  Otherwise, let $a$ be the largest index such that
\[
    \prod_{s=a}^t q(B_s)\le\eta.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
    \prod_{s=a}^t q(B_s)\le\eta
    \quad\text{and}\quad
    \prod_{s=a+1}^t q(B_s)>\eta.
\]
The relevant suffix is then $(B_a,B_{a+1},\dots,B_t)$.

A state consists of the set $U\subseteq V$ of already placed clients and the
relevant suffix~$\Omega$ of their partial partition.  Its value is the minimum,
over all represented prefixes, of the internal terms of the placed blocks plus
all cross-block terms whose right endpoint has already been placed.  The
initial state is $(\emptyset,(B_0))$ with value~$0$.

A feasible next block~$B$ is either a singleton containing an unplaced heavy
client or a nonempty set of unplaced light clients with
$\mu(B)\le\delta$.  If the last block of the current prefix and~$B$ are both
light, we additionally require $\mu(B)\ge\delta/2$.  Suppose
$\Omega=(B_a,\dots,B_t)$.  Appending~$B$ adds
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dp-increment}
    I(B)
    +
    \sum_{s=a}^t
    \left(\prod_{s<u\le t}q(B_u)\right)C(B_s,B).
\end{equation}
Every block in the relevant suffix occurs in this sum, because the complete
blocks between it and~$B$ have inactivity product greater than~$\eta$.
After the transition, the suffix is trimmed according to the same rule.  Once
every client has been placed, the terminal depot block~$\{r\}$ is appended
using the cross-block part of~\eqref{eq:dp-increment} and internal cost zero.

\begin{lemma}[Correctness and running time of the DP]\label{lem:local-dp}
For fixed $\delta$ and~$\eta$, the dynamic program minimizes
$F_\eta$ over all admissible ordered partitions and runs in time
$c_{\delta,\eta}^n\poly(n)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
When a block~$B$ is appended, the only new terms of~$F_\eta$ are its internal
term $I(B)$ and the cross-block terms whose right endpoint is~$B$.  These are
exactly the terms in~\eqref{eq:dp-increment}.

If a previous block is not in the relevant suffix, then the complete blocks
between it and the current end have inactivity product at most~$\eta$.
Appending more blocks only multiplies this product by numbers in~$[0,1]$.
Hence a forgotten block can never occur in a future term of~$F_\eta$.  The
state therefore contains precisely the information needed to evaluate every
continuation, and the recurrence is an exact shortest-path computation over all
admissible partial partitions.

It remains to bound the number of states.  We use the elementary inequality
\[
    \prod_{v\in S}(1-p_v)
    \le \exp\left(-\sum_{v\in S}p_v\right).
\]
If the whole prefix is retained, then its inactivity product exceeds
$\eta$, so its total activation mass is less than
$\log(1/\eta)$.  Otherwise, deleting the first retained block leaves an
inactivity product greater than~$\eta$, so the remaining blocks have total
activation mass less than $\log(1/\eta)$; the first retained block has mass at
most~$1$.  Thus the total activation mass of a relevant suffix is less than
\[
    M_\eta:=\log(1/\eta)+1.
\]

Every retained heavy singleton has mass greater than~$\delta/2$, and every
retained light block except the first one in a light run has mass at least
$\delta/2$.  Let $\mathcal G$ be the collection of these blocks.  Since their
total mass is less than $M_\eta$,
\[
    |\mathcal G|<2\frac{M_\eta}{\delta}.
\]
Every remaining retained block is the first light block of a light run.  The
number of light runs is at most one more than the number of retained heavy
blocks, and hence at most $|\mathcal G|+1$.  Therefore the number of retained
client blocks is at most
\[
    2|\mathcal G|+1
    <4\frac{M_\eta}{\delta}+1.
\]
Including the depot block, the relevant suffix has at most
\[
    K:=\left\lceil4\frac{M_\eta}{\delta}\right\rceil+2
    =O\!\left(\frac{\log(1/\eta)+1}{\delta}\right)
\]
blocks.  A state can be encoded by assigning each client one of the labels
``unplaced'', ``placed and forgotten'', or ``position $1,\dots,K$ in the
relevant suffix''.  Thus there are at most $(K+2)^n\poly(n)$ states.  A
transition may enumerate a subset of the unplaced light clients, contributing
at most another factor~$2^n$, and all numerical terms can be evaluated in
polynomial time.  This gives a running time
$c_{\delta,\eta}^n\poly(n)$.  The recurrence
\eqref{eq:internal-block-recurrence} for the values $I(B)$ takes
$O(n2^n)$ time and is absorbed in the same bound.
\end{proof}

\section{Approximation guarantee}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:directed-apriori-ptas}
For every fixed $0<\varepsilon\le1$, a~priori TSP on directed
metrics has a deterministic algorithm running in time
$c_\varepsilon^n\poly(n)$ and returning an a~priori order~$\hat\pi$ such that
\[
    \cost(\hat\pi)\le(1+\varepsilon)\optprior.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Set
\[
    \delta:=\frac{\varepsilon}{16},
    \qquad
    \alpha:=\frac{\varepsilon}{32},
    \qquad
    \eta:=\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{64}\right)^{32/\varepsilon}.
\]
Then
\[
    \eta^\alpha=\frac{\varepsilon}{64}.
\]

Let $\pi^*$ be an optimal a~priori order.  By
Lemma~\ref{lem:block-comparison}(ii), there is an admissible ordered partition
$\mathcal B^*$ into consecutive sets of~$\pi^*$ such that
\[
    F_\eta(\mathcal B^*)
    \le(1-\delta)^{-2}\cost(\pi^*)
    =(1-\delta)^{-2}\optprior.
\]
Let $\widehat{\mathcal B}$ be a partition minimizing $F_\eta$, as computed by
Lemma~\ref{lem:local-dp}, and put
$\hat\pi:=\pi(\widehat{\mathcal B})$.  Lemma~\ref{lem:block-comparison}(i)
gives
\[
    \cost^{\loc}_{\eta}(\hat\pi)
    \le F_\eta(\widehat{\mathcal B})
    \le F_\eta(\mathcal B^*)
    \le(1-\delta)^{-2}\optprior.
\]
Applying Lemma~\ref{lem:long-gap-truncation},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:final-approximation-chain}
    \cost(\hat\pi)
    \le
    \frac{(1+\alpha)^2}
         {1-(1+\alpha)^2\eta^\alpha}
    (1-\delta)^{-2}\optprior.
\end{equation}

Put $\kappa:=(1+\alpha)^2$.  Since $0<\varepsilon\le1$,
\[
    \kappa
    =1+\frac{\varepsilon}{16}+\frac{\varepsilon^2}{1024}
    \le1+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}
    \le\frac98.
\]
Therefore
\[
    \kappa\eta^\alpha
    =\kappa\frac{\varepsilon}{64}
    \le\frac{9\varepsilon}{512}
    <\frac{\varepsilon}{32}.
\]
Using $(1-x)^{-1}\le1+2x$ for $0\le x\le1/2$, we get
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\kappa}{1-\kappa\eta^\alpha}
    &\le
    \left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}\right)
    \left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{16}\right)\\
    &\le 1+\frac{25\varepsilon}{128}
    <1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.
\end{align*}
Also,
\[
    (1-\delta)^{-2}
    \le(1-2\delta)^{-1}
    =\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon/8}
    \le1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.
\]
Substituting these bounds into~\eqref{eq:final-approximation-chain} yields
\[
    \cost(\hat\pi)
    \le
    \left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)^2\optprior
    \le(1+\varepsilon)\optprior.
\]
Finally,
\[
    \log(1/\eta)
    =\frac{32}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{64}{\varepsilon},
\]
so $\delta$ and~$\eta$ depend only on~$\varepsilon$.  The running time remains
$c_\varepsilon^n\poly(n)$.
\end{proof}

\section{Directions for future research}

The results of this thesis suggest several possible directions for subsequent
work.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Symmetric clairvoyance gap.}
    What is the correct supremum of the clairvoyance gap for symmetric stochastic TSP?
    The three-chain construction gives a lower bound of~$11/10$, while Proposition~\ref{prop:general-p} gives an upper bound of~$4/3$ for the special case of one stochastic vertex.

    \item \textbf{Symmetric adaptivity gap.}
    Our probabilistic construction gives a lower bound of~$2$, while the
    obliviousness-gap upper bound of~$3$ also bounds the adaptivity gap.
    Is the adaptivity gap for symmetric metrics equal to~$2$, or can a
    different metric construction produce a larger separation?

    \item \textbf{Asymmetric clairvoyance gap.}
    Is the clairvoyance gap for asymmetric metrics $\Theta(1)$?  Our
    layered-poset construction gives a lower bound of~$3/2$, strengthening
    the recursive construction whose ratio tends to~$4/3$.  Can this be
    matched by an $O(1)$ upper bound, or does the gap grow with~$n$?

    \item \textbf{Efficient adaptive policies.}
    Can one design polynomial-time adaptive policies whose expected cost is within a constant factor of~$\optadapt$ for asymmetric metrics?
    Chapter~\ref{ch:circulation} carries the backbone-circulation approach
    through a low-cost fractional construction and an endpoint rounding with
    logarithmic interval discrepancy.  Hoffman's theorem also gives a
    logarithmic-multiplicity closed walk after the full realization is known.
    The remaining question is whether this offline existence argument can be
    made causal and cost-sensitive, so that the rounded chords can be
    discovered and scheduled by an adaptive policy.

    \item \textbf{Faster a~priori approximation algorithms.}
    Our $(1{+}\varepsilon)$-approximation for directed a~priori TSP runs in
    time $c_\varepsilon^n\poly(n)$ and is therefore exponential in~$n$.
    A polynomial-time $(1{+}\varepsilon)$-approximation scheme would also
    apply when every vertex has activation probability~$1$, and would thus
    give a PTAS for the classical directed TSP.  Since that problem is
    APX-hard, this is impossible unless $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$~\cite{Karpinski2015}.
    A more realistic direction is
    to seek a polynomial-time constant-factor approximation for directed
    a~priori TSP, or more generally to improve the tradeoff between running
    time and approximation ratio.
\end{enumerate}
