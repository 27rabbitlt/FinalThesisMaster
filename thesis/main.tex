\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

We study the stochastic traveling salesperson problem on a finite set of
vertices with travel costs satisfying the triangle inequality.  One
designated, always-active vertex is the depot, and every other vertex is
independently active with a given probability.  The salesperson must start
and end at the depot, visit every active vertex, and minimize the expected
travel cost.

An a~posteriori solution chooses a tour after learning the complete active
set, whereas an a~priori solution fixes an ordering of all vertices before any
activations are revealed and visits the active vertices in that order.  We
introduce an adaptive model between these extremes.  Vertices are probed
remotely one at a time; whenever a probe reveals an active vertex, the
salesperson must visit it immediately; and each next probe may depend on the
previous outcomes.

The \emph{adaptivity gap} is the worst-case ratio of the optimal a~priori
expected cost to the optimal adaptive expected cost, while the
\emph{clairvoyance gap} is the worst-case ratio of the optimal adaptive
expected cost to the optimal a~posteriori expected cost.  For symmetric travel
costs, we prove lower bounds of~$2$ on the adaptivity gap and~$10/9$ on the
clairvoyance gap.  For symmetric instances in which exactly one non-depot
vertex has activation probability in~$(0,1)$ and every other non-depot vertex
is always active, we prove that the clairvoyance gap is at
most~$(2+\sqrt6)/4$.  For asymmetric travel costs, we prove that the
adaptivity gap is $\Omega(n^{1/3}/\log n)$, where $n$ is the number of
non-depot vertices, and that the clairvoyance gap is at least~$3/2$.

For every asymmetric instance with $n$ non-depot vertices, we prove the
existence of an adaptive policy whose expected cost is within an
$O(n^{1/3})$ factor of the optimal a~posteriori cost.  Consequently, the
asymmetric clairvoyance gap is $O(n^{1/3})$.  Separately, for every fixed
$\varepsilon>0$, we give a deterministic algorithm that computes a
$(1+\varepsilon)$-approximate a~priori solution in time
$c_\varepsilon^n\operatorname{poly}(n)$, where $c_\varepsilon$ depends only
on~$\varepsilon$.

\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

Many real-world routing and scheduling problems are subject to uncertainty.
In a delivery service, for example, a courier must plan a route through several customer locations, yet some customers may have no packages to receive or their requests may be canceled.
A classical abstraction is the \emph{stochastic traveling salesperson problem} (\StochTSP{}), where each client~$v$ comes with a specified probability~$p_v$ and becomes \emph{active} (requires service) independently with probability~$p_v$.
The goal is to find a tour that visits all active vertices and minimizes the expected travel cost, where the cost comes from the underlying metric on vertices.

An important modeling choice is whether the salesperson is allowed to adapt future decisions based on information revealed during the execution of the tour.
In the \emph{nonadaptive} (a~priori) setting, the salesperson commits to a fixed tour before observing the realization \sharat{It's best to not say "the realization". Makes it sound like the set of active vertices are realized at the same time. It's an independent distribution so the wording should not refer to one grand realization but rather a set of active vertices.}; during execution the salesperson follows this tour while shortcutting inactive vertices.
In the \emph{adaptive} setting, decisions may depend on observations revealed along the way, allowing the tour to be adjusted on the fly.
In the \emph{a~posteriori} setting, the salesperson has full knowledge of the realization.

A fundamental question is how much adaptivity can improve performance relative to an optimal nonadaptive tour, and how much full clairvoyance improves over the best adaptive policy.
Note that computing an optimal adaptive policy (or an optimal a~priori tour) for \StochTSP{} is computationally hard, since the setting with $p_v=1$ for all~$v$ corresponds to the classical TSP, which is known to be APX-hard~\cite{Karpinski2015}.
Also, describing an optimal adaptive policy might require exponential space.
Thus, we are interested in understanding the separations between the three benchmarks, and in designing policies or algorithms whose expected cost can be provably bounded.

\section{Our contributions}\label{sec:contributions}

We obtain the following results.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Adaptivity gap lower bound for symmetric metrics} (\cref{ch:sym-adapt}).
    We first construct an explicit family of instances based on cycle-subdivided cliques
    for which $\optprior/\optadapt$ tends to~$5/4$
    (\cref{thm:cycle-subdivision}).  Motivated by the random activation of the
    semi-active vertices in this construction, we then transfer the randomness
    to the metric and use the probabilistic method to obtain deterministic
    symmetric instances whose adaptivity ratio tends to~$2$
    (\cref{thm:random-metric-gap}).  Since
    $\optpost\le\optadapt$, the same factor-two lower bound applies to the
    obliviousness gap.

    \item \textbf{Adaptivity gap lower bound for asymmetric metrics}
    (\cref{ch:asym-adapt}).
    We give a rectangular variant of the asymmetric grid construction of
    Christalla, Puhlmann, and Traub~\cite{Christalla2025}, with $k$ vertices
    in each of $k^2$ columns.  A packet argument lower-bounds every a~priori
    tour, while an adaptive column-sweep policy has cost controlled by the
    maximum active load of a column.  The resulting adaptivity gap is
    $\Omega(n^{1/3}/\log n)$
    (\cref{thm:asymmetric-adaptivity-lower}).  The same lower bound also
    applies to the obliviousness gap.

    \item \textbf{Clairvoyance gap for symmetric metrics} (\cref{ch:sym-clair}).
    We give a three-chain family with one semi-active vertex of
    activation probability~$1/2$ whose ratios converge to~$10/9$.  We also
    prove the upper bound
    \[
        \frac{\optadapt}{\optpost}\le\frac{2+p}{2+p^2}
    \]
    for every symmetric instance with one semi-active vertex of activation
    probability~$p$.  The right-hand side is maximized at
    $p=\sqrt6-2$, giving the uniform upper bound
    $(2+\sqrt6)/4$ for this class.

    \item \textbf{Clairvoyance gap lower bound for asymmetric metrics} (\cref{ch:asym-clair}).
    We give a layered-column construction with four routing vertices per
    stage for which
    \[
        \liminf\frac{\optadapt}{\optpost}\ge\frac32
    \]
    (\cref{thm:layered-column-gap}).  The construction uses independent
    activations and a strictly positive asymmetric metric.  Before the main
    construction, we record a natural recursive-triangle family whose ratio
    tends to~$4/3$ (\cref{rem:recursive-triangle}); its longer analysis is
    kept in a separate technical note.  We also give an explicit finite
    member whose ratio is strictly larger than~$4/3$.

    \item \textbf{Adaptive-policy upper bound for asymmetric metrics}
    (\cref{ch:circulation}).
    We prove the existence of an adaptive policy of expected cost
    $O(n^{1/3})\optpost$ for every asymmetric instance
    (\cref{thm:asymmetric-clairvoyance-upper}).  The proof starts from a
    circulation lower bound for the posterior optimum, assigns every
    outside-vertex detour to an ordered backbone chord, and jointly rounds the
    auxiliary backbone flow.  Every cyclic interval has imbalance
    $O(\log^2 n)$.  A maximal inequality controls the remaining Bernoulli
    imbalance, and a virtual active-chord-first walk yields the adaptive
    policy.  Splitting vertices at probability~$n^{-1/3}$ gives the stated
    bound and the asymmetric clairvoyance-gap corollary
    (\cref{cor:asymmetric-clairvoyance-upper}).

    \item \textbf{Approximate a~priori TSP} (\cref{ch:dp}).
    We give a $(1{+}\varepsilon)$-approximation algorithm for a~priori TSP on
    both symmetric and asymmetric metrics, running in time
    $c_\varepsilon^n\poly(n)$ (\cref{thm:apriori-ptas}).
    The algorithm discards pairs for which it is very unlikely that every
    vertex lying strictly between their endpoints is inactive, losing only a
    negligible amount, and then uses a dynamic program whose state stores just
    the relevant suffix of the partial order.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Classical TSP.}
The traveling salesperson problem is one of the most studied problems in combinatorial optimization.
For symmetric metrics, the classical $3/2$-approximation of Christofides~\cite{Christofides2022} stood for decades until Karlin, Klein, and Oveis~Gharan~\cite{Karlin2021} obtained a slightly improved factor.
For asymmetric metrics, Svensson, Tarnawski, and V\'egh~\cite{Svensson2020} gave the first constant-factor approximation.

\paragraph{A~priori TSP.}
The a~priori model was introduced by Jaillet~\cite{Jaillet1985}.
Shmoys and Talwar~\cite{Shmoys2008} proved that the obliviousness gap $\optprior/\optpost$ is at most~$3$ for symmetric metrics, yielding a constant-factor approximation via a master tour.
Blauth, Neuwohner, Puhlmann, and Vygen~\cite{Blauth2024} recently obtained improved approximation guarantees for a~priori TSP.
For asymmetric metrics, Christalla, Puhlmann, and
Traub~\cite{Christalla2025} showed that the obliviousness gap is
$O(\sqrt{n})$ and $\Omega(n^{1/4}/\log n)$.  Their square-grid construction
also implies the same lower bound for the adaptivity gap in our terminology.
The rectangular grid in \cref{ch:asym-adapt} strengthens both lower bounds to
$\Omega(n^{1/3}/\log n)$.

\paragraph{Activation models.}
Several foundational and recent approximation results for a~priori TSP use
independent vertex activations~\cite{Shmoys2008,Blauth2024,Christalla2025}.
Other formulations permit dependencies among vertex activations.  Van Ee,
van Iersel, Janssen, and
Sitters~\cite{vanEeIerselJanssenSitters2018} study a~priori TSP in the scenario
model, where the input explicitly lists active sets and their probabilities
and may therefore encode arbitrary dependencies.

\paragraph{Probing models.}
Gupta, Nagarajan, and Ravi~\cite{GuptaNagarajanRavi2017} study an adaptive TSP
model in which demand at a vertex is revealed only when the salesperson visits
it.  Ene, Nagarajan, and Saket~\cite{EneNagarajanSaket2017} and Jiang, Li, Liu,
and Singla~\cite{JiangLiLiuSingla2020} study stochastic $k$-TSP, where random
vertex rewards are likewise revealed only upon a visit; the route may adapt to
the observed rewards until a target total is reached.  Thus, in both models,
acquiring information requires physical travel.  By contrast, we are not aware
of prior stochastic-TSP work using our exact information structure: the
salesperson may remotely probe any unprobed vertex, remains in place after an
inactive outcome, and must immediately visit the vertex after an active
outcome.  Every vertex is eventually probed, and every active vertex must be
visited.

\section{Model and benchmarks}\label{sec:model}

This section fixes the notation used throughout the thesis.  We first define
the underlying metric space and its tours, then formalize the three stochastic
benchmarks, and finally introduce the ratios that compare their optimal costs.

\subsection{Metric spaces and tours}\label{sec:metric-spaces-tours}

Let $V$ be a finite vertex set of cardinality $n+1$, fix a distinguished
\emph{root} $r\in V$, and write
\[
    V^\circ:=V\setminus\{r\}
\]
for the $n$ non-root vertices.  A \emph{possibly asymmetric metric}%
\footnote{Distance functions satisfying the usual metric axioms except that
symmetry is not required are often called \emph{quasi-metrics}; terminology
varies across the literature.}
on~$V$ is a function
$d:V\times V\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}$ such that $d(x,x)=0$, $d(x,y)>0$ for
distinct $x,y\in V$, and
\[
    d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z)
    \qquad\forall x,y,z\in V.
\]
A metric~$d$ is \emph{symmetric} if $d(x,y)=d(y,x)$ for all $x,y\in V$,
and \emph{asymmetric} otherwise.  Unless explicitly stated otherwise, metrics
are allowed to be asymmetric.

A \emph{tour} on a subset $S\subseteq V$ containing the root is a cyclic
sequence $T=(r,u_1,\ldots,u_k,r)$, where $k=|S|-1$ and
$(u_1,\ldots,u_k)$ is an ordering of $S\setminus\{r\}$.  If $k=0$, the
root-only tour $T=(r)$ has cost~$0$.  If $k\ge1$, its \emph{cost} is
\[
    \cost(T)
    :=d(r,u_1)+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}d(u_i,u_{i+1})+d(u_k,r).
\]
For a subset $A\subseteq V^\circ$, we write $\opt(A)$ for the minimum cost
of a tour on $A\cup\{r\}$.

\subsection{Three benchmarks for \StochTSP{}}\label{sec:stoch-tsp-benchmarks}

In the stochastic setting, the root is always active, so $p_r=1$.  We assume
that $p_v\in(0,1]$ for every $v\in V^\circ$.  This is without loss of
generality: a vertex with activation probability~$0$ is never active and can
be deleted without changing any of the three benchmark costs.  The set of
active non-root vertices is a subset $A\subseteq V^\circ$ generated by
independent activations: each $v\in V^\circ$ is active (that is, $v\in A$)
independently with probability~$p_v$.  We denote by~$\mathcal Q$ the resulting
product distribution on~$2^{V^\circ}$, so
\[
    \mathcal Q(A)
    =\prod_{v\in A}p_v
     \prod_{v\in V^\circ\setminus A}(1-p_v).
\]

We consider three benchmarks for \StochTSP{}, distinguished by how much the solution may depend on the realization of the active set. In each case we define the cost of a feasible solution via an overloaded function $\cost(\cdot)$.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A posteriori TSP.}
        An \emph{a~posteriori solution} is a family
        $\mathcal{S}=(T_A)_{A\subseteq V^\circ}$, where $T_A$ is an optimal
        tour on $A\cup\{r\}$ and therefore has cost~$\opt(A)$.
        We define
        \[
        \optpost
        :=\cost(\mathcal{S})
        :=\E_{A\sim\mathcal Q}\bigl[\cost(T_A)\bigr]
        =\E_{A\sim\mathcal Q}\bigl[\opt(A)\bigr].
        \]

    \item \textbf{A priori (nonadaptive) TSP.}
        An \emph{a~priori tour} is a cyclic ordering of all vertices,
        represented as $T=(r,v_1,\ldots,v_n,r)$, where
        $V^\circ=\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}$.
        For a realization~$A$, the \emph{shortcut} $T\vert_A$ is obtained by retaining only the vertices in $A\cup\{r\}$ in the cyclic order prescribed by~$T$.
        If $A=\emptyset$, set $T\vert_A=(r)$.  Otherwise, if
        $1 \leq i_1 < i_2 < \cdots < i_{|A|} \leq n$ are such that
        $\{v_{i_k} : 1 \leq k \leq |A|\} = A$, then
        $T\vert_A = (r,v_{i_1},v_{i_2},\ldots,v_{i_{|A|}},r)$.
        We overload:
        \[
        \cost(T) := \E_{A\sim\mathcal Q}\bigl[\cost(T\vert_A)\bigr].
        \]
        The optimum is $\optprior := \min_{T}\,\cost(T)$, where the minimum is over all a~priori tours.

    \item \textbf{Adaptive TSP.}
        The salesperson knows all the activation probabilities but not the realization~$A$.
        During execution, the salesperson \emph{probes} non-root vertices one
        by one. A probe is performed remotely and reveals whether the probed
        vertex is active before the salesperson moves.  If the probed vertex~$v$ lies
        in~$A$, the salesperson \emph{must} travel to~$v$ immediately;
        otherwise the salesperson remains in place.  The order of probes may
        depend on the outcomes observed so far.

        Define the set of valid states by
        \[
            \mathcal D
            :=\{(u,S)\in V\times2^{V^\circ}:u\notin S\}.
        \]
        Formally, an adaptive policy is a function
        $\pi:\mathcal D\rightarrow V$, where
        $\pi(u,S)$ denotes the next vertex to probe when the salesperson is
        at~$u$ and the set of unprobed vertices is~$S$.
        We require $\pi(u,\emptyset)=r$, corresponding to the final return to
        the root, and $\pi(u,S)\in S$ whenever $S\ne\emptyset$.

        Restricting the policy to the state $(u,S)$ is without loss of
        generality.  Indeed, independence implies that, conditional on the
        probe outcomes observed so far, the vertices in~$S$ retain their original
        independent activation probabilities.  If $J(u,S)$ denotes the
        minimum expected remaining cost, then backward induction gives
        \[
            J(u,\emptyset)=d(u,r)
        \]
        and, for $S\neq\emptyset$,
        \[
            J(u,S)
            =
            \min_{v\in S}
            \left\{
                (1-p_v)J(u,S\setminus\{v\})
                +p_v\bigl(d(u,v)+J(v,S\setminus\{v\})\bigr)
            \right\}.
        \]
        Thus a deterministic Markov policy attains the optimum; allowing
        dependence on the full history or private randomization does not
        decrease the optimal expected cost.

        Given a realization~$A$, the policy induces a realized tour $T(A;\pi)$ on $A\cup\{r\}$ as follows.
        Initialize $u \leftarrow r$, $S \leftarrow V^\circ$.
        While $S \neq \emptyset$: compute $v = \pi(u,S)$ and probe~$v$; if $v \in A$ then travel to~$v$ and set $u\leftarrow v$; set $S \leftarrow S \setminus \{v\}$.
        Finally, return to~$r$.
        We overload:
        \[
        \cost(\pi) := \E_{A\sim\mathcal Q}\bigl[\cost(T(A;\pi))\bigr].
        \]
        The optimum is $\optadapt := \min_{\pi}\,\cost(\pi)$, where the minimum is over all adaptive policies.
\end{enumerate}

Throughout the thesis, we reserve $\pi$ for adaptive policies and use $T$ for
tours, including fixed a~priori orders.  We use $p$ (or $p_v$) for activation
probabilities and $q$ (or $q_v$) for the corresponding inactivity
probabilities.

\subsection{Gap measures}\label{sec:gaps}

Among the three benchmarks, a~posteriori TSP has the most information (the full realization~$A$), a~priori TSP has the least (only the metric and probabilities), and adaptive TSP lies in between.

We define an \emph{instance} of \StochTSP{} by
\[
    \mathcal{I}:=(V,r,d,(p_v)_{v\in V}),
    \qquad
    p_r=1,
    \qquad
    p_v\in(0,1]\quad\forall v\in V^\circ.
\]
For any instance~$\mathcal{I}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optineq}
\optpost \le \optadapt \le \optprior
\end{equation}
since from left to right the solution has less information about the realization, resulting in higher optimal cost.

We define the following gap measures:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Adaptivity gap}: $\sup_{\mathcal{I}} \f{\optprior}{\optadapt}$ --- the worst-case benefit of adaptivity over a fixed a~priori tour.
    \item \emph{Clairvoyance gap}: $\sup_{\mathcal{I}} \f{\optadapt}{\optpost}$ --- the benefit of full clairvoyance (knowing $A$ upfront) over the best adaptive policy.
    \item \emph{Obliviousness gap}: $\sup_{\mathcal{I}} \f{\optprior}{\optpost}$ --- combines both effects.
\end{itemize}
Whenever one of these ratios has both numerator and denominator equal to zero,
we define its value to be~$0$.  Under the strict positivity assumption on the
metric, a zero denominator in any of the three ratios forces the corresponding
numerator to be zero as well.
When a bound depends on the number of non-root vertices, we use the
size-restricted gaps
\[
    \operatorname{AG}(n)
    \coloneqq
    \sup_{\mathcal I:\,|V^\circ|=n}
    \frac{\optprior(\mathcal I)}{\optadapt(\mathcal I)},
    \qquad
    \operatorname{CG}(n)
    \coloneqq
    \sup_{\mathcal I:\,|V^\circ|=n}
    \frac{\optadapt(\mathcal I)}{\optpost(\mathcal I)},
\]
and
\[
    \operatorname{OG}(n)
    \coloneqq
    \sup_{\mathcal I:\,|V^\circ|=n}
    \frac{\optprior(\mathcal I)}{\optpost(\mathcal I)}.
\]
The unrestricted gaps above are the suprema of these quantities over~$n$.
We remark that the literature on a~priori TSP uses the term adaptivity gap for what we call the obliviousness gap; we deviate from this convention following \cite{Christalla2025} for the sake of clarity.

For symmetric metrics, the obliviousness gap is known to be at most $O(1)$; in particular, $\optprior/\optpost \le 3$ is shown in~\cite{Shmoys2008}.
The same upper bound carries over to the clairvoyance gap and the adaptivity gap by~\cref{eq:optineq}.
For asymmetric metrics, Christalla, Puhlmann, and Traub~\cite{Christalla2025}
show that the obliviousness gap is $O(\sqrt{n})$.  The rectangular grid
construction in \cref{ch:asym-adapt} gives lower bounds of
$\Omega(n^{1/3}/\log n)$ on both the adaptivity and obliviousness gaps.

\chapter{Preliminaries}\label{ch:preliminaries}

\section{Probabilistic tools}\label{sec:probabilistic-inequalities}

This section collects the probabilistic tools used throughout the thesis.

\subsection{Elementary bounds}

For events $E_1,\ldots,E_m$, the union bound gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:union-bound}
    \P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^m E_i\right)
    \le\sum_{i=1}^m\P(E_i).
\end{equation}
For every nonnegative random variable~$Z$ and every $t>0$, Markov's
inequality gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:markov}
    \P(Z\ge t)\le\frac{\E[Z]}{t}.
\end{equation}
If $N$ is a nonnegative integer-valued random variable, its expectation can
be recovered from its upper tails:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tail-sum}
    \E[N]=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\P(N\ge t).
\end{equation}

We repeatedly use the following two bounds for products of inactivity
probabilities.  For $x_1,\ldots,x_m\in[0,1]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bernoulli-product-bounds}
    1-\sum_{i=1}^m x_i
    \le\prod_{i=1}^m(1-x_i)
    \le\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^m x_i\right).
\end{equation}
The left inequality follows by induction, or equivalently from the union
bound, and the right inequality follows from $1-x\le\e^{-x}$.

We also use the standard monotone coupling of product distributions.
\begin{lemma}[Monotone product coupling]\label{lem:monotone-product-coupling}
Let $A$ and $B$ be random subsets of a finite ground set~$U$, with independent
inclusion probabilities $(\alpha_v)_{v\in U}$ and $(\beta_v)_{v\in U}$,
respectively.  If $\alpha_v\le\beta_v$ for every~$v$, then they can be coupled
so that $A\subseteq B$ almost surely.  Consequently, every nondecreasing
function $F:2^U\to\mathbb R$ satisfies
\[
    \E[F(A)]\le\E[F(B)]
\]
whenever the expectations exist.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose independent uniform random variables $U_v$ on~$[0,1]$ and put
$A:=\{v:U_v\le\alpha_v\}$ and $B:=\{v:U_v\le\beta_v\}$.
\end{proof}

Finally, we use the averaging principle without further mention: a
real-valued random variable attains a value no larger than its expectation
on some outcome, and every positive-probability event contains at least one
outcome.

\subsection{Concentration for independent sums}

\begin{lemma}[Chernoff--Bernstein bounds]
\label{lem:chernoff-bernstein}
Let $X_1,\ldots,X_m$ be independent Bernoulli random variables, put
$X:=\sum_iX_i$, and write $\mu:=\E[X]$.  If $\mu>0$, then
\begin{align}
    \P(X\ge\mu+s)
    &\le\exp\left(-\frac{s^2}{2(\mu+s/3)}\right)
    &&\text{for }s\ge0,
    \label{eq:chernoff-upper}\\
    \P(X\le\mu-s)
    &\le\exp\left(-\frac{s^2}{2\mu}\right)
    &&\text{for }0\le s\le\mu.
    \label{eq:chernoff-lower}
\end{align}
In all cases, for every $t\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chernoff-useful}
    \P(X>2\mu+t)\le\e^{-t/3},
    \qquad
    \P(X<\mu/2)\le\e^{-\mu/8}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

If $\mu=0$, then $X=0$ almost surely and the two consequences in
\cref{eq:chernoff-useful} are immediate.  If $\mu>0$, the first consequence
follows from \cref{eq:chernoff-upper} with $s=\mu+t$, using
\[
    \frac{(\mu+t)^2}{2(\mu+(\mu+t)/3)}\ge\frac t3,
\]
and the second follows from \cref{eq:chernoff-lower} with $s=\mu/2$.

\begin{lemma}[Weighted Hoeffding inequality]
\label{lem:weighted-hoeffding}
Let $Y_1,\ldots,Y_m$ be independent random variables with
$Y_i\in[a_i,b_i]$ almost surely.  Then, for every $t>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-hoeffding}
    \P\left[\sum_{i=1}^m(Y_i-\E[Y_i])\ge t\right]
    \le
    \exp\left(-\frac{2t^2}{\sum_{i=1}^m(b_i-a_i)^2}\right).
\end{equation}
Here the right-hand side is interpreted as~$0$ when
$\sum_i(b_i-a_i)^2=0$; in that case the centered sum is~$0$ almost surely.
In particular, if the $X_i$ are independent Bernoulli random variables and
$c_i\ge0$, then~\cref{eq:weighted-hoeffding} applies to
$Y_i=c_iX_i\in[0,c_i]$.
\end{lemma}

\subsection{Maximal inequalities}

\begin{lemma}[Maximal inequality for independent sums]
\label{lem:maximal-independent-sums}
Let $Z_1,\ldots,Z_m$ be independent centered random variables with finite
second moments, and put $S_k:=\sum_{i=1}^kZ_i$.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l2-maximal}
    \E\left[\max_{0\le k\le m}|S_k|\right]
    \le
    \left(\E\left[\max_{0\le k\le m}|S_k|^2\right]\right)^{1/2}
    \le2\left(\sum_{i=1}^m\E[Z_i^2]\right)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The partial sums $(S_k)_{k=0}^m$ form an $L^2$ martingale.  The first
inequality follows from Cauchy--Schwarz, and the second is Doob's $L^2$
maximal inequality together with
$\E[S_m^2]=\sum_{i=1}^m\E[Z_i^2]$.
\end{proof}

\chapter{Adaptivity Gap for Symmetric \StochTSP{}}\label{ch:sym-adapt}

In this chapter we focus on symmetric \StochTSP{}.
The main result of this chapter is that the adaptivity gap is at least~$2$.
We give two qualitatively different constructions.
In \Cref{sec:cycle-subdivided-construction}, we establish the
weaker lower bound of~$5/4$ through an explicit family of instances
whose metrics arise from cycle-subdivided cliques.  In
\Cref{sec:random-metric-gap}, we strengthen the lower bound to~$2$ using
the probabilistic method on $(1,2)$-TSP metrics induced by a random graph~$G_{n,p}$
in the Erdős--Rényi model.

\section{The cycle-subdivided clique construction}
\label{sec:cycle-subdivided-construction}

This section gives an explicit family of instances for which the ratio between
the a~priori and adaptive optima tends to~$5/4$.
The construction begins with a complete graph, selects a
Hamiltonian cycle, and inserts one vertex of activation probability~$1/2$ on
each edge of that cycle.

\begin{definition}[Cycle-subdivided clique]\label{def:cycle-sub-graph}
Fix $n\ge 7$, and let $b_1,\dots,b_n$ be the vertices of the complete graph
$K_n$.  Throughout this construction, vertex indices are interpreted
modulo~$n$ with representatives in~$[n]$; in particular, $n+1$ is identified
with~$1$.  We call
$C_n=(b_1,\dots,b_n,b_1)$ the \emph{base cycle}.  For every $i\in[n]$,
subdivide the edge $b_ib_{i+1}$ of the base cycle by introducing a new
vertex~$s_i$.  Denote the resulting unweighted graph
by~$\Sub(K_n;C_n)$.
\end{definition}
The instance $\mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{CSC}}$ based on the cycle-subdivided clique
has root $r:=b_1$ and vertex set
\[
V_n:=\{b_1,\dots,b_n\}\cup\{s_1,\dots,s_n\}.
\]
The underlying distance function is the shortest-path metric induced
by~$\Sub(K_n;C_n)$.  The vertices $b_1,\dots,b_n$ are the
\emph{always-active} vertices: the root~$b_1$ is always active by definition,
and every non-root vertex $b_2,\dots,b_n$ is active with probability~$1$.
The vertices $s_1,\dots,s_n$ are the \emph{semi-active} vertices, each active
independently with probability~$1/2$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:cycle-subdivision}
The adaptivity gap is at least $\frac54$.
\end{theorem}

We compute the exact a~priori optimum by decomposing every tour into
segments between always-active vertices, and then analyze an adaptive
policy that changes its sweep direction after inactive probes.  Together,
these two estimates yield the following lower bound.

\Cref{thm:cycle-subdivision} follows from the next two lemmas.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:cycle-prior}
For every $n\ge 7$, the instance $\mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{CSC}}$ satisfies
$\optprior=\frac{15}{8}n$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:cycle-adapt}
For every $n\ge 7$, the instance $\mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{CSC}}$ satisfies
$\frac32n\le\optadapt\le \frac32n+5$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:cycle-subdivision}]
By \cref{lem:cycle-prior,lem:cycle-adapt},
\[
    \optprior=\frac{15}{8}n,
    \qquad
    \frac32n\le\optadapt\le\frac32n+5.
\]
Therefore
\[
    \frac{15n/8}{3n/2+5}
    \le
    \frac{\optprior}{\optadapt}
    \le
    \frac{15n/8}{3n/2}
    =\frac54.
\]
The lower bound also converges to~$5/4$, so the ratio converges to~$5/4$ as
$n\to\infty$.
\end{proof}


\subsection{The a priori optimum}
\label{sec:cycle-prior}
We prove \cref{lem:cycle-prior} in two steps.  First, we bound the
expected contribution of every segment of an arbitrary a~priori tour between
two always-active vertices.  Summing these local bounds gives a global lower
bound.  We then construct tours attaining it.

We begin by recording the relevant distances in the metric completion.  For
always-active vertices,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cycle-base-distances}
    d(b_i,b_{i+1})=2,
    \qquad
    d(b_i,b_j)=1
    \quad\text{if }j\not\equiv i\pm1\pmod n.
\end{equation}
For a semi-active vertex and an always-active vertex,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cycle-mixed-distances}
    d(s_i,b_i)=d(s_i,b_{i+1})=1,
    \qquad
    d(s_i,b_j)=2
    \quad\text{if }b_j\notin\{b_i,b_{i+1}\}.
\end{equation}
Finally, for distinct semi-active vertices,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cycle-subdivision-distances}
    d(s_i,s_j)
    =
    \begin{cases}
        2, & \text{if the base-cycle edges }b_ib_{i+1}
             \text{ and }b_jb_{j+1}\text{ are adjacent},\\
        3, & \text{otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Given always-active vertices $u,v$ and an ordered sequence
$W=(w_1,\dots,w_k)$ of distinct semi-active vertices, let
$\Phi(u;W;v)$ be the expected length of the realized path obtained from
\[
    (u,w_1,\dots,w_k,v)
\]
after deleting the semi-active vertices that are inactive.

\begin{lemma}[Segment lower bound]\label{lem:cycle-segment-bound}
Every such segment satisfies
\[
    \Phi(u;W;v)\ge 1+\frac{7}{8}|W|.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We first treat the cases $k\le3$.

\begin{claim}\label{clm:short-seg}
For every segment $(u;W;v)$ with $k=|W|\le3$,
\[
    \Phi(u;W;v)
    \ge
    \begin{cases}
        1, & k=0,\\
        2, & k=1,\\
        \frac{11}{4}, & k=2,\\
        \frac{29}{8}, & k=3.
    \end{cases}
\]
Moreover, equality is attained for $k=2$ by
$(b_i,s_i,s_{i+1},b_{i+1})$, and for $k=3$ by
$(b_{i+1},s_{i+1},s_i,s_{i-1},b_i)$.
\end{claim}
\begin{proof}
If $k=0$, then the segment contributes the single edge $uv$, so
$\Phi(u;\varnothing;v)=d(u,v)\ge1$.

If $k=1$, say $W=(w)$, then
\[
    \Phi(u;w;v)
    =
    \tfrac12d(u,v)
    +\tfrac12\bigl(d(u,w)+d(w,v)\bigr).
\]
If $u$ and $v$ are consecutive on the base cycle, then $d(u,v)=2$
and also $d(u,w)+d(w,v)\ge2$.  If they are not consecutive, then
$d(u,v)=1$, and no semi-active vertex is incident with both $u$ and $v$,
so $d(u,w)+d(w,v)\ge3$.  In either case
$\Phi(u;w;v)\ge2$.

Now let $k=2$, say $W=(w_1,w_2)$.  There are four realizations.  If
$d(u,v)=1$, then neither $w_1$ nor $w_2$ is incident with both $u$ and
$v$, so each singleton realization has cost at least~$3$, while the
realization with both active has cost at least~$4$.  Hence
\[
    \Phi(u;W;v)
    \ge\frac{1+3+3+4}{4}
    =\frac{11}{4}.
\]
If $d(u,v)=2$, then exactly one semi-active vertex is incident with both
$u$ and~$v$, so at most one singleton realization has cost~$2$; the two
singleton costs therefore sum to at least~$5$, and the realization with
both active has cost at least~$4$.  Thus
\[
    \Phi(u;W;v)
    \ge\frac{2+5+4}{4}
    =\frac{11}{4}.
\]
Equality is attained, for example, by
$(b_i,s_i,s_{i+1},b_{i+1})$, whose four realization costs are
$2,2,3,4$.

Finally, let $k=3$, say $W=(w_1,w_2,w_3)$.  Suppose first that
$d(u,v)=1$.  Then each singleton realization has cost at least~$3$.
A two-vertex realization can have cost~$4$ only if one of its active vertices
is incident with~$u$, the other is incident with~$v$, and the corresponding
base-cycle edges are adjacent.  For fixed nonconsecutive $u$ and $v$, there
is at most one unordered pair of semi-active vertices with this property.
Hence among the three two-vertex realizations, at most one has cost~$4$,
and the other two have cost at least~$5$.  The realization with all three
active has cost at least~$6$.  Therefore
\[
    \Phi(u;W;v)
    \ge\frac{1+9+14+6}{8}
    =\frac{15}{4}
    >\frac{29}{8}.
\]

Now suppose that $d(u,v)=2$.  Then exactly one semi-active vertex is
incident with both $u$ and~$v$, so among the three singleton realizations
at most one has cost~$2$.  Hence the sum of the singleton costs is at least
$2+3+3=8$.  A two-vertex realization can have cost~$4$ only if one of its
active vertices is the unique semi-active vertex on the edge~$uv$, and the
other comes from one of the two base-cycle edges adjacent to~$uv$.  Thus among the three
two-vertex realizations, at most two have cost~$4$, and the remaining one
has cost at least~$5$; the sum of these three costs is therefore at
least~$13$.  The realization with all three active has cost at least~$6$.
Consequently,
\[
    \Phi(u;W;v)
    \ge\frac{2+8+13+6}{8}
    =\frac{29}{8}.
\]
Equality is attained, for example, by
$(b_{i+1},s_{i+1},s_i,s_{i-1},b_i)$, whose eight realization costs are
\[
    2,\ 3,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 5,\ 4,\ 6.
\]
\end{proof}

It remains to consider $k\ge4$.  Set
\[
    z_0:=u,\qquad
    z_t:=w_t\ (1\le t\le k),\qquad
    z_{k+1}:=v.
\]
Two retained vertices $z_i,z_j$ become consecutive after shortcutting
precisely when every vertex strictly between them is deleted.  Since each
$w_t$ is retained independently with probability~$1/2$, we obtain
\begin{align*}
    \Phi(u;W;v)
    &=
    2^{-k}d(u,v)
    +\sum_{t=1}^k2^{-t}d(u,w_t)
    +\sum_{t=1}^k2^{-(k-t+1)}d(w_t,v)\\
    &\qquad
    +\sum_{1\le i<j\le k}2^{-(j-i+1)}d(w_i,w_j).
\end{align*}
Introduce the indicators
\[
    \alpha_t:=\1_{\{w_t\text{ is incident with }u\}},
    \qquad
    \beta_t:=\1_{\{w_t\text{ is incident with }v\}},
\]
and, for $1\le i<j\le k$,
\[
    \gamma_{ij}
    :=
    \1_{\{w_i,w_j\text{ correspond to adjacent base-cycle edges}\}}.
\]
By~\crefrange{eq:cycle-base-distances}{eq:cycle-subdivision-distances},
\begin{align*}
    d(u,v)
    &=1+\1_{\{u,v\text{ are consecutive on the base cycle}\}},\\
    d(u,w_t)&=2-\alpha_t,\\
    d(w_t,v)&=2-\beta_t,\\
    d(w_i,w_j)&=3-\gamma_{ij}.
\end{align*}
Substituting these identities gives
\[
    \Phi(u;W;v)
    =
    1+\frac{3k}{2}
    +2^{-k}\1_{\{u,v\text{ consecutive}\}}
    -D,
\]
where
\[
    D
    :=
    \sum_{t=1}^k2^{-t}\alpha_t
    +\sum_{t=1}^k2^{-(k-t+1)}\beta_t
    +\sum_{1\le i<j\le k}2^{-(j-i+1)}\gamma_{ij}.
\]
Since an always-active vertex is incident with exactly two semi-active vertices,
\[
    \sum_{t=1}^k2^{-t}\alpha_t
    \le\frac34,
    \qquad
    \sum_{t=1}^k2^{-(k-t+1)}\beta_t
    \le\frac34.
\]
For the last sum, consider the graph on
$\{w_1,\dots,w_k\}$ in which two vertices are adjacent when the
corresponding semi-active vertices arise from adjacent edges of the base
cycle.  This graph is a subgraph of the base cycle, hence has maximum degree at
most~$2$ and at most $k$ edges.  Every edge contributes weight at
most~$1/4$, so
\[
    \sum_{1\le i<j\le k}
    2^{-(j-i+1)}\gamma_{ij}
    \le\frac{k}{4}.
\]
Therefore
\[
    D\le\frac32+\frac{k}{4},
\]
and hence
\[
    \Phi(u;W;v)
    \ge
    1+\frac{3k}{2}
    -\left(\frac32+\frac{k}{4}\right)
    =
    \frac54k-\frac12.
\]
Since $k\ge4$,
\[
    \frac54k-\frac12
    \ge1+\frac{7k}{8}.
\]
Together with \cref{clm:short-seg}, this proves the lemma.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{lem:cycle-prior}]
Fix an arbitrary a~priori tour
\[
    T=(b_1,x_1,x_2,\dots,x_{2n-1},b_1)
\]
on~$V_n$, where $x_1,\dots,x_{2n-1}$ is a permutation of
$\{b_2,\dots,b_n,s_1,\dots,s_n\}$.  Deleting all semi-active vertices
from~$T$ leaves a cyclic ordering
\[
    (b_1=u_1,u_2,\dots,u_n,b_1)
\]
of the always-active vertices.  For each $j\in[n]$, let
\[
    W_j=(w_{j,1},\dots,w_{j,k_j})
\]
be the ordered list, possibly empty, of semi-active vertices appearing
between $u_j$ and $u_{j+1}$, where $u_{n+1}:=b_1$.  Every semi-active
vertex belongs to exactly one such segment, so
\[
    \sum_{j=1}^n k_j=n.
\]
Because all always-active vertices are present in every realization, the realized
tour decomposes into these segment contributions.  Taking expectation over
$A\sim\mathcal Q$ and applying
\cref{lem:cycle-segment-bound}, we obtain
\begin{align*}
    \cost(T)
    &=\E_{A\sim\mathcal Q}[\cost(T|_A)]\\
    &=
    \sum_{j=1}^n\Phi(u_j;W_j;u_{j+1})\\
    &\ge
    \sum_{j=1}^n\left(1+\frac{7}{8}|W_j|\right)\\
    &=n+\frac78\sum_{j=1}^nk_j
    =
    \frac{15}{8}n.
\end{align*}
Since $T$ was arbitrary,
\[
    \optprior\ge\frac{15}{8}n.
\]

For the reverse inequality, we construct a tour attaining this bound.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Even \(n\).}
Write $n=2m$ and consider
\begin{align*}
T^{\mathrm{even}}
:=(&b_1,s_1,s_2,b_2,
   b_4,s_4,s_3,b_3,
   b_6,s_6,s_5,b_5,
   \dots,\\
  &b_{2m},s_{2m},s_{2m-1},b_{2m-1},b_1).
\end{align*}
This tour has $m$ nonempty segments, namely
\[
    (b_1,s_1,s_2,b_2)
    \quad\text{and}\quad
    (b_{2t},s_{2t},s_{2t-1},b_{2t-1})
    \qquad(2\le t\le m),
\]
each with expected contribution $11/4$, together with $m$ direct edges
between always-active vertices, each of length~$1$.  Hence
\begin{align*}
    \cost(T^{\mathrm{even}})
    &=\E_{A\sim\mathcal Q}[\cost(T^{\mathrm{even}}|_A)]\\
    &=
    m\left(\frac{11}{4}+1\right)
    =
    \frac{15}{8}n.
\end{align*}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Odd \(n\).}
Write $n=2m+1$ with $m\ge2$ and consider
\begin{align*}
T^{\mathrm{odd}}
:=(&b_n,b_2,s_2,s_1,s_n,b_1,
   b_4,s_4,s_3,b_3,
   b_6,s_6,s_5,b_5,
   \dots,\\
  &b_{2m},s_{2m},s_{2m-1},b_{2m-1},b_n).
\end{align*}
This tour has one segment
\[
    (b_2,s_2,s_1,s_n,b_1)
\]
with expected contribution $29/8$, together with $m-1$ segments
\[
    (b_{2t},s_{2t},s_{2t-1},b_{2t-1})
    \qquad(2\le t\le m),
\]
each contributing $11/4$, and $m+1$ direct edges between always-active vertices,
each of length~$1$.  Therefore
\begin{align*}
    \cost(T^{\mathrm{odd}})
    &=\E_{A\sim\mathcal Q}[\cost(T^{\mathrm{odd}}|_A)]\\
    &=
    \frac{29}{8}
    +(m-1)\frac{11}{4}
    +(m+1)\\
    &=
    \frac{15}{8}(2m+1)
    =
    \frac{15}{8}n.
\end{align*}

In both parity cases we have constructed an a~priori tour of expected cost
$15n/8$.  Together with the lower bound, this proves
\[
    \optprior=\frac{15}{8}n. \qedhere 
\]
\end{proof}

\subsection{An adaptive policy}
\label{sec:cycle-adaptive-policy}

We now prove \cref{lem:cycle-adapt} by first giving a universal lower bound and
then analyzing an adaptive policy with asymptotically matching cost.

\paragraph{The alternating-sweep policy.}
Let
\[
P \;:=\; (b_2,\,s_2,\,b_3,\,\dots,\,s_{n-1},\,b_n)
\]
be viewed as an ordered path.  The policy first probes $s_1$, then $s_n$, and
then the always-active vertex~$b_2$.  Its current location is now~$b_2$, and it
begins sweeping along~$P$ toward~$b_n$.

At every subsequent step, the unprobed vertices of~$P$ form a contiguous
subpath, and the current location is a probed always-active vertex adjacent to
one end of that subpath.  The policy probes the semi-active vertex at this end.
If it is active, the policy visits it, probes the next always-active vertex in
the current direction if one remains, and continues the sweep.  If it is
inactive, the current location is unchanged; the policy probes the
always-active vertex at the opposite end of the remaining subpath and reverses
the sweep direction.  When no unprobed vertex remains, the policy returns to
the root.  This rule preserves the stated invariant and therefore defines the
policy until every non-root vertex has been probed.

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{lem:cycle-adapt}]
Every realization contains the $n$ always-active vertices.  If $Y$ denotes the
number of active semi-active vertices, then every realized tour visits $n+Y$
vertices and therefore uses $n+Y$ metric edges.  Each such edge has length at
least~$1$, so every adaptive policy has expected cost at least
\[
    \E[n+Y]=n+\frac n2=\frac32n.
\]

For the upper bound, fix an activation set~$A$.  The movement
generated by the preliminary probes is a shortcut of the walk
$(r,s_1,s_n,b_2)$ and therefore costs at most
\[
    d(r,s_1)+d(s_1,s_n)+d(s_n,b_2)
    =1+2+2=5.
\]

Let
\[
    X:=\bigl|\{i\in\{2,\dots,n-1\}:s_i\text{ is active}\}\bigr|.
\]
Each of the $X$ semi-active vertices on~$P$ that are active causes at most two
unit-length moves: one to that vertex and one to the next always-active
vertex.  A semi-active vertex that is inactive can cause one switch to the
always-active vertex at the opposite end of the remaining subpath.  Every
such switch costs~$1$, except possibly the last, which may cost~$2$ when its endpoints
are consecutive on the base cycle.  Hence the total switch cost is at most
$(n-2)-X+1$.  Finally, the return to~$r$ costs at most~$2$.  Therefore, for
every realization,
\[
    \cost(T(A;\pi))
    \le
    5+2X+\bigl((n-2)-X+1\bigr)+2
    =n+X+6.
\]
Since $\E[X]=(n-2)/2$, it follows that
\[
    \cost(\pi)=\E_{A\sim\mathcal Q}[\cost(T(A;\pi))]
    \le \frac32n+5.
\]
Together with the preceding lower bound, this proves the lemma.
\end{proof}

\section{Randomizing the metric}
\label{sec:random-metric-gap}

Unlike the preceding explicit construction, we now randomize the metric.  An
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graph determines which vertex pairs have distance~$1$;
nonedges have distance~$2$.  Up to an additive $O(1)$ term, which is
asymptotically negligible because the expected number of active vertices tends
to infinity, every realized tour has cost between~$|A|$ and~$2|A|$.  Thus
factor~$2$ is the largest separation possible within this family.  We show
that, with positive probability over the sampled metric, every a~priori tour
has expected cost $(2-o(1))np$, whereas one adaptive policy has expected cost
$(1+o(1))np$.

The random graph is only a probabilistic-method device.  At the end of the
argument we fix a deterministic realization, which is then known completely
to both benchmarks.  The randomness used to prove existence is distinct from
the activation randomness faced by a policy on the resulting instance.

\begin{theorem}[Probabilistic factor-two construction]
\label{thm:random-metric-gap}
There exists a sequence $(\mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{RM}})_n$ of deterministic
symmetric \StochTSP{} instances
for which
\[
    \frac{\optprior(\mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{RM}})}
         {\optadapt(\mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{RM}})}
    \longrightarrow 2.
\]
In particular, the adaptivity gap for symmetric \StochTSP{} is at
least~$2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:random-metric-oblivious}
The obliviousness gap for symmetric \StochTSP{} is at least~$2$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For every instance, $\optpost\le\optadapt$.  Hence
\[
    \frac{\optprior}{\optpost}
    \ge
    \frac{\optprior}{\optadapt},
\]
and the claim follows from \cref{thm:random-metric-gap}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The random metric}\label{sec:random-metric-model}

We now specify the random family.  All logs in this section are to
base~$\mathrm{e}$.  Fix a sufficiently large integer~$n$, label the non-root
vertices by $[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}$, let $V:=\{r\}\cup[n]$, and set
\[
    p:=\frac{1}{(\log n)^4},
    \qquad
    \theta:=\frac{1}{\log n},
    \qquad
    q:=1-p.
\]
Every non-root vertex is active independently with probability~$p$.

Independently for every unordered pair
$e=\{x,y\}\subseteq V$, sample a Bernoulli random variable
$X_e$ with mean~$\theta$, and define
\[
    D(x,y):=2-X_{\{x,y\}}
    \quad (x\neq y),
    \qquad
    D(x,x):=0.
\]
Thus a pair has distance~$1$ with probability~$\theta$ and distance~$2$
otherwise.  Let $H(D)$ be the graph whose edge set is
$\{e:X_e=1\}$.  Then $H(D)\sim G_{n+1,\theta}$, and for $k\ge1$ we use
$G_{k,\theta}$ for the same distribution on any fixed set of $k$ vertices.
We call the edges of~$H(D)$ \emph{short}.  Every realization of~$D$ is a
symmetric $(1,2)$-TSP metric.
For every deterministic realization~$d$ of~$D$, let
$\mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{RM}}(d)$ denote the resulting random-metric
\StochTSP{} instance on~$V$ with root~$r$: its metric is~$d$, and its
non-root vertices are activated independently with probability~$p$.

Let $A$ be the random set of active vertices, and let $K:=|A|$.  Then
$K\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p)$.
Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:random-metric-baseline}
    W
    :=
    \E\bigl[(K+1)\1_{\{K\ge1\}}\bigr]
    =
    np+1-q^n.
\end{equation}
Since $np=n/(\log n)^4\to\infty$ and $0\le1-q^n\le1$,
\[
    W=np+O(1)=(1+o(1))np.
\]
For a nonempty realization with $K=k$, every feasible tour has exactly
$k+1$ edges.  Since all distances are at least~$1$, $W$ is a
lower bound on the expected cost of every benchmark.  Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:random-metric-baseline-lower-bound}
    W\ge np=\frac{n}{(\log n)^4}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{A uniform lower bound for a priori tours}
\label{sec:random-metric-prior-bound}

We next show that, with high probability over the metric, every a~priori tour
has nearly the same large expected cost.  For a fixed order, its expected cost
over the activations is a weighted sum of the independent random distances.
The weights are the probabilities that the corresponding pairs become
consecutive in the shortcut.  We identify these weights, bound their squared
sum, and then apply Hoeffding's inequality.  The random graph is invariant
under relabeling the non-root vertices, so a sufficiently strong tail bound for
the canonical order $r,1,\ldots,n,r$ applies to every order by symmetry.  A
union bound over the at most $n!$ orders then gives a simultaneous lower bound.

By the definition of a~priori cost in~\cref{sec:stoch-tsp-benchmarks}, for a
deterministic realization~$d$ of~$D$ and an a~priori tour
\[
    T=(r,v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n,r)
\]
we write
\[
    C_d(T):=\E_{A\sim\mathcal Q}[\cost_d(T|_A)]=\cost_d(T).
\]

Recall that $q=1-p$.

\begin{lemma}[Expected-cost expansion]\label{lem:random-metric-expansion}
For $i\in[n]$, define
\[
    \alpha_i:=p\bigl(q^{i-1}+q^{n-i}\bigr),
\]
and, for $1\le i<j\le n$, define
\[
    \beta_{ij}:=p^2q^{j-i-1}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:random-metric-expansion}
    C_d(T)
    =
    \sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i d(r,v_i)
    +
    \sum_{1\le i<j\le n}\beta_{ij}d(v_i,v_j).
\end{equation}
The coefficients in~\cref{eq:random-metric-expansion} sum to~$W$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The edge from $r$ to $v_i$ is used when $v_i$ is active and
$v_1,\ldots,v_{i-1}$ are inactive, which occurs with probability $pq^{i-1}$.
Similarly, the edge from $v_i$ to~$r$ is used with probability
$pq^{n-i}$.  For $i<j$, the vertices $v_i$ and $v_j$ are consecutive
active vertices in the shortcut precisely when both are active and all vertices
strictly between them are inactive.  This event has probability
$p^2q^{j-i-1}$, proving~\cref{eq:random-metric-expansion}.

If every distance in the expansion is replaced by~$1$, the right-hand side
is the expected number of edges in the shortcut tour.  This number is
$(K+1)\1_{\{K\ge1\}}$, whose expectation is~$W$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Coefficient-square bound]
\label{lem:random-metric-coefficients}
For every a~priori order and all sufficiently large~$n$,
\[
    \sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i^2
    +
    \sum_{1\le i<j\le n}\beta_{ij}^2
    \le 2np^3.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Using $(a+b)^2\le2a^2+2b^2$,
\begin{align*}
    \sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i^2
    &\le
    2p^2\sum_{i=1}^n
        \bigl(q^{2(i-1)}+q^{2(n-i)}\bigr)\\
    &\le
    \frac{4p^2}{1-q^2}
    =
    \frac{4p}{2-p}
    \le4p.
\end{align*}
Also,
\begin{align*}
    \sum_{1\le i<j\le n}\beta_{ij}^2
    &=
    p^4\sum_{g=0}^{n-2}(n-g-1)q^{2g}\\
    &\le
    \frac{np^4}{1-q^2}
    =
    \frac{np^3}{2-p}
    \le np^3.
\end{align*}
Since $np^2\to\infty$, we have $4p\le np^3$ for all sufficiently
large~$n$.  Adding the two estimates proves the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Uniform a~priori lower bound]
\label{lem:random-metric-prior}
With probability $1-o(1)$ over the random metric~$D$,
\[
    \optprior(\mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{RM}}(D))
    \ge
    \left(2-\frac{2}{\log n}\right)W.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Consider the canonical tour $T_0=(r,1,\ldots,n,r)$, and let $c_e$ be the
coefficient of the unordered pair~$e$ in~\cref{eq:random-metric-expansion}.
Since $D(e)=2-X_e$, $\E[X_e] = \theta$, and the
coefficients sum to~$W$,
\[
    C_D(T_0)=2W-\sum_e c_eX_e,
    \qquad
    \E_D[C_D(T_0)]=(2-\theta)W.
\]
Thus the canonical tour has unusually low cost only if the total coefficient
weight of distance-$1$ shortcut pairs is unusually large.

Set $\varepsilon:=1/\log n$.  The event
\[
    C_D(T_0)\le(2-\theta-\varepsilon)W
\]
is equivalent to
\[
    \sum_e c_e(X_e-\theta)\ge\varepsilon W.
\]
The variables $X_e$ are independent and each $c_eX_e$ lies in~$[0,c_e]$.
Hence \cref{lem:weighted-hoeffding} gives
\[
    \P_D\left[
        C_D(T_0)\le(2-\theta-\varepsilon)W
    \right]
    \le
    \exp\left(
        -\frac{2\varepsilon^2W^2}{\sum_ec_e^2}
    \right).
\]
For all sufficiently large~$n$, the coefficient bound
\cref{lem:random-metric-coefficients} applies; together with $W\ge np$, it gives
\begin{align*}
    \P_D\left[
        C_D(T_0)\le(2-\theta-\varepsilon)W
    \right]
    &\le
    \exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^2W^2}{np^3}\right)\\
    &\le
    \exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^2n}{p}\right)\\
    &=
    \exp\bigl(-n(\log n)^2\bigr).
\end{align*}

For any other a~priori tour~$T$, a permutation of the non-root labels maps
$T$ to~$T_0$.  Because the distribution of~$D$ is invariant under every such
permutation, the same tail bound holds for~$C_D(T)$.  There are at most $n!$
master tours with the root fixed, so the union bound~\cref{eq:union-bound}
shows that the probability
that some tour violates the claimed lower bound is at most
\[
    n!\exp\bigl(-n(\log n)^2\bigr)
    \le
    \exp\bigl(n\log n-n(\log n)^2\bigr)
    =
    o(1).
\]
The conclusion follows because
$\theta=\varepsilon=1/\log n$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A low-cost adaptive policy}\label{sec:random-metric-adaptive-policy}

To complement the uniform lower bound, we construct a policy that exhausts
distance-$1$ unprobed neighbors before making a distance-$2$ move.  If $s$
active vertices remain when the policy reaches a fresh vertex, the probability
that at least one of them is a distance-$1$ neighbor is
$1-(1-\theta)^s$.  We bound the average cost of this policy over the random
metric and then fix a metric
realization for which this bound and the preceding a~priori bound hold
simultaneously.

Fix a deterministic realization~$d$ in the support of~$D$.  Its short-edge
graph $H(d)$, and hence the whole metric, is known before any activations are
probed.  Define the \emph{nearest-unprobed-neighbor policy}~$\pi_d$ as follows.
At a state $(u,S)$ with $S\ne\varnothing$, choose a vertex~$v\in S$ that
minimizes $d(u,v)$, breaking ties by the labels $1,\ldots,n$, and probe~$v$.
If $S=\varnothing$, return to~$r$.  Equivalently, the policy probes every
distance-$1$ unprobed neighbor of its current location before it probes a
vertex at distance~$2$.

The metric is sampled in advance and is known to the policy.  For the
analysis, however, we may reveal its independent edge variables only when
the policy uses them.  The following invariant formalizes this deferred
exposure.

\begin{lemma}[Fresh-edge invariant]\label{lem:random-metric-fresh-edges}
Consider the joint experiment over the random metric~$D$ and the active
set~$A$, and expose only the metric edges incident to the policy's current
location.  Initially, when $u=r$, and immediately after every arrival at a
new active vertex~$u$, let $S$ be the set of unprobed vertices.  Conditional
on the complete exposure and probe history so far, no edge induced by
$\{u\}\cup S$ has been exposed.  Consequently, the conditional distribution
of the short-edge graph induced by $\{u\}\cup S$ is
$G_{1+|S|,\theta}$, independently of the activation states on~$S$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
At the initial state no metric edge has been exposed.  Suppose the invariant
holds at a current vertex~$u$.  Reveal the edges $\{u,v\}$ for $v\in S$;
these are the only metric variables needed to determine the probes made while
the policy remains at~$u$.  If an active probed vertex~$v$ is reached,
let $S'\subseteq S\setminus\{v\}$ be the vertices still unprobed.  The edges
induced by $\{v\}\cup S'$ were not among the edges just exposed, and they
were not exposed earlier by the induction hypothesis.  Independence of the
edge variables gives their conditional law $G_{1+|S'|,\theta}$.  The metric
and activation processes are independent, so this law is also independent
of the activation states on~$S'$.  This proves the invariant by induction.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Cost of the adaptive policy]
\label{lem:random-metric-adaptive-average}
Let
\[
    U(d):=\cost_d(\pi_d)
    =\E_{A\sim\mathcal Q}[\cost_d(T(A;\pi_d))].
\]
Then $U(d)\ge W$ for every~$d$, and
\[
    \E_D[U(D)]
    \le
    np+\left(\frac1\theta+1\right)(1-q^n)
    \le
    W+\frac1\theta.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix $k\ge1$ and condition on $K=k$.  Consider the execution jointly over
the random metric and the active set.  Immediately after the policy
arrives at an active vertex~$u$, let $S$ be the set of unprobed vertices and
suppose that exactly $s$ vertices in~$S$ are active.

By \cref{lem:random-metric-fresh-edges}, the edges from~$u$ to these $s$
active vertices are conditionally independent, and each is short with
probability~$\theta$.  The next edge used has length~$2$ exactly when
none of them is short.  Its conditional expected length is therefore
\[
    1+(1-\theta)^s.
\]
As the policy visits the $k$ active vertices, the value of~$s$ before the
successive edges used is $k,k-1,\ldots,1$.  The final return to the root
costs at most~$2$.  Hence
\begin{align*}
    \E_{D,A}[\cost_D(T(A;\pi_D))\mid K=k]
    &\le
    \sum_{s=1}^k\bigl(1+(1-\theta)^s\bigr)+2\\
    &\le
    k+\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}+2\\
    &=
    k+\frac1\theta+1.
\end{align*}
For $K=0$ the cost is~$0$.  Averaging over~$K$ gives
\[
    \E_D[U(D)]
    \le \E[K]
       +\left(\frac1\theta+1\right)\P[K\ge1]
    =W+\frac{\P[K\ge1]}{\theta}
    \le W+\frac1\theta.
\]

For the pointwise lower bound, if $K=k\ge1$, the realized tour uses exactly
$k+1$ edges, each of length at least~$1$.  Taking expectation over the active
set gives $U(d)\ge W$.
\end{proof}

Since $W\ge np$,
\[
    \theta W\ge\frac{n}{(\log n)^5}\longrightarrow\infty.
\]
Set $\delta_n:=1/\sqrt{\theta W}=o(1)$.  The following lemma shows that the
adaptive optimum is at most $(1+o(1))np$ for most sampled metrics.

\begin{lemma}[High-probability adaptive upper bound]
\label{lem:random-metric-adaptive-whp}
\[
    \P_D\bigl[
        \optadapt(\mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{RM}}(D))
        \le(1+\delta_n)W
    \bigr]
    \ge1-\delta_n.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The nonnegative random variable $U(D)-W$ satisfies
\[
    \E_D[U(D)-W]\le\frac1\theta
\]
by \cref{lem:random-metric-adaptive-average}.  Markov's inequality
\cref{eq:markov} gives
\begin{align*}
    \P_D[U(D)>(1+\delta_n)W]
    &=
    \P_D[U(D)-W>\delta_nW]\\
    &\le
    \frac{1/\theta}{\delta_nW}
    =
    \frac1{\sqrt{\theta W}}
    =
    \delta_n.
\end{align*}
Since $\optadapt(\mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{RM}}(d))\le U(d)$ for every~$d$,
the lemma follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:random-metric-gap}]
By \cref{lem:random-metric-prior}
and~\cref{lem:random-metric-adaptive-whp}, with probability $1-o(1)$ the
sampled metric satisfies both
\[
    \optprior(\mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{RM}}(D))
    \ge
    \left(2-\frac{2}{\log n}\right)W
\]
and
\[
    \optadapt(\mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{RM}}(D))
    \le(1+\delta_n)W.
\]
The two events therefore have a nonempty intersection for all sufficiently
large~$n$.  Choose a deterministic realization~$d_n$ in this intersection
and set
\[
    \mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{RM}}
    :=\mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{RM}}(d_n).
\]
Then
\[
    \frac{\optprior(\mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{RM}})}
         {\optadapt(\mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{RM}})}
    \ge
    \frac{2-2/\log n}{1+\delta_n}
    =
    2-o(1).
\]

It remains to verify the stated convergence, rather than only a lower limit.
For every metric in the support of the construction, every shortcut tour on a
nonempty active set of size~$k$ uses $k+1$ edges of length at most~$2$.
Consequently, $\optprior(\mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{RM}})\le2W$.  On the other
hand, every adaptive policy has expected cost at least~$W$.  Thus
\[
    \frac{\optprior(\mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{RM}})}
         {\optadapt(\mathcal{I}_n^{\mathrm{RM}})}
    \le2.
\]
Combining the two bounds proves that the ratio converges to~$2$.
\end{proof}

\chapter{Clairvoyance Gap for Symmetric Metrics}\label{ch:sym-clair}

\providecommand{\Gammaone}{\Gamma_{1}}
\newcommand{\threechaininstance}[1]{\mathcal{I}_{#1}^{\mathrm{3C}}}

In this chapter, we prove that the clairvoyance gap for symmetric \StochTSP{}
is at least~$10/9$ using a family of instances with one semi-active vertex
$s$ of activation probability~$1/2$.
Throughout this chapter, all instances discussed are symmetric.

Optimal tours for the active and inactive realizations use incompatible chain
orders.  An adaptive policy learns which realization applies only when it
probes~$s$.  Delaying the probe forces it to begin its route without knowing
which order is preferable, whereas probing~$s$ too early can be costly because
an active probe requires immediate travel to~$s$.

As a complementary result, we prove that every instance with one
semi-active vertex of activation probability~$p\in[0,1]$ satisfies
\[
  \frac{\optadapt}{\optpost}\le\frac{2+p}{2+p^2}.
\]
The right-hand side is maximized at $p=\sqrt6-2$.  Therefore the following
uniform bound holds for instances with exactly one semi-active vertex:
\[
  \frac{\optadapt}{\optpost}
  \le\frac{2+\sqrt6}{4}
  \approx1.1124.
\]

\section{The three-chain construction}\label{sec:three-chain-construction}

We use a family of instances with one semi-active vertex to obtain a lower
bound of~$10/9$ on the clairvoyance gap.  When $s$ is inactive, one optimal
tour uses the order $A,B,C$; when $s$ is active, one uses the incompatible order
$A,C,s,B$.  An a posteriori tour can choose the appropriate order after seeing
whether $s$ is active, but an adaptive policy must commit to part of its route
before it has this information.

Fix an integer $m\ge3$ and set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:HUP}
  H:=2m,
  \qquad
  U:=3m+2,
  \qquad
  P:=4m=2H.
\end{equation}

\begin{construction}\label{con:three-chain}
The graph contains the root $r=a_0$, one semi-active vertex $s$, and three
paths
\[
  A=(a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_m),
  \qquad
  B=(b_0,b_1,\ldots,b_m),
  \qquad
  C=(c_0,c_1,\ldots,c_m).
\]
Every path edge has length $2$, so each chain has total length $H=2m$.
Add four edges of length $U$, which we call the \emph{$U$-edges},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:u-edges}
  a_0b_m,
  \qquad
  a_0c_m,
  \qquad
  a_mb_0,
  \qquad
  a_mc_0,
\end{equation}
and three edges of length $P$, which we call the \emph{$P$-edges},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:p-edges}
  b_mc_0,
  \qquad
  b_0s,
  \qquad
  c_ms.
\end{equation}
The $P$-edges provide two ways to connect $B$ and $C$: directly through
$b_mc_0$, or through $s$ using $b_0s$ and $sc_m$.  The $U$-edges are the
remaining connections between chains, joining $A$ to either $B$ or $C$.
The metric $d$ is the shortest-path metric of this graph.  Every non-root
vertex other than $s$ is always active, while $s$ is active with probability
$1/2$.  Denote the resulting three-chain instance by
$\threechaininstance{m}$.
\end{construction}

To compute the a posteriori benchmark, we identify an optimal tour in each
case.  When $s$ is inactive, an optimal tour is $T_0$, which visits all
vertices on the three always-active chains in the order $A,B,C$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:T0}
\begin{split}
  T_0:\quad
  &a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_m,
  b_0,b_1,\ldots,b_m,\\
  &c_0,c_1,\ldots,c_m,a_0,
\end{split}
\end{equation}
When $s$ is active, an optimal tour is $T_1$, with order $A,C,s,B$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:T1}
\begin{split}
  T_1:\quad
  &a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_m,
  c_0,c_1,\ldots,c_m,\\
  &s,b_0,b_1,\ldots,b_m,a_0.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Their costs are
\begin{align}
  L_0&:=\cost(T_0)=3H+2U+P=16m+4,
  \label{eq:L0}\\
  L_1&:=\cost(T_1)=3H+2U+2P=20m+4.
  \label{eq:L1}
\end{align}

For $m\ge3$, these parameters also satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:three-chain-parameter-relations}
  U<P<2U,
  \qquad
  4H-4+4U=3H+2U+2P=L_1.
\end{equation}

The incompatibility lies in which chain is visited first after~$A$:
$T_0$ visits $B$ before $C$, whereas $T_1$ visits $C$ before $B$.

Here is how this incompatibility gives a lower bound for every adaptive
policy.  Let $C_0$ and $C_1$ be the costs of a policy when $s$ is inactive
and active, respectively.  Until the policy probes~$s$, it receives the same information
in both cases and must therefore follow the same initial route up to that
probe.  We show that when $s$ is inactive, a route with cost $C_0<L_1$ must have the same chain order
as~$T_0$; while when $s$ is active, a route with cost $C_1<L_1+P$ must have the same chain order as
$T_1$, up to reversing the entire tour.  In the second
case, the policy must use either $a_mc_0$ or $a_0b_m$ before probing~$s$, but then neither edge can occur in a route of cost below~$L_1$ when $s$ is inactive.
Thus the two inequalities cannot hold simultaneously.

Consequently, either $C_0\ge L_1$ or $C_1\ge L_1+P$.  We also always have
$C_0\ge L_0$ and $C_1\ge L_1$, because $T_0$ and $T_1$ are optimal in their
respective cases.  If $C_0\ge L_1$, then both costs are at least~$L_1$.  If
$C_1\ge L_1+P$, then $C_0+C_1\ge L_0+L_1+P=2L_1$.  Since the two cases each
have probability~$1/2$, every adaptive policy has expected cost at least
$L_1$.  A policy that follows~$T_1$ costs at most~$L_1$ whether $s$ is active
or inactive, so the optimal adaptive cost is exactly~$L_1$.
\Cref{lem:inactive-low-cost} characterizes all routes of cost below~$L_1$
when $s$ is inactive, and \cref{lem:active-low-cost} characterizes all routes
of cost below~$L_1+P$ when $s$ is active.
\Cref{lem:adaptive-lower-bound} combines these two characterizations to prove
that every adaptive policy has expected cost at least~$L_1$.

\subsection{Adaptive policy lower bound}
\label{sec:three-chain-low-cost-routes}

Because $d$ is the shortest-path metric, every tour move can be realised by moving along
graph edges without changing its cost.  We therefore treat tours as
closed walks in the graph.  If a shortest path is not unique, fix one choice
and use its reverse for the reverse move.  Merely passing through an
intermediate vertex does not probe it, while probing an inactive vertex adds
no movement to the walk.
We call each traversal of one of the seven non-chain edges in
\cref{eq:u-edges,eq:p-edges} a \emph{crossing}.  \Cref{lem:chain-contribution}
provides the cost bounds used throughout this section: it shows how much the
chain edges contribute and when connecting to a chain at the same endpoint
forces additional cost.  These bounds are then used to determine how a
low-cost tour can move between the chains.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:chain-contribution}
Let $W$ be the closed graph walk representing a tour that visits all vertices
on $A,B,C$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item The internal edges of each chain contribute at least $H$ to
  $\cost(W)$.
  \item If a chain has exactly two crossing incidences and both are at the
  same endpoint, the internal edges of that chain contribute at least $2H$.
  \item Suppose all four $U$-edges in \cref{eq:u-edges} are traversed
  exactly once.  Then $A$ has two external incidences at each endpoint, and
  its internal contribution is at least
  \[
    2(H-2)=2H-4.
  \]
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Each chain is a path connected to the rest of the graph only at its two
endpoints.  If
two internal edges were unused, the vertices in a component strictly between
two unused edges would be disconnected from $W$.  Hence at most
one internal edge can be unused.

If every internal edge is used, the contribution is at least $H$.  If exactly
one length-$2$ edge is unused, each of the two remaining path components is
attached to the rest of the walk at only one endpoint.  Since the walk is
closed, every used edge in either component is traversed at least twice.
Consequently the contribution is at least $2(H-2)\ge H$, proving (i).

For (ii), both external incidences occur at one endpoint.  To visit the other
endpoint and return, every internal edge must be traversed at least twice, for
a contribution of at least $2H$.

For (iii), the number of external incidences at each endpoint of $A$ is even.
Because $W$ is closed, every vertex has even degree in its edge multiset.
Thus every used edge of the path $A$ has even multiplicity.  At most one
internal edge can be unused, so the contribution is at least $2(H-2)$.
\end{proof}

We next determine which connections between the chains are possible when the
tour cost is smaller than the thresholds stated above.  The first lemma
considers the case in which $s$ is inactive, and the second considers the case
in which it is active.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:inactive-low-cost}
When $s$ is inactive, the optimal tour cost is $L_0$.  Moreover, every tour of
cost strictly less than $L_1$ has, up to reversal, exactly the following three
crossings:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inactive-crossings}
  a_mb_0,
  \qquad
  b_mc_0,
  \qquad
  c_ma_0.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Represent a tour visiting all always-active vertices as a closed graph walk.
If this walk passes through $s$ on one of its chosen shortest paths, then it
has at least two crossings incident to $A$, each of cost $U$, and at least
two crossings incident to $s$, each of cost $P$.  By
\cref{lem:chain-contribution}(i), its cost is at least
\[
  3H+2U+2P=L_1.
\]
Thus a tour of cost below $L_1$ does not traverse $s$.

Contract each of $A,B,C$ to one vertex.  Let $x,y,z$ be the respective numbers
of $A$--$B$, $A$--$C$, and $B$--$C$ crossings.  The contracted multigraph is
connected and Eulerian, so
\[
  x+y\equiv x+z\equiv y+z\equiv0\pmod2.
\]
Hence $x,y,z$ have the same parity.

Suppose first that they are odd.  Then $x,y,z\ge1$.  If one is at least $3$,
the crossing cost is at least $4U+P$, and the total cost is at least
\[
  3H+4U+P>L_1,
\]
because $2U>P$.  Therefore a tour below $L_1$ must have
$x=y=z=1$.  Its contracted walk is the triangle through $A,B,C$ and its
crossing cost is $2U+P$.

The $B$--$C$ crossing is necessarily $b_mc_0$.  For $B$ to use opposite
endpoints, the $A$--$B$ crossing must be $a_mb_0$; for $C$ to use opposite
endpoints, the $A$--$C$ crossing must be $a_0c_m$.  These choices also make
$A$ use opposite endpoints.  Conversely, if one chain uses the same endpoint
twice, at least one other chain does as well.  By
\cref{lem:chain-contribution}, a noncanonical port choice has internal
contribution at least $2H+2H+H=5H$.  Its total cost is therefore at least
\[
  5H+2U+P=L_1,
\]
where we used $P=2H$.  Hence every tour below $L_1$ in the odd case has the
three crossings in \cref{eq:inactive-crossings}, up to reversal.  Its cost is
at least $3H+2U+P=L_0$, and $T_0$ attains this bound.

Now suppose $x,y,z$ are even.  Connectivity implies that at least two of them
are positive.  If $z>0$, then there are at least two $B$--$C$ crossings and at
least two crossings incident to $A$, so the total cost is at least
\[
  3H+2U+2P=L_1.
\]
Assume therefore that $z=0$.  Then $x,y\ge2$.  If $x+y\ge6$, the total cost is
at least $3H+6U>L_1$.  The only remaining possibility is
\[
  x=y=2,
  \qquad
  z=0.
\]
If $B$ or $C$ uses the same endpoint twice, the total internal contribution is
at least $4H$, and hence the total cost is at least
\[
  4H+4U=L_1+4>L_1.
\]
Otherwise both $B$ and $C$ use opposite endpoints.  Hence each of the four
$U$-edges is used exactly once.  By
\cref{lem:chain-contribution}(iii), the internal contribution is at least
\[
  H+H+(2H-4)=4H-4.
\]
Thus the total cost is at least
\[
  4H-4+4U
  =20m+4
  =L_1.
\]
This excludes the even case below $L_1$ and completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:active-low-cost}
When $s$ is active, the optimal tour cost is $L_1$.  Moreover, every tour of
cost strictly less than $L_1+P$ has, up to reversal, exactly the following
four crossings:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:active-crossings}
  a_mc_0,
  \qquad
  c_ms,
  \qquad
  sb_0,
  \qquad
  b_ma_0.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $W$ be the closed graph walk representing any tour that visits all active
vertices.  It has at least two crossings incident to $A$, each of cost $U$,
and at least two crossings incident to $s$, each of cost $P$.  Therefore
\[
  \cost(W)\ge3H+2U+2P=L_1
\]
by \cref{lem:chain-contribution}(i), and $T_1$ attains this value.

Now consider a tour of cost below $L_1+P$, and let $N_\times$ be its total number of
crossings.  After contracting $A,B,C$ and retaining $s$, we obtain a connected
Eulerian multigraph on four vertices.  Hence every contracted vertex has
positive even degree and $N_\times\ge4$.

If $N_\times=4$, every contracted degree is $2$, so the quotient is the four-cycle
\[
  A-B-s-C-A
\]
up to reversal.  The two edges incident to $s$ are necessarily $b_0s$ and
$c_ms$.  In order that $B$ and $C$ use opposite endpoints, the remaining two
crossings must be $a_0b_m$ and $a_mc_0$; these choices also make $A$ use
opposite endpoints.  Any other port choice makes at least two chains use the
same endpoint twice.  Its internal contribution is then at least $5H$, so its
cost is at least
\[
  5H+2U+2P=L_1+P.
\]
Thus strict inequality forces exactly the four crossings in
\cref{eq:active-crossings}, up to reversal.

If $N_\times=5$, let $N_A$ and $N_s$ be the numbers of crossings incident to
$A$ and $s$, respectively, and let $N_{BC}$ count the $B$--$C$ crossings.
The first two numbers are positive and even.  Every crossing belongs to
exactly one of these three categories, so
$N_\times=5$ forces
\[
  N_A=2,
  \qquad
  N_s=2,
  \qquad
  N_{BC}=1.
\]
The crossing cost is at least $2U+3P$, and hence the total cost is at least
\[
  3H+2U+3P=L_1+P.
\]

Finally, if $N_\times\ge6$, the two mandatory $A$-crossings and two mandatory
$s$-crossings are supplemented by at least two further crossings.  Every
additional crossing costs at least $U$, so
\[
  \cost(W)
  \ge3H+4U+2P
  >3H+2U+3P
  =L_1+P,
\]
where the strict inequality is $2U>P$.  This proves the claim.
\end{proof}

We now combine the two descriptions of low-cost tours to obtain the adaptive
lower bound.  The executions when $s$ is active and inactive are identical
until $s$ is probed, so an adaptive policy cannot follow both low-cost
crossing patterns.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:adaptive-lower-bound}
Every adaptive policy for $\threechaininstance{m}$ has expected cost at
least
\[
  L_1=20m+4.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $C_0$ and $C_1$ be the costs of an arbitrary adaptive policy when $s$ is
inactive and active, respectively.  Suppose, for contradiction, that its
expected cost is smaller than~$L_1$, so that
\[
  \frac{C_0+C_1}{2}<L_1.
\]
By \cref{lem:inactive-low-cost,lem:active-low-cost}, every valid tour in the
two cases costs at least $L_0$ and $L_1$, respectively.  Hence
\[
  C_0<L_1,
  \qquad
  C_1<2L_1-L_0=L_1+P.
\]

Represent every movement by the fixed shortest paths chosen above.  Before
the policy probes $s$, all probed vertices are always active.  Therefore the
execution when $s$ is active and the execution when $s$ is inactive make
exactly the same probes, occupy the same locations, and traverse the same
graph edges up to the moment at which $s$ is probed.

By \cref{lem:inactive-low-cost}, the inactive execution uses only the three
crossings in \cref{eq:inactive-crossings}.  By
\cref{lem:active-low-cost}, the active execution uses only the four crossings
in \cref{eq:active-crossings}.

No movement in the active execution can pass through $s$ before it is probed:
such a transit would already use two crossings incident to~$s$, while the
mandatory post-probe movement to the active vertex~$s$ would use another;
the four crossings in \cref{eq:active-crossings} include only two incident
to~$s$.  After contracting $A,B,C$, the active execution follows a four-cycle.
It starts in $A$, so the first visit to $s$ is preceded by one of the quotient
paths
\[
  A-C-s
  \qquad\text{or}\qquad
  A-B-s.
\]
The corresponding chain $X\in\{B,C\}$ has exactly two crossing incidences in
the entire walk: one from $A$ and one to $s$.  The $X$--$s$ crossing occurs in
the movement immediately after the remote probe of the active vertex~$s$.
The $A$--$X$ crossing must occur earlier.  Otherwise the execution has not
entered $X$ before probing~$s$, so none of the always-active vertices of~$X$
has been probed: probing any one of them would force an immediate movement
into~$X$.  After the movement to~$s$, both crossings incident to $X$ would
already have been used, and visiting any remaining vertex of~$X$ would require
an additional crossing, contradicting \cref{lem:active-low-cost}.

Thus, before probing $s$, the common prefix has already used either
\[
  a_mc_0
  \qquad\text{or}\qquad
  a_0b_m,
\]
depending on the orientation of the active-case tour.  Neither crossing occurs
among the three crossings in \cref{eq:inactive-crossings}.  This contradicts
the fact that the execution when $s$ is inactive has the same prefix before
the probe of $s$.  Therefore every adaptive policy has expected cost at
least~$L_1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The adaptive optimum}\label{sec:three-chain-adaptive-optimum}

\begin{theorem}[Cost of the three-chain instance]\label{thm:three-chain-cost}
For every integer $m\ge3$, the instance $\threechaininstance{m}$ in
\cref{con:three-chain} satisfies
\[
  \optpost(\threechaininstance{m})=18m+4,
  \qquad
  \optadapt(\threechaininstance{m})=20m+4.
\]
Therefore
\[
  \frac{\optadapt(\threechaininstance{m})}
       {\optpost(\threechaininstance{m})}
  =\frac{20m+4}{18m+4}
  =\frac{10m+2}{9m+2},
\]
and the ratio converges to $10/9$ as $m\to\infty$.  Consequently, the
clairvoyance gap is at least~$10/9$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{lem:inactive-low-cost,lem:active-low-cost}, the two posterior optimum
costs are $L_0$ and $L_1$.  Since $p_s=1/2$,
\[
  \optpost(\threechaininstance{m})
  =\frac{L_0+L_1}{2}=18m+4.
\]

For an adaptive upper bound, follow the order of $T_1$ and probe $s$ upon
reaching $c_m$.  If $s$ is active, the realized tour is exactly $T_1$.  If it
is inactive, probe $b_0$ next.  The metric distance satisfies
\[
  d(c_m,b_0)\le d(c_m,s)+d(s,b_0)=2P,
\]
which is exactly the cost of the two $P$-edges used by the active branch.
Thus both realized costs are at most $L_1$, and
$\optadapt(\threechaininstance{m})\le L_1$.

The reverse inequality follows from \cref{lem:adaptive-lower-bound}.  Hence
$\optadapt(\threechaininstance{m})=L_1$.
\end{proof}

\section{Clairvoyance gap with a single semi-active vertex}
\label{sec:one-stochastic-upper-bound}

For $p\in[0,1]$, let $\Gammaone(p)$ denote the worst-case clairvoyance gap
among symmetric instances with exactly one semi-active vertex whose activation
probability is~$p$.  The subscript~$1$ refers to this single semi-active
vertex.  Thus,
\[
  \Gammaone(p)
  :=\sup_{\mathcal I}
  \frac{\optadapt(\mathcal I)}{\optpost(\mathcal I)},
\]
where the supremum ranges over all such instances, with any number of
always-active non-root vertices.

We now consider an arbitrary instance with exactly one semi-active vertex $s$,
active with probability $p\in(0,1)$.  Let $D$ be the
set of always-active non-root vertices.  If $D=\varnothing$, then probing
$s$ at the root is posterior-optimal whether $s$ is active or inactive, so the
clairvoyance gap is $1$.  We therefore assume $D\ne\varnothing$.  Let $T_0$ be
an optimal tour on
$\{r\}\cup D$, and let $T_1$ be an optimal tour on
$\{r\}\cup D\cup\{s\}$.  Write
\[
  \ell_0:=\cost(T_0),
  \qquad
  \ell_1:=\cost(T_1),
  \qquad
  \Delta:=\ell_1-\ell_0.
\]
Shortcutting $s$ from $T_1$ shows that $\ell_0\le\ell_1$, so $\Delta\ge0$.  The
posterior optimum is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:post-general}
  \optpost=(1-p)\ell_0+p\ell_1=\ell_0+p\Delta.
\end{equation}

We need two local exchange quantities.

First, define the cheapest insertion surcharge of $s$ into $T_0$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:alpha}
  \alpha
  :=\min_{uv\in E(T_0)}
  \bigl(d(u,s)+d(s,v)-d(u,v)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Inserting $s$ into the minimizing edge gives a full tour of cost $\ell_0+\alpha$,
so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta-alpha}
  \Delta\le\alpha.
\end{equation}

Second, let $x,y$ be the two neighbors of $s$ in $T_1$, and define the saving
from shortcutting $s$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta}
  \beta:=d(x,s)+d(s,y)-d(x,y).
\end{equation}
The triangle inequality gives $\beta\ge0$, and deleting $s$ from $T_1$ gives
a tour on the always-active vertices of cost $\ell_1-\beta$.  Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta-delta}
  0\le\beta\le\Delta.
\end{equation}

The key point is that the cheapest insertion surcharge into $T_0$ is
controlled by the deletion saving in $T_1$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:exchange}
The quantities in \cref{eq:alpha,eq:beta} satisfy
\[
  \alpha\le\frac{\ell_0}{2}+\beta.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The vertices $x,y$ split the cycle $T_0$ into two $x$--$y$ arcs.  Let $P$ be
the shorter arc.  Then
\[
  \cost(P)\le\frac{\ell_0}{2},
  \qquad
  d(x,y)\le\cost(P)\le\frac{\ell_0}{2}.
\]
By the definition of $\beta$,
\[
  d(x,s)+d(s,y)=d(x,y)+\beta\le\frac{\ell_0}{2}+\beta.
\]
Therefore one of $x,y$, say $z$, satisfies
\[
  d(z,s)\le\frac{\ell_0}{4}+\frac\beta2.
\]
Let $z'$ be either neighbor of $z$ in $T_0$.  Inserting $s$ into the edge
$zz'$ costs
\[
\begin{aligned}
  d(z,s)+d(s,z')-d(z,z')
  &\le d(z,s)+\bigl(d(s,z)+d(z,z')\bigr)-d(z,z')\\
  &=2d(z,s)\\
  &\le\frac{\ell_0}{2}+\beta.
\end{aligned}
\]
Since $\alpha$ is the minimum insertion surcharge, the claim follows.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Two adaptive policies}\label{sec:one-stochastic-policies}

We translate the geometric quantities above into an adaptive upper bound by
considering two complementary policies.  One follows the optimal tour on the
always-active vertices and inserts $s$ when it is active; the other follows
the active-realization tour and shortcuts $s$ when it is inactive.  Taking the
better of the two gives the following estimate.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:two-policies}
For every instance with one semi-active vertex,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:two-policy-bound}
  \optadapt
  \le
  \min\left\{
    \ell_0+p\left(\frac{\ell_0}{2}+\beta\right),
    \;\ell_0+\Delta-(1-p)\beta
  \right\}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For the first policy, orient $T_0$ and follow it until reaching one endpoint
$u$ of an edge $uv$ attaining the minimum in \cref{eq:alpha}.  Probe $s$ at
$u$.  If $s$ is inactive, continue directly to $v$ and complete $T_0$, at cost
$\ell_0$.  If $s$ is active, traverse $u,s,v$ and then complete $T_0$, at cost
$\ell_0+\alpha$.  Thus the expected cost is
\[
  \ell_0+p\alpha
  \le \ell_0+p\left(\frac{\ell_0}{2}+\beta\right)
\]
by \cref{lem:exchange}.

For the second policy, orient $T_1$ so that the local order is
$x,s,y$.  Follow $T_1$ to $x$ and probe $s$.  If $s$ is active, complete
$T_1$, at cost $\ell_1$.  If $s$ is inactive, move directly from $x$ to $y$ and
complete the shortcut tour, at cost $\ell_1-\beta$.  Its expected cost is
\[
  p\ell_1+(1-p)(\ell_1-\beta)
  =\ell_1-(1-p)\beta
  =\ell_0+\Delta-(1-p)\beta.
\]
Taking the better policy gives \cref{eq:two-policy-bound}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Choice of activation probability}\label{sec:one-stochastic-optimization}

It remains to optimize the two-policy estimate over the insertion and
shortcut savings permitted by the metric.  After normalizing these quantities
by the cost of the tour on the always-active vertices, the problem reduces to
comparing two affine bounds in one variable.  This yields the general ratio
below.

\begin{theorem}[One semi-active vertex]\label{thm:general-upper}
For every instance with exactly one semi-active vertex of activation
probability $p\in[0,1]$,
\[
  \frac{\optadapt}{\optpost}
  \le\frac{2+p}{2+p^2}.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
  \Gammaone(p)\le\frac{2+p}{2+p^2}.
\]
Moreover, the right-hand side is maximized at $p=\sqrt6-2$, and hence
\[
  \Gammaone(p)\le\frac{2+\sqrt6}{4}
  \qquad\text{for every }p\in[0,1].
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The cases $p=0$ and $p=1$ have no information gap.  When the common
optimum is positive, the ratio is $1$; when it is zero, our convention gives
ratio $0$.  Thus the claimed bound holds, so assume $p\in(0,1)$.  Because
$D\ne\varnothing$ and $d$ is a metric, $\ell_0>0$.  Normalize
\[
  \delta:=\frac{\Delta}{\ell_0},
  \qquad
  b:=\frac{\beta}{\ell_0}.
\]
By \cref{eq:post-general,eq:two-policy-bound},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalized-bound}
  \frac{\optadapt}{\ell_0}
  \le
  \min\left\{
    1+\frac p2+pb,
    \;1+\delta-(1-p)b
  \right\},
  \qquad
  \frac{\optpost}{\ell_0}=1+p\delta.
\end{equation}

Suppose first that $\delta\le p/2$.  The second policy and $b\ge0$ give
\[
  \frac{\optadapt}{\ell_0}\le1+\delta.
\]
The function $(1+\delta)/(1+p\delta)$ is increasing in $\delta$ because
$p<1$.  Hence
\[
  \frac{\optadapt}{\optpost}
  \le\frac{1+\delta}{1+p\delta}
  \le\frac{1+p/2}{1+p^2/2}
  =\frac{2+p}{2+p^2}.
\]

Now suppose that $\delta\ge p/2$ and set
\[
  b_0:=\delta-\frac p2\ge0.
\]
If $b\le b_0$, the first term in \cref{eq:normalized-bound} gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \frac{\optadapt}{\ell_0}
  &\le1+\frac p2+pb\\
  &\le1+\frac p2+p\left(\delta-\frac p2\right)\\
  &=1+p\delta+\frac{p(1-p)}2.
\end{aligned}
\]
If $b\ge b_0$, the second term gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \frac{\optadapt}{\ell_0}
  &\le1+\delta-(1-p)b\\
  &\le1+\delta-(1-p)\left(\delta-\frac p2\right)\\
  &=1+p\delta+\frac{p(1-p)}2.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus in either case,
\[
  \frac{\optadapt}{\optpost}
  \le
  1+\frac{p(1-p)/2}{1+p\delta}.
\]
Since $\delta\ge p/2$,
\[
  \frac{\optadapt}{\optpost}
  \le
  1+\frac{p(1-p)/2}{1+p^2/2}
  =\frac{1+p/2}{1+p^2/2}
  =\frac{2+p}{2+p^2}.
\]
This proves the probability-dependent bound.

Finally, for
\[
  f(p):=\frac{2+p}{2+p^2},
\]
the sign of
\[
  f'(p)=\frac{2-4p-p^2}{(2+p^2)^2}
\]
changes from positive to negative at the unique point
$p=\sqrt6-2$ in~$[0,1]$.  Therefore $f$ is maximized there, with
\[
  f(\sqrt6-2)=\frac{2+\sqrt6}{4}.
\]
\end{proof}

At $p=1/2$, the bound in \cref{thm:general-upper} equals~$10/9$, and
\cref{thm:three-chain-cost} shows that it is tight within the
one-semi-active-vertex class.

\chapter{Clairvoyance Gap for Asymmetric \StochTSP{}}\label{ch:asym-clair}

We now turn to asymmetric \StochTSP{} and present two constructions.
The first is a small subdivided-triangle example and its natural recursive
generalization.  At each recursive level, a clairvoyant tour chooses the
cheaper orientation after seeing whether a new midpoint is active, whereas an
adaptive policy must make this choice before knowing the activation.

Our main construction proves a clairvoyance gap lower bound of~$3/2$ using a
cyclic layered graph with alternating gate and routing columns.  A
clairvoyant tour needs essentially two left-to-right paths, while every
adaptive policy needs a third wraparound with high probability.

The subdivided-triangle instances have a clear motivation, but its
ratio tends only to~$4/3$ and its full analysis is more complicated,
so we do not expand the full analysis here.

\section{The recursive subdivided-triangle construction}
\label{sec:recursive-construction}

A tiny instance in \cref{fig:recursive-triangle-idea} is constructed in this way:
start from the symmetric unit triangle on
$r=a,b,c$, keep the arc $b\to c$, and subdivide the reverse arc by a new
vertex~$d$. So in summary we have edges:
\[
  a\leftrightarrow b,\qquad
  a\leftrightarrow c,\qquad
  b\to c, \qquad
  c\to d\to b.
\]
The vertices $a,b,c$ are always active, while $d$ is active with probability~$1/2$.

If $d$ is inactive, the posterior tour $a\to b\to c\to a$ costs~$3$.
If $d$ is active, the posterior tour
$a\to c\to d\to b\to a$ costs~$4$.  Hence
\[
  \optpost=\frac12\cdot3+\frac12\cdot4=\frac72.
\]
The two realizations have incompatible optimal visit orders: when $d$ is
inactive, $b$ precedes $c$; when $d$ is active, $c$ precedes $b$, with $d$
between them.
A one-step Bellman calculation over the three possible first probes
$b,c,d$ gives $\optadapt=4$, attained for example by probing in the order $c,d,b$, and the
clairvoyance ratio is
\[
  \frac{\optadapt}{\optpost}=\frac{4}{7/2}=\frac87.
\]
More generally, if $d$ is active with probability~$p$, then
$\optpost=3+p$ and $\optadapt=\min\{3+2p,4\}$; the ratio is maximized at
$p=1/2$.

The recursive family in \cref{fig:recursive-triangle-idea} is the direct
generalization of this example.  At level~$k$, the vertices $b$ and~$c$ are
replaced by two copies $B_{k-1}^{\mathrm L}$ and
$B_{k-1}^{\mathrm R}$, the vertex $d$ becomes a new semi-active midpoint
$m_k$, and each displayed unit arc becomes a complete bundle of port arcs of
length $w_k=2^{k-1}$.  The leaves are always active and all midpoints
are independently active with probability~$1/2$.  With root arcs of length
$w_l$ at depth~$l$, the exact ratio is
\[
  \frac{\optadapt(\mathcal I_l^{\mathrm{REC}})}
       {\optpost(\mathcal I_l^{\mathrm{REC}})}
  =\frac{4(l+1)}{3l+4}
  \longrightarrow\frac43.
\]
The depth-one member is precisely the four-vertex instance above.

\section{A layered-column lower bound}
\label{sec:layered-column-gap}

To motivate the construction, consider a layered graph in which every local
arc moves from left to right and has a small length~$\varepsilon$.  The only
way to return from the last layer to the first is to take a wraparound arc of
length~$1$.  Since the total local-arc cost will be negligible, the cost of a
tour is essentially the number of wraparounds it uses.

The graph has two alternating types of layers.  A \emph{gate column}
\[
    X_i:=\{X_i^1,X_i^2\}
\]
contains two always-active gate vertices, whereas a \emph{routing column}
\[
    Y_i:=\{Y_i^{1,\ast},Y_i^{2,\ast},Y_i^{\ast,1},Y_i^{\ast,2}\}
\]
contains four independently active routing vertices.  A left-to-right
traversal contains at most one vertex from each column, so the maximum number
of active vertices in one column is the natural benchmark for the number of
wraparounds.  The stage below is designed so that two traversals can be
chosen after the realization is known, whereas an adaptive policy may have
to commit before it knows which two local routes are compatible.

A single stage is a $2\times2$ routing switch formed by a routing column~$Y_i$
between the gate columns~$X_{i-1}$ and~$X_i$.  A local two-arc route enters
through a gate~$X_{i-1}^a$, visits one routing vertex, and leaves through a
gate~$X_i^b$.  We record this route by the cell~$(a,b)$;
\cref{fig:layered-column-stage} shows both representations.

The four routing vertices have the following cell representation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:layered-column-matrix}
\begin{array}{c|cc}
&b=1&b=2\\ \hline
a=1&Y_i^{1,\ast},Y_i^{\ast,1}&Y_i^{1,\ast},Y_i^{\ast,2}\\
a=2&Y_i^{2,\ast},Y_i^{\ast,1}&Y_i^{2,\ast},Y_i^{\ast,2}.
\end{array}
\end{equation}
Thus $Y_i^{a,\ast}$ constrains the entrance gate, while $Y_i^{\ast,b}$
constrains the exit gate.  The local route through a routing vertex~$v$ in
cell~$(a,b)$ is the two-arc path $X_{i-1}^a\to v\to X_i^b$.

Condition now on exactly two routing vertices in~$Y_i$ being active.  Since
their activation probabilities are equal, the active pair is uniform among
the six unordered pairs.  In every case, the two active routing vertices can
be assigned to complementary cells---different entrance gates and different
exit gates.  Equivalently, the six vertices consisting of the active pair
and the two adjacent gate columns can be covered by the following two
vertex-disjoint local routes:
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
\text{active pair}
    &\text{route from }X_{i-1}^1
    &\text{route from }X_{i-1}^2\\ \hline
\{Y_i^{1,\ast},Y_i^{2,\ast}\}
    &Y_i^{1,\ast}\mapsto(1,1)&Y_i^{2,\ast}\mapsto(2,2)\\
\{Y_i^{\ast,1},Y_i^{\ast,2}\}
    &Y_i^{\ast,1}\mapsto(1,1)&Y_i^{\ast,2}\mapsto(2,2)\\
\{Y_i^{1,\ast},Y_i^{\ast,1}\}
    &Y_i^{1,\ast}\mapsto(1,2)&Y_i^{\ast,1}\mapsto(2,1)\\
\{Y_i^{1,\ast},Y_i^{\ast,2}\}
    &Y_i^{1,\ast}\mapsto(1,1)&Y_i^{\ast,2}\mapsto(2,2)\\
\{Y_i^{2,\ast},Y_i^{\ast,1}\}
    &Y_i^{\ast,1}\mapsto(1,1)&Y_i^{2,\ast}\mapsto(2,2)\\
\{Y_i^{2,\ast},Y_i^{\ast,2}\}
    &Y_i^{\ast,2}\mapsto(1,2)&Y_i^{2,\ast}\mapsto(2,1).
\end{array}
\]

An a~posteriori solution chooses these two complementary local routes after
seeing the pair.  An adaptive policy may have to choose the exit of its first
route earlier.  For example, suppose that it enters through
$X_{i-1}^1$ and visits~$Y_i^{1,\ast}$.  Leaving through~$X_i^1$ commits to
cell~$(1,1)$; if $Y_i^{\ast,1}$ is the other active routing vertex, then it
cannot use the complementary cell~$(2,2)$, so the routing cannot be completed
with two traversals.  Leaving through~$X_i^2$ instead commits to cell~$(1,2)$;
if $Y_i^{\ast,2}$ is the other active routing vertex, then it cannot use the
complementary cell~$(2,1)$.  The remaining cases follow by exchanging the two
gate labels and, where necessary, interchanging entrance and exit.  The
formal proof below also handles policies that probe
additional routing vertices or later stages before choosing an exit.

We amplify this local incompatibility by concatenating $L$ independent
stages and giving every routing vertex a common activation probability~$p$.
A specified pair is exactly the active set of one routing column with
probability
\[
    h:=p^2(1-p)^2.
\]
The six exact-pair events have total probability~$6h$, but at every fresh
stage the adaptive proof needs only one bad pair determined by the preceding
policy history.  It therefore obtains a failure probability of at least~$h$
per fresh stage.  Consequently, the probability that a policy retains a
two-traversal routing through all stages is at most~$(1-h)^L$.
By contrast, an a~posteriori solution needs more than two traversals only if
some routing column has at least three active vertices, whose probability in
one stage is
\[
    4p^3(1-p)+p^4=O(p^3).
\]
We therefore let $L\to\infty$, $p\to0$, and $\varepsilon\to0$ so that
\[
    Lp^2\to\infty,
    \qquad
    Lp^3\to0,
    \qquad
    L\varepsilon\to0.
\]
The first condition makes a bad-pair stage, and hence a third wraparound,
overwhelmingly likely.  The second keeps the posterior tour at two traversals
with high probability, and the third makes the local-arc cost negligible.
The estimates below therefore give $\optpost=2+o(1)$ and
$\optadapt\ge3-o(1)$, producing the factor~$3/2$.

\subsection{The layered-column instance and its wraparound cost}
\label{sec:layered-column-instance}

\begin{construction}\label{con:layered-column}
Fix an integer $L\ge1$ and parameters $p,\varepsilon\in(0,1)$.  For each
$0\le i\le L$, introduce the gate column
\[
    X_i:=\{X_i^1,X_i^2\},
\]
set the root to be $r:=X_0^1$, and make every gate always active.  For every
stage $1\le i\le L$, introduce the routing column
\[
    Y_i:=\{Y_i^{1,\ast},Y_i^{2,\ast},Y_i^{\ast,1},Y_i^{\ast,2}\},
\]
whose four routing vertices are independently active with probability~$p$.

Define an arc-weighted graph~$H_L$ on these vertices.  For every stage~$i$
and every $a,b\in\{1,2\}$, add the two local two-arc routes
\[
    X_{i-1}^a\to Y_i^{a,\ast}\to X_i^b
    \qquad\text{and}\qquad
    X_{i-1}^a\to Y_i^{\ast,b}\to X_i^b.
\]
Every local arc has length~$\varepsilon$.  Finally, add all four wraparound
arcs from~$X_L$ to~$X_0$, each of length~$1$.

The graph~$H_L$ is strongly connected.  Let $d_\varepsilon$ be its
shortest-path metric, and denote the resulting \StochTSP{} instance by
$\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{LC}}(L,p,\varepsilon)$.  Since every arc of~$H_L$ has
positive length, $d_\varepsilon$ is an asymmetric metric that is strictly
positive off the diagonal.
\end{construction}

The columns occur in the cyclic order
\[
    X_0
    \xrightarrow{\ \varepsilon\ }
    Y_1
    \xrightarrow{\ \varepsilon\ }
    X_1
    \cdots
    X_{L-1}
    \xrightarrow{\ \varepsilon\ }
    Y_L
    \xrightarrow{\ \varepsilon\ }
    X_L
    \xrightarrow{\ 1\ }
    X_0.
\]
This display is schematic: the local arcs are precisely those encoded by
\cref{eq:layered-column-matrix}.  In particular, $r=X_0^1$ is both the starting
point of the tour and one of the two gates in the first column.  Every local
arc moves one column to the right, whereas every return from the last gate
column to the first costs~$1$.

For a realization~$A$, let
\[
    Z_i:=|A\cap Y_i|,
    \qquad
    K(A):=\max\{2,Z_1,\ldots,Z_L\}.
\]
Thus $K(A)$ is the maximum number of active vertices in any column, counting
the two always-active gates in each gate column.

\begin{lemma}[Wraparound cost of a realization]
\label{lem:layered-column-realization}
Every realization~$A$ satisfies
\[
    K(A)
    \le
    \opt(A)
    \le
    (1+2L\varepsilon)K(A).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Expand an optimal metric tour into a closed walk in~$H_L$ by replacing every
metric transition with a shortest path.  If the walk uses $q$ wraparound
arcs, deleting them produces $q$ left-to-right paths, each of which contains
at most one vertex from any column.  The two always-active gates in every
gate column require at least two such paths, and the $Z_i$ active routing
vertices in~$Y_i$ require at least~$Z_i$.  Hence $q\ge K(A)$.  Since every
wraparound arc has length~$1$, $\opt(A)\ge K(A)$.

For the upper bound, construct $K(A)$ left-to-right traversals from~$X_0$
to~$X_L$.  Maintain the invariant that one traversal reaches~$X_i^1$ and the
other $K(A)-1$ traversals reach~$X_i^2$.  Initially, the first traversal
starts at $r=X_0^1$ and the others start at~$X_0^2$.

If $Z_i\le2$, add arbitrary inactive routing vertices, if necessary, to form
a pair and use the six-pair table above to route two traversals through that
pair.  Any remaining traversals enter through~$X_{i-1}^2$ and may reuse a
routing vertex that realizes cell~$(2,2)$.  If $Z_i\ge3$, proceed as follows.
When $Y_i^{1,\ast}$ is active, route it on the unique traversal entering
through $X_{i-1}^1$.  If $Y_i^{\ast,1}$ is also active, send
$Y_i^{1,\ast}$ to~$X_i^2$ and route $Y_i^{\ast,1}$ from~$X_{i-1}^2$ to
$X_i^1$; if $Y_i^{\ast,1}$ is inactive, send $Y_i^{1,\ast}$ to~$X_i^1$.
When $Y_i^{1,\ast}$ is inactive, all of
$Y_i^{2,\ast},Y_i^{\ast,1},Y_i^{\ast,2}$ are active, so route
$Y_i^{\ast,1}$ from~$X_{i-1}^1$ to~$X_i^1$.  In every case, route the
remaining active vertices, which are $Y_i^{2,\ast}$ and/or~$Y_i^{\ast,2}$,
on distinct traversals from~$X_{i-1}^2$ to~$X_i^2$.  Both vertices admit
cell~$(2,2)$.  Any unused traversals may reuse one of them.  This covers every
active routing vertex and preserves the invariant.

Join the $K(A)$ traversals cyclically with wraparound arcs, using the last
one to return to~$r$.  Each traversal uses $2L$ local arcs.  After
shortcutting repeated and inactive vertices, the resulting tour has cost at
most
\[
    (1+2L\varepsilon)K(A).
\]
\end{proof}

\subsection{The posterior tour}\label{sec:posterior-tour}

We first evaluate what an a posteriori solution can achieve once all active
routing vertices are known.  By
\cref{lem:layered-column-realization}, the required number of wraparounds is
governed by the largest routing-column load.  In particular, two traversals
suffice unless some routing column contains at least three active vertices.

\begin{lemma}[A posteriori cost of the layered-column instance]
\label{lem:layered-column-posterior}
For every $L\ge1$ and $p,\varepsilon\in(0,1)$, the a~posteriori optimum
satisfies
\[
    2
    \le
    \optpost\bigl(\mathcal I^{\mathrm{LC}}(L,p,\varepsilon)\bigr)
    \le
    (1+2L\varepsilon)
    \bigl(2+L(4p^3-2p^4)\bigr)
    \le
    (1+2L\varepsilon)(2+4Lp^3).
\]
In particular, if $\varepsilon L=o(1)$ and $Lp^3=o(1)$, then
\[
    \optpost\bigl(\mathcal I^{\mathrm{LC}}(L,p,\varepsilon)\bigr)
    =2+o(1).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For every stage, $Z_i\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(4,p)$.  Since
$K(A)\in\{2,3,4\}$, the union bound gives
\begin{align*}
    \E[K(A)]
    &=2+\P(K(A)\ge3)+\P(K(A)=4)\\
    &\le2+L\bigl(\P(Z_i\ge3)+\P(Z_i=4)\bigr)\\
    &=2+L(4p^3-2p^4)\\
    &\le2+4Lp^3.
\end{align*}
Taking expectations in \cref{lem:layered-column-realization} proves both the
lower and upper bounds.  The asymptotic claim follows immediately.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The adaptive obstruction}\label{sec:adaptive-obstruction}

We next show why an adaptive policy cannot reproduce the posterior tour with
only two wraparounds.  Fix an adaptive policy~$\pi$ and a realization~$A$.
Expand every
metric transition of the realized tour into a shortest path in~$H_L$, choosing
one with the fewest wraparound arcs in case of a tie, and let $W_\pi(A)$ be
the total number of wraparound arcs in the resulting closed walk.  The tour
cost is at least $W_\pi(A)$.

\paragraph{Two-wraparound structure.}
Every realized tour has $W_\pi(A)\ge2$, because it must visit both vertices
of~$X_0$ and return to $r=X_0^1$.  On the event $W_\pi(A)\le2$, deleting the
two wraparound arcs divides the tour order into two left-to-right paths.  Each
path contains exactly one gate from every gate column and at most one active
routing vertex from every routing column.  Hence, when these paths pass from
$X_{i-1}$ to~$X_i$, the policy chooses one entrance-to-exit transition for
each path.  If the first path enters in row~$a$ and leaves in column~$b$, any
other active routing vertex must fit the complementary cell $(3-a,3-b)$ on the
second path.

\paragraph{Stage-respecting policies.}
We first explain why an adaptive policy does not benefit from probing a
future stage.  Suppose that the policy probes a vertex in a column to the
right of~$X_j$ before probing either gate in~$X_j$.  If the probed vertex is
active, the current left-to-right path has passed~$X_j$.  Both gates in~$X_j$
remain unprobed and must occur later in the realized tour, requiring two
further left-to-right paths.  Together with the current path, this requires
at least three wraparound arcs.

In particular, probing a gate in~$X_k$ while an earlier gate column~$X_j$,
$j<k$, is still completely unprobed always forces a third wraparound arc,
because every gate is active.  A prematurely probed routing vertex might instead be
inactive.  Modify the policy by feeding its internal simulation the answer
``inactive'' without actually probing that routing vertex, and postpone the real
probe until its predecessor gate column has been reached.  On every
realization where the original policy uses only two wraparound arcs, every
such routing vertex is indeed inactive by the preceding paragraph.  The modified
policy therefore has the same active-vertex tour on that realization.  Hence,
if $\widehat\pi$ denotes the modified policy, then
\[
    \{A:W_\pi(A)\le2\}
    \subseteq
    \{A:W_{\widehat\pi}(A)\le2\}.
\]
Repeating this transformation lets us restrict attention to
\emph{stage-respecting} policies: while forming the first path, they visit
the stages in order and do not probe routing vertices of a stage before
reaching its predecessor gate column.
We now prove that every stage-respecting policy needs a third wraparound arc
with high probability.

\begin{lemma}[Adaptive cost of the layered-column instance]
\label{lem:layered-column-adaptive}
For every $L\ge1$ and $p,\varepsilon\in(0,1)$, the adaptive optimum
satisfies
\[
    \optadapt\bigl(\mathcal I^{\mathrm{LC}}(L,p,\varepsilon)\bigr)
    \ge
    3-\bigl(1-p^2(1-p)^2\bigr)^L.
\]
In particular, if $Lp^2(1-p)^2\to\infty$, then
\[
    \optadapt\bigl(\mathcal I^{\mathrm{LC}}(L,p,\varepsilon)\bigr)
    \ge3-o(1).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\emph{A bad pair in every fresh stage.}
Fix a deterministic stage-respecting policy; for a randomized policy, we
condition on its private seed.  Expose the routing columns in increasing
order.  Fix a history before stage~$i$ that is still compatible with two
wraparound arcs, and let the first path enter~$X_{i-1}$ through
$X_{i-1}^a$.  Conditional on this history, all four routing vertices in~$Y_i$ are
fresh independent Bernoulli-$p$ variables.

We now specify a bad pair $Q_i\subseteq Y_i$ before exposing~$Y_i$.  Follow
the policy counterfactually until it either probes a routing vertex of~$Y_i$ or
leaves through a gate of~$X_i$.  Here and in the continuation below, if the
policy first makes a move that is not part of the current left-to-right path,
then that path has already ended while both output gates remain unprobed; a
third path is necessary, and we choose~$Q_i$ arbitrarily.  If the policy
leaves through~$X_i$ first, let~$Q_i$ be any pair.
When exactly this pair is active, both active routing vertices occur later than the
first output gate and require two further left-to-right paths, so
$W_\pi(A)\ge3$.

Otherwise, let~$u$ be the first routing vertex of~$Y_i$ probed, and answer
counterfactually that~$u$ is active.  If~$u$ is not reachable from
$X_{i-1}^a$ using local arcs, choose any $v\in Y_i\setminus\{u\}$ and set
$Q_i:=\{u,v\}$.  On the exact-pair realization, visiting~$u$ ends the first
path before either gate of~$X_i$ has been probed.  The two output gates must
occur later, so a third path is necessary.

It remains that~$u$ is locally reachable.  Continue the counterfactual
execution from~$u$, answering each further routing-vertex probe in~$Y_i$ with
``inactive.''  If the policy probes another routing vertex~$v\in Y_i$ before an
output gate, stop at the first such~$v$ and set $Q_i:=\{u,v\}$.  On the
exact-pair realization, the policy follows this execution until~$v$, but now
both~$u$ and~$v$ are active.  They therefore occur in two different
left-to-right paths before either output gate has been probed.  After~$v$,
both output gates must still occur in the realized tour, while one
left-to-right path can contain at most one of them.  Thus $W_\pi(A)\ge3$.

The remaining possibility is that the policy first probes an output gate
$X_i^b$.  If the move from~$u$ to~$X_i^b$ is not local, the first path has
already ended before the other output gate and the second active routing vertex
have been probed, so any pair containing~$u$ is bad.  Otherwise the first
path has assigned~$u$ to the cell~$(a,b)$.  Let~$v$ be the unique other
routing vertex that allows this cell and set $Q_i:=\{u,v\}$.  Neither~$u$ nor~$v$
allows the complementary cell~$(3-a,3-b)$.  Hence, on the exact-pair
realization, the second path cannot cover the remaining active routing vertex, and
again $W_\pi(A)\ge3$.

\medskip
\noindent\emph{Product amplification.}
The pair~$Q_i$ depends only on the preceding history and these counterfactual
answers, not on the realization of~$Y_i$.  Therefore
\[
    \P\bigl(Y_i\cap A=Q_i\mid\mathcal F_{i-1}\bigr)
    =p^2(1-p)^2=:h,
\]
where~$\mathcal F_{i-1}$ includes the policy history and the complete
realizations of the preceding routing columns.  On histories no longer
compatible with two wraparound arcs, define~$Q_i$ arbitrarily.  Iterated
conditioning and independence now give
\[
    \P\bigl(W_\pi(A)\le2\bigr)
    \le
    \P\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^L\{Y_i\cap A\ne Q_i\}\right)
    \le(1-h)^L.
\]
Finally, $W_\pi(A)\ge2$ implies
\[
    \E[W_\pi(A)]
    \ge2+\P\bigl(W_\pi(A)\ge3\bigr)
    =3-\P\bigl(W_\pi(A)\le2\bigr)
    \ge3-(1-h)^L.
\]
Since the tour cost is at least $W_\pi(A)$, every adaptive policy~$\pi$
therefore satisfies
\[
    \cost(\pi)\ge3-(1-h)^L.
\]
Taking the infimum over all policies proves the first claim.  If
$Lh\to\infty$, then $(1-h)^L\le\e^{-Lh}=o(1)$ by
\cref{eq:bernoulli-product-bounds}, proving the second claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Layered-column lower bound]
\label{thm:layered-column-gap}
The clairvoyance gap for asymmetric \StochTSP{} is at least~$3/2$.
More precisely, there is a sequence of \StochTSP{} instances
$(\mathcal{I}_t^{\mathrm{LC}})_{t\ge2}$ such that
\[
    \liminf_{t\to\infty}
    \frac{\optadapt(\mathcal{I}_t^{\mathrm{LC}})}
         {\optpost(\mathcal{I}_t^{\mathrm{LC}})}
    \ge\frac32.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For an integer $t\ge2$, choose
\[
    L:=t^5,
    \qquad
    p:=t^{-2},
    \qquad
    \varepsilon:=t^{-10}=L^{-2},
\]
and define the resulting instance by
\[
    \mathcal{I}_t^{\mathrm{LC}}
    :=\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{LC}}(L,p,\varepsilon).
\]
Here $\varepsilon L=t^{-5}=o(1)$ and $Lp^3=t^{-1}=o(1)$, so
\cref{lem:layered-column-posterior} directly gives
\[
    \optpost(\mathcal{I}_t^{\mathrm{LC}})=2+o(1).
\]

On the adaptive side,
\[
    Lp^2(1-p)^2
    =t(1-t^{-2})^2
    \longrightarrow\infty.
\]
\cref{lem:layered-column-adaptive} directly gives
\[
    \optadapt(\mathcal{I}_t^{\mathrm{LC}})\ge3-o(1).
\]
Combining the two estimates yields
\[
    \liminf_{t\to\infty}
    \frac{\optadapt(\mathcal{I}_t^{\mathrm{LC}})}
         {\optpost(\mathcal{I}_t^{\mathrm{LC}})}
    \ge\frac32.
\]
\end{proof}

\chapter{Adaptive Policies for Asymmetric \StochTSP{}}
\label{ch:circulation}

In this chapter we focus on asymmetric \StochTSP{} and present an adaptive policy
with cost upper bound compared to $\optpost$. The cost upper bound implies an upper bound for clairvoyance gap.

\begin{theorem}[Adaptive policy cost upper bound]
\label{thm:asymmetric-clairvoyance-upper}
Every asymmetric \StochTSP{} instance with $n$ non-root vertices admits an
adaptive policy~$\pi$ satisfying
\[
    \cost(\pi)=O(n^{1/3})\optpost.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:asymmetric-clairvoyance-upper}
For asymmetric metrics,
\[
    \operatorname{CG}(n)=O(n^{1/3}).
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For every instance, $\optadapt\le\cost(\pi)$.  The claim follows from
\cref{thm:asymmetric-clairvoyance-upper} and the definition of the
clairvoyance gap.
\end{proof}

The theorem is immediate when $n\le1$, so throughout this chapter we assume
$n\ge2$. The problem is purely analytical so we make no claim about the run time
required to compute the resulting policy.  The theorem
improves the $O(\sqrt n)$ clairvoyance gap bound inherited from the asymmetric
obliviousness gap bound of Christalla, Puhlmann, and
Traub~\cite{Christalla2025}.%
\footnote{Christalla et al. use the term \emph{adaptivity gap} for
$\optprior/\optpost$, which we call the obliviousness gap.  Their grid
construction also admits an adaptive column-sweep policy of expected cost
$O(k\log k)$, matching the posterior upper bound used in their analysis.
Consequently, that
construction gives an $\Omega(n^{1/4}/\log n)$ lower bound for our adaptivity
gap as well.  The rectangular grid in \cref{ch:asym-adapt} strengthens this
lower bound.}

\section{From symmetric round trips to asymmetric chords}
\label{sec:asymmetric-backbone-motivation}

We begin with the sampled master route of Shmoys and
Talwar~\cite{Shmoys2008}.  The factor-three formulation below is made
explicit by Blauth et al.~\cite{Blauth2024}.  First suppose that the metric is
symmetric.  Independently include every non-root vertex~$v$ in a sample~$S$
with probability~$p_v$, and put $R:=S\cup\{r\}$.  Let~$T_R$ be an optimal
tour on~$R$.  For every vertex $u\notin R$, choose a nearest backbone vertex
$\mu_S(u)\in R$ and put $D_S(u):=d(u,\mu_S(u))$.  Joining~$u$ to
$\mu_S(u)$ by a doubled spoke and walking around the resulting structure
gives a master tour~$M_S$.  After the actual active set is realized, inactive
vertices are shortcut.  Conditional on~$S$, its expected cost is at most
\begin{equation}\label{eq:symmetric-master-route}
    \cost(M_S)
    \le \opt(S)
       +2\sum_{u\in V^\circ\setminus S}p_uD_S(u).
\end{equation}

The two terms in \cref{eq:symmetric-master-route} admit a cumulative charge to
the posterior optimum.  Since~$S$ has the same distribution as an activation
set,
\[
    \E_S[\opt(S)]=\optpost.
\]
Independence of the sample coordinates also gives
\begin{align*}
    &\E_S\!\left[
      \sum_{u\in V^\circ\setminus S}p_uD_S(u)
    \right] \\
    &\qquad\le
    \E_S\!\left[
      \sum_{u\in S}
      \min_{w\in(S\setminus\{u\})\cup\{r\}}d(u,w)
    \right]
    \le \E_S[\opt(S)].
\end{align*}
For the last inequality, charge the nearest-other-sample distance of every
$u\in S$ to the edge leaving~$u$ in an optimal tour on~$S\cup\{r\}$.  Thus
the expected backbone cost is~$\optpost$, while the cumulative expected cost
of leaving the backbone and returning to it is at most~$2\optpost$.
Averaging \cref{eq:symmetric-master-route} over~$S$ yields a master tour of
cost at most~$3\optpost$.  This factor-three statement is an existence result
because it uses an optimal tour on the sample.  Replacing that tour by a
$3/2$-approximate symmetric TSP tour gives instead a polynomial-time
randomized factor of~$7/2$.

The same-anchor detours can be expensive
in an asymmetric metric.  Consider the grid construction of Christalla et
al.~\cite[Section~3.1]{Christalla2025}: there are $k$ cyclic columns, each
containing $k$ vertices, and the metric is induced by unit-length arcs from
every vertex of one column to every vertex of the next.  Declare one grid
vertex to be the always-active root and give every other vertex activation
probability~$1/k$.  For a realization~$A$, put
\[
    M(A):=\max_j |(A\cup\{r\})\cap C_j|.
\]
The active vertices can be visited in at most~$M(A)$ complete passes through
the columns, so $\opt(A)\le kM(A)$.  The maximum of the $k$ column loads has
expectation $O(\log k)$, and hence $\optpost=O(k\log k)$.  An independent
backbone sample has the same law and therefore also admits a tour of expected
cost $O(k\log k)$.

On the other hand, if~$u$ is outside the sample and~$s$ is any sampled
backbone vertex, then
\[
    d(s,u)+d(u,s)\ge k.
\]
Moving from one column to another and back completes one revolution of the
grid; two distinct vertices in the same column require two revolutions.  The
expected number of vertices that are active but outside the sample is
$\Theta(k)$.  Therefore any strategy required to return to its departure
vertex after every active outside-vertex visit has expected detour cost
$\Omega(k^2)$, an $\Omega(k/\log k)$ loss relative to~$\optpost$.  An adaptive
probing order does not remove this loss under the same-anchor restriction.

This obstruction forces us to allow a visit to leave the backbone at one
vertex~$a$ and rejoin it at a different vertex~$b$.  We represent such a
visit by a two-arc detour $a\to i\to b$ and collapse it to the backbone chord
$a\to b$.  Different chords may create large incoming--outgoing imbalances,
so choosing individually cheap endpoints is not enough: the chords must form
a globally balanced structure.

First, \cref{sec:low-cost-circulation} constructs a fractional circulation
whose average cost is $O(\log n)\,\optpost$ and interprets it as a lower bound
on the posterior benchmark up to a logarithmic factor.  Next,
\cref{sec:detour-chord-assignment} couples the flow through each outside
vertex into fractional two-arc detours.
\Cref{sec:joint-circulation-rounding} jointly assigns their endpoints and
rounds the auxiliary backbone chords; every outcome has only
$O(\log^2 n)$ imbalance on each cyclic interval.  Only then do we reveal the
activations.  \Cref{sec:adaptive-probing-rare-vertices} turns the rounded
structure into an adaptive policy for rare vertices, and
\cref{sec:asymmetric-approximate-adaptive-policy} balances this policy against
a fixed tour for the remaining vertices.  Together these sections prove
\cref{thm:asymmetric-clairvoyance-upper}.

\section{A circulation lower bound for the posterior optimum}
\label{sec:low-cost-circulation}

This section constructs a fractional surrogate for the posterior benchmark.
Fix a sampled backbone~$R$ and let~$i$ be a vertex outside it.  If~$i$ is
probed and found active, the route must leave a backbone vertex~$a$, visit
$i$, and rejoin the backbone at a vertex~$b$.  We represent this possible
visit by the two-arc detour $a\to i\to b$ and its collapsed backbone chord
$a\to b$.  Since~$i$ is active with probability~$p_i$, the fractional model
sends $p_i$ units of flow through~$i$.  Flow conservation enforces the global
balance needed to combine all these detours with the backbone tour.

Following the sampled-backbone framework of Shmoys and
Talwar~\cite{Shmoys2008}, let $S\subseteq V^\circ$ be an auxiliary random
set, independent of the actual activation set, obtained by including each
vertex~$v$ independently with probability~$p_v$.  Set
\[
    R:=S\cup\{r\}
    \qquad\text{and}\qquad
    U:=V^\circ\setminus S
\]
and call~$R$ the \emph{backbone}; the vertices in~$U$ are the \emph{outside
vertices}.

For a nonnegative vector $z=(z_{uv})_{u,v\in V}$, interpret~$z_{uv}$ as the
amount of flow sent from~$u$ to~$v$.  The total outgoing and incoming flow at
a vertex~$w$ are, respectively,
\[
    z(\delta^+(w)):=\sum_{v\in V} z_{wv},
    \qquad
    z(\delta^-(w)):=\sum_{u\in V} z_{uw}.
\]

For a fixed choice of~$S$, consider the following minimum-cost fractional
circulation.  Because vertex~$i$ is active with probability~$p_i$, the
relaxation sends~$p_i$ units of flow through each outside vertex~$i\in U$:
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\min\quad
    &\sum_{u,v\in V}d(u,v)z_{uv}\\
\text{subject to}\quad
    &z(\delta^+(w))=z(\delta^-(w))
        &&\forall w\in V,\\
    &z(\delta^+(i))=z(\delta^-(i))=p_i
        &&\forall i\in U,\\
    &z_{ij}=0
        &&\forall i,j\in U,\\
    &z_{uv}\ge0
        &&\forall u,v\in V.
\end{aligned}
\tag{$\mathrm{CIRC}(S)$}\label{eq:backbone-circulation-lp}
\end{equation*}
For a feasible vector~$z$, write
\[
    \cost(z):=\sum_{u,v\in V}d(u,v)z_{uv}.
\]
Let $\operatorname{circ}(S)$ denote the optimum value of
$\mathrm{CIRC}(S)$.
The constraint $z_{ij}=0, \forall i,j\in U$ ensures that every flow arc entering or
leaving an outside vertex has an endpoint in the backbone.  Since the
incoming and outgoing totals at~$i$ are both~$p_i$, the flow through~$i$ can
be paired into fractional two-arc detours $a\to i\to b$ with $a,b\in R$.

\begin{remark}[Why endpoint balance is necessary]
Fix $m\ge3$ and $\varepsilon\in(0,1]$.  Let~$r$ and~$q$ be two always-active
vertices, and introduce $m^2$ stochastic vertices
$u_1,\ldots,u_{m^2}$, each active with probability~$1/m$.  Let~$d$ be the
shortest-path metric of the auxiliary graph containing the following arcs:
\[
\begin{aligned}
    r\to u_i \text{ and } u_i\to q
        &\quad\text{have length }\varepsilon,\\
    u_i\to u_j
        &\quad\text{has length }2m &&(i\ne j),\\
    q\to r
        &\quad\text{has length }m^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
The graph is strongly connected and its shortest-path metric is asymmetric.
Every posterior tour costs at least~$m^2$, because it must leave~$q$.
Conversely, if~$X$ stochastic vertices are active, visiting them between~$r$
and~$q$ and then returning to~$r$ costs at most
\[
    m^2+2\varepsilon+2mX.
\]
Since $\E[X]=m$, it follows that $\optpost=\Theta(m^2)$.

Now fix the two-vertex backbone~$\{r,q\}$.  Assigning every stochastic vertex
the individually cheapest detour $r\to u_i\to q$ has
activation-weighted cost
\[
    \sum_{i=1}^{m^2}\frac1m
      \bigl(d(r,u_i)+d(u_i,q)\bigr)
    =2\varepsilon m.
\]
These detours nevertheless send a total of~$m$ units from~$r$ to~$q$.
Any balanced augmentation that keeps these endpoint choices must send~$m$
units back from~$q$ to~$r$, at cost
\[
    m\,d(q,r)=m^3.
\]
Thus individually cheap endpoint assignments may require an unbounded-factor
repair before they form a traversable structure.  Taking~$\varepsilon$ small
makes the raw detour cost negligible; the obstruction is the imbalance, not
the price of the individual detours.
\end{remark}

We next bound the circulation cost on average over the sampled backbone by giving
a solution $z^S$ to $\mathrm{CIRC}(S)$ for every choice of $S$ and bounding its expected cost
over the random choice of~$S$.

For an induced subinstance on $W\subseteq V^\circ$, write
$\optpost(W):=\E[\opt(A\cap W)]$.
Let $\widetilde A\subseteq V^\circ$ be an auxiliary activation sample,
independent of~$S$, using the same probabilities
$(p_v)_{v\in V^\circ}$, and put $C:=\widetilde A\cup S$.  For every possible
set~$C$, fix an optimal rooted tour~$T_C$.  Cutting this tour $T_C$ at the vertices
of~$R:=S\cup\{r\}$ produces segments in tour order
\[
    P_j=(s_j,u_{j,1},\ldots,u_{j,k_j},s_{j+1}),
\]
where $s_j,s_{j+1}\in R$ and every internal vertex belongs to
$\widetilde A\setminus S$.  Indices on the vertices of~$R$ are cyclic.  Define
\[
    M(\widetilde A,S):=\max_j k_j.
\]

For every segment~$P_j$, send one unit of flow along
$s_j\to u_{j,\ell}\to s_{j+1}$ for each $1\le\ell\le k_j$, and send
$M(\widetilde A,S)-k_j$ units directly from $s_j$ to~$s_{j+1}$.  Denote the
resulting flow by~$z^{\widetilde A,S}$.  Every outside vertex in
$\widetilde A\setminus S$ has one unit of
incoming and outgoing flow.  Moreover, every backbone vertex has
$M(\widetilde A,S)$ units coming from the preceding segment and the same
amount leaving to the next segment, so $z^{\widetilde A,S}$ is a circulation.

By triangle inequality, the cost of each detour
$s_j\to u_{j,\ell}\to s_{j+1}$, as well as the arc
$s_j\to s_{j+1}$, is at most the length of~$P_j$.  Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:realization-circulation-cost}
    \cost(z^{\widetilde A,S})
    \le M(\widetilde A,S)\cost(T_C)
    =M(\widetilde A,S)\opt(C).
\end{equation}

For a fixed backbone~$S$, averaging this construction over~$\widetilde A$
gives
\[
    z^S:=\E_{\widetilde A}[z^{\widetilde A,S}].
\]
If $i\in U$, then $i$ receives one unit of flow exactly when
$i\in\widetilde A$.
Hence its expected incoming and outgoing flow is~$p_i$.  All other
constraints in~\cref{eq:backbone-circulation-lp} are preserved under
averaging, so $z^S$ is feasible for $\mathrm{CIRC}(S)$.

We bound the expected cost of this construction by bounding the longest segment
between consecutive backbone vertices.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:circulation-longest-run}
For every integer $t\ge1$ and every fixed set~$C$ with
$\P(\widetilde A\cup S=C)>0$,
\[
    \P\bigl(M(\widetilde A,S)\ge t\mid \widetilde A\cup S=C\bigr)
    \le n2^{-t}.
\]
In particular,
\[
    \E\bigl[M(\widetilde A,S)\mid \widetilde A\cup S=C\bigr]
    =O(\log n).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Condition on $\widetilde A\cup S=C$.  The tour~$T_C$ is fixed by~$C$.  For each
$v\in C$, the conditional probability that it is active but outside the
backbone is
\[
    \P(v\in\widetilde A\setminus S\mid v\in\widetilde A\cup S)
    =
    \frac{p_v(1-p_v)}{1-(1-p_v)^2}
    =
    \frac{1-p_v}{2-p_v}
    \le\frac12.
\]
A run of length~$t$ can start in at most~$n$ positions of the
fixed cyclic tour.  The union bound~\cref{eq:union-bound} therefore gives the
claimed tail estimate.

Let $L:=\lceil\log_2 n\rceil+1$.  The tail-sum formula
\cref{eq:tail-sum} yields
\[
    \E[M(\widetilde A,S)\mid \widetilde A\cup S=C]
    \le
    L+\sum_{t>L}n2^{-t}
    =O(\log n). \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

We retain the distribution over backbones for now; an outcome will be selected
only after the rounded structure has been analyzed.

\begin{theorem}[Average circulation lower bound]
\label{thm:average-circulation-lower-bound}
Let $W\subseteq V^\circ$, and apply the preceding construction to the
subinstance induced by~$W$.  If $S\subseteq W$ is sampled with the activation
probabilities and $z^S$ is the resulting feasible circulation, then
\[
    \E_S[\opt(S)]=\optpost(W).
\]
Moreover,
\[
    \E_S[\operatorname{circ}(S)]
    \le \E_S[\cost(z^S)]
    =O(\log n)\,\optpost(W).
\]
Equivalently,
\[
    \optpost(W)
    =\Omega\!\left(
      \frac{\E_S[\operatorname{circ}(S)]}{\log n}
    \right).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $B:=\optpost(W)$, let $\widetilde A\subseteq W$ be an independent
auxiliary activation sample, and write $C:=\widetilde A\cup S$.  Since~$S$
has the same distribution as~$\widetilde A$,
\[
    \E_S[\opt(S)]=B.
\]
By linearity of cost, \cref{eq:realization-circulation-cost}, and
\cref{lem:circulation-longest-run}, whose implied constant is uniform in~$C$,
\begin{align*}
    \E_S[\cost(z^S)]
    &=\E_{\widetilde A,S}[\cost(z^{\widetilde A,S})]\\
    &\le \E_{\widetilde A,S}
       [M(\widetilde A,S)\opt(\widetilde A\cup S)]\\
    &=\E_C\!\left[
        \opt(C)\E[M(\widetilde A,S)\mid \widetilde A\cup S=C]
      \right]\\
    &= O(\log n)\,
       \E_{\widetilde A,S}[\opt(\widetilde A\cup S)].
\end{align*}
Concatenating optimal tours for~$\widetilde A$ and~$S$ at the root and
shortcutting gives
\[
    \opt(\widetilde A\cup S)
    \le \opt(\widetilde A)+\opt(S).
\]
Both terms on the right have expectation~$B$, so
$\E_S[\cost(z^S)]=O(\log n)B$.
Finally, $z^S$ is feasible for $\mathrm{CIRC}(S)$, so
$\operatorname{circ}(S)\le\cost(z^S)$ for every~$S$.
\end{proof}

The circulation is useful not merely because it is cheap: its balance
constraints retain the structure needed to assign detours to backbone chords
and round them in the next two sections.

\subsection{A boosted backbone}
\label{sec:boosted-backbone}

For the sharper rounding and the offline construction below, we need two
additional properties: bounded circulation degree at backbone vertices and
small activation probabilities outside the backbone.  Both can be obtained
by boosting the sampling probabilities.

\begin{proposition}[Boosted backbone]\label{prop:boosted-backbone}
There are absolute constants $c,D_0,K_0>0$ such that the following holds.
Put
\[
    \lambda:=\left\lceil c\log n\right\rceil
    \qquad\text{and}\qquad
    \widehat p_v:=\min\{1,\lambda p_v\}.
\]
There exist a set~$S\subseteq V^\circ$, a tour~$T_R$ on
$R:=S\cup\{r\}$, and a feasible solution~$z$ to
$\mathrm{CIRC}(S)$ such that
\begin{align}
    \cost(T_R)+\cost(z)
    &\le K_0\log n\optpost,
    \label{eq:boosted-cost}\\
    z(\delta^+(s))=z(\delta^-(s))
    &\le D_0\log n
    \qquad\forall s\in R,
    \label{eq:boosted-degree}\\
    p_i&<\frac1\lambda
    \qquad\forall i\in V^\circ\setminus S.
    \label{eq:boosted-outside-probability}
\end{align}
The set~$S$ may be chosen from the product distribution with inclusion
probabilities~$(\widehat p_v)_{v\in V^\circ}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
First consider one ordinary sample~$B$, in which each non-root vertex~$v$ is included
with probability~$p_v$, and the averaged witness~$z^B$ constructed above.
Put $Z(B):=\E_A[M(A,B)\mid B]$.  By
\cref{lem:circulation-longest-run},
\[
    \E_B[Z(B)]=O(\log n).
\]
The proof of \cref{thm:average-circulation-lower-bound} also gives
\[
    \E_B[\cost(z^B)]
    =O(\log n)\optpost.
\]
By Markov's inequality~\cref{eq:markov}, after choosing absolute constants
$D_0$ and~$K_0$
large enough, both
\[
    Z(B)\le D_0\log n
    \quad\text{and}\quad
    \cost(z^B)\le K_0\log n\optpost
\]
hold with probability at least~$3/4$.  Call such a sample \emph{good}.

Take $\lambda$ independent ordinary samples
$B^1,\ldots,B^\lambda$ and let $S_0:=\bigcup_jB^j$.  The probability that
none is good is at most $4^{-\lambda}$.  Moreover,
\[
    \P(v\in S_0)=1-(1-p_v)^\lambda
    \le\min\{1,\lambda p_v\}=\widehat p_v.
\]
The inequality is the left-hand bound in
\cref{eq:bernoulli-product-bounds} applied to $\lambda$ copies of~$p_v$.
Thus, by \cref{lem:monotone-product-coupling}, a product sample~$S$ with
marginals~$\widehat p_v$ can be coupled with~$S_0$ so that $S_0\subseteq S$.

Suppose $B^j$ is good.  The same vector~$z^{B^j}$ remains feasible when the
backbone is enlarged from~$B^j$ to~$S$.  Indeed, every vertex still outside
$S$ was outside $B^j$ and retains incoming and outgoing degree~$p_v$.
Every new backbone vertex $s\in S\setminus B^j$ previously had degree
$p_s\le1$, while the old backbone vertices have degree at most
$D_0\log n$.  The support restriction can only become weaker when the
outside set shrinks.  Hence~\cref{eq:boosted-degree} holds and the circulation
cost is unchanged.

It remains to control the backbone tour.  After sampling~$S$, independently
give each $v\in S$ a uniform label in~$[\lambda]$, and let $S_j$ contain the
vertices carrying label~$j$.  The sets~$S_j$ partition~$S$, and, for each
fixed~$j$, vertex memberships are independent with
\[
    \P(v\in S_j)=\frac{\widehat p_v}{\lambda}
    =\min\left\{\frac1\lambda,p_v\right\}
    \le p_v.
\]
By \cref{lem:monotone-product-coupling} and the monotonicity of~$\opt(\cdot)$,
$\E[\opt(S_j)]\le\optpost$.  Concatenating tours for
the sets~$S_j$ at the root and shortcutting gives
\[
    \opt(S)\le\sum_{j=1}^{\lambda}\opt(S_j),
    \qquad
    \E[\opt(S)]\le\lambda\optpost.
\]
Markov's inequality~\cref{eq:markov} therefore gives
$\opt(S)\le4\lambda\optpost$ with probability at least~$3/4$.
For a sufficiently large choice of~$c$, this event and the existence of a
good~$B^j$ have positive joint probability.  Fix an outcome in their
intersection and let~$T_R$ be an optimal tour on~$R$.  Enlarging~$K_0$ absorbs
the term $4\lambda\optpost$ and proves~\cref{eq:boosted-cost}.

Finally, if $i\notin S$, then $\widehat p_i<1$.  By the definition of~$\widehat p_i$, this
implies $\lambda p_i<1$, which is
\cref{eq:boosted-outside-probability}.
\end{proof}

\section{Endpoint rounding with interval discrepancy}
\label{sec:circulation-rounding}

Fix a backbone~$R=S\cup\{r\}$ and a feasible circulation~$z$.  Since there
are no arcs between outside vertices, the flow through each $i\in U$ can be
coupled into paths with both endpoints in~$R$.  More precisely, there are
nonnegative variables
\[
    x_{i,ab}
    \qquad(i\in U,\ a,b\in R)
\]
such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coupled-marginals}
    \sum_{b\in R}x_{i,ab}=z_{ai},
    \qquad
    \sum_{a\in R}x_{i,ab}=z_{ib}.
\end{equation}
Such a coupling exists because both marginal sums equal~$p_i$; it can be
obtained, for example, by greedily matching the incoming and outgoing flow
at~$i$.  Put $y_{ab}:=z_{ab}$ for $a,b\in R$.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coupled-outside-constraint}
    \sum_{a,b\in R}x_{i,ab}=p_i
    \qquad\forall i\in U,
\end{equation}
and the coupling preserves cost:
\begin{align}
    &\sum_{i\in U}\sum_{a,b\in R}
       \bigl(d(a,i)+d(i,b)\bigr)x_{i,ab}
      +\sum_{a,b\in R}d(a,b)y_{ab}
      \notag\\
    &\hspace{35mm}=
      \sum_{u,v\in V}d(u,v)z_{uv}.
    \label{eq:coupled-cost}
\end{align}

Let
\[
    T_R=(s_0,s_1,\ldots,s_{m-1},s_0)
\]
be a tour on the backbone.  A \emph{cyclic interval} is a consecutive set of
vertices in this cyclic order.  For $I\subseteq R$ and $a,b\in R$, define
\[
    \chi_I(a,b):=\1_{\{a\in I\}}-\1_{\{b\in I\}},
\]
and define the weighted outgoing-minus-incoming imbalance of~$I$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:interval-imbalance}
    \Delta_{x,y}(I)
    :=
    \sum_{i\in U}\sum_{a,b\in R}\chi_I(a,b)x_{i,ab}
    +\sum_{a,b\in R}\chi_I(a,b)y_{ab}.
\end{equation}
Summing the original flow-conservation equations over the backbone vertices
in~$I$ shows that
\[
    \Delta_{x,y}(I)=0
    \qquad\forall I\subseteq R.
\]

The goal is to replace every $x_{i,ab}$ by either~$0$ or~$p_i$.  By
\cref{eq:coupled-outside-constraint}, this assigns every outside vertex of
positive probability to exactly one ordered pair~$(a,b)$ of backbone
vertices.  Preserving all subset-balance constraints while doing this need not
leave a direction in which to move.  We instead preserve only a laminar
family of interval constraints and discard a constraint once few fractional
variables still touch it.

\begin{lemma}[Endpoint rounding]\label{lem:circulation-endpoint-rounding}
Let $p_{\max}:=\max_{i\in U}p_i$, with $p_{\max}:=0$ when $U=\emptyset$.
There is a vector~$\widehat x$ satisfying
\[
    \widehat x_{i,ab}\in\{0,p_i\}
    \qquad\text{and}\qquad
    \sum_{a,b\in R}\widehat x_{i,ab}=p_i
    \quad\forall i\in U
\]
such that
\begin{align}
    \sum_{i\in U}\sum_{a,b\in R}
       \bigl(d(a,i)+d(i,b)\bigr)\widehat x_{i,ab}
    &\le
    \sum_{i\in U}\sum_{a,b\in R}
       \bigl(d(a,i)+d(i,b)\bigr)x_{i,ab},
    \label{eq:rounding-cost}\\
    |\Delta_{\widehat x,y}(I)|
    &\le O\bigl(p_{\max}\log^2 n\bigr).
    \label{eq:rounding-interval-balance}
\end{align}
The second inequality holds for every cyclic interval~$I$ of~$T_R$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The claim is immediate when $|R|=1$, so assume $m:=|R|\ge2$.  Pad the linear
order $s_0,\ldots,s_{m-1}$ with empty positions to the next power of two and
let $\mathfrak D$ be the standard dyadic interval family represented by the
nodes of the resulting binary interval tree.  Put
\[
    h:=\lceil\log_2 m\rceil,
    \qquad
    L:=8(h+1).
\]
Each backbone position belongs to at most $h+1$ members of~$\mathfrak D$.
Consequently, a variable~$x_{i,ab}$ has nonzero coefficient in at most
$2(h+1)$ of the constraints
\[
    \Delta_{x,y}(D)=0,
    \qquad D\in\mathfrak D.
\]

Call a variable \emph{unfixed} when $0<x_{i,ab}<p_i$ and \emph{fixed} when
$x_{i,ab}\in\{0,p_i\}$.  Let~$F$ be the set of unfixed variables.  Throughout
the procedure we preserve
\cref{eq:coupled-outside-constraint}.  We also preserve a dyadic interval
constraint as long as it touches at least~$L$ unfixed variables; once fewer than
$L$ unfixed variables touch it, we discard it permanently.

Let $c_1$ be the number of outside-vertex constraints that contain an unfixed
variable.  No such constraint can contain exactly one unfixed variable: after
all other variables in the constraint have values in~$\{0,p_i\}$, the equality with
right-hand side~$p_i$ would force the remaining variable to an endpoint as
well.  Hence
\[
    c_1\le\frac{|F|}{2}.
\]
Let $c_2$ be the number of currently preserved dyadic constraints.  Counting
incidences between these constraints and the unfixed variables gives
\[
    c_2L
    \le 2(h+1)|F|,
\]
and therefore $c_2\le |F|/4$.  Thus
\[
    c_1+c_2<|F|.
\]
The homogeneous system associated with the preserved equality constraints has a
nonzero direction supported on~$F$.

Move in both signs of this direction until an unfixed variable first reaches
either~$0$ or its upper bound~$p_i$.  The current point is a convex
combination of the two resulting endpoints.  Since the objective in
\cref{eq:rounding-cost} is linear, at least one endpoint has cost no larger
than the current point; choose such an endpoint.  This preserves all current
constraints, never increases cost, and fixes at least one additional variable.
Repeating the step terminates with every variable in
$\{0,p_i\}$, while all outside-vertex constraints still hold.  This proves
\cref{eq:rounding-cost} and the first displayed properties of~$\widehat x$.

Consider a dyadic interval constraint at the moment it is discarded.  Its imbalance is
still zero, and fewer than~$L$ of its variables are unfixed.  Only those
variables can subsequently change.  Each changes in absolute value by at
most $p_i\le p_{\max}$ and has coefficient in~$\{-1,0,1\}$.  It follows that
at the end of the procedure,
\[
    |\Delta_{\widehat x,y}(D)|\le Lp_{\max}
    \qquad\forall D\in\mathfrak D.
\]
Every linear interval has a canonical decomposition into $O(h+1)$ disjoint
dyadic intervals.  A cyclic interval is the union of at most two linear
intervals, so it has a decomposition into $O(h+1)$ members of~$\mathfrak D$.
The imbalance is additive over disjoint unions.  Therefore
\[
    |\Delta_{\widehat x,y}(I)|
    \le O((h+1)Lp_{\max})
    =O\bigl(p_{\max}\log^2 n\bigr)
\]
for every cyclic interval~$I$, proving
\cref{eq:rounding-interval-balance}.
\end{proof}

Combining the low-cost construction and endpoint rounding gives the first
structural result of the chapter.

\begin{theorem}[Backbone circulation and rounding]
\label{thm:backbone-circulation-rounding}
For every \StochTSP{} instance on an asymmetric metric, there exist a backbone
$R=S\cup\{r\}$, a tour
$T_R=(s_0,\ldots,s_{m-1},s_0)$, nonnegative backbone variables
$(y_{ab})_{a,b\in R}$, and, for every
$i\in V^\circ\setminus S$, an
ordered pair $(a_i,b_i)\in R\times R$ such that
\begin{align}
    \cost(T_R)
    +\sum_{a,b\in R}d(a,b)y_{ab}
    +\sum_{i\in V^\circ\setminus S}
       p_i\bigl(d(a_i,i)+d(i,b_i)\bigr)
    \le O(\log n)\optpost.
    \label{eq:partial-circulation-total-cost}
\end{align}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rounded-backbone-degree}
    \sum_{b\in R}y_{ab}\le O(\log n),
    \qquad
    \sum_{b\in R}y_{ba}\le O(\log n)
    \qquad\forall a\in R,
\end{equation}
and every outside vertex satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rounded-small-probability}
    p_i\le O\left(\frac1{\log n}\right).
\end{equation}
If $\widehat x_{i,a_i b_i}:=p_i$ and all other $\widehat x$-variables are
zero, then for every cyclic interval~$I$ of~$T_R$,
\[
    |\Delta_{\widehat x,y}(I)|
    \le O(\log n).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Choose $S$ and a feasible circulation~$z$ as in
\cref{prop:boosted-backbone}.  Couple the flow through each outside
vertex according to~\cref{eq:coupled-marginals}, and apply
\cref{lem:circulation-endpoint-rounding}.  The coupling preserves the
circulation cost by~\cref{eq:coupled-cost}, and the rounding does not
increase it by~\cref{eq:rounding-cost}.  Thus
\cref{eq:partial-circulation-total-cost} follows from
\cref{eq:boosted-cost}.  The backbone variables~$y$ form a subvector of~$z$,
so~\cref{eq:rounded-backbone-degree} follows from
\cref{eq:boosted-degree}; \cref{eq:rounded-small-probability} is
\cref{eq:boosted-outside-probability}.  Finally,
$p_{\max}=O(1/\log n)$, and hence
\cref{eq:rounding-interval-balance} improves to
$O(\log n)$.
\end{proof}

\section{An offline bounded-multiplicity closed walk}
\label{sec:offline-backbone-walk}

We now use the rounded structure after the active set has been revealed.
The construction in this section is therefore offline.  Its main tool is the
following classical form of Hoffman's circulation theorem~\cite{Hoffman1960}.

\begin{theorem}[Hoffman's circulation theorem]\label{thm:hoffman-circulation}
Let $G=(W,E)$ be a multigraph with vertex set~$W$ and arc multiset~$E$, and let
$\ell,u:E\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}$ satisfy $\ell_e\le u_e$ for every arc~$e$.
There is a circulation~$g$ with
\[
    \ell_e\le g_e\le u_e
    \qquad\forall e\in E
\]
if and only if, for every $X\subseteq W$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hoffman-condition}
    \ell(\delta^-(X))\le u(\delta^+(X)).
\end{equation}
If $\ell$ and~$u$ are integral, then~$g$ may be chosen integral.
\end{theorem}

Fix the objects supplied by
\cref{thm:backbone-circulation-rounding}.  For an activation
set~$A$, every active outside vertex $i\in A\cap U$ supplies the chord
\[
    e_i=(a_i,b_i)
\]
on~$R$; after constructing a circulation on these collapsed chords, we will
expand $e_i$ back to the path $a_i\to i\to b_i$.  Parallel chords are kept as
distinct arcs.

For $X\subseteq R$, write
\begin{align*}
    F_A^-(X)
    &:=
    \bigl|\{i\in A\cap U:a_i\notin X,\ b_i\in X\}\bigr|,\\
    F_A^+(X)
    &:=
    \bigl|\{i\in A\cap U:a_i\in X,\ b_i\notin X\}\bigr|,
\end{align*}
and let their expectations be
\begin{align*}
    \mu^-(X)
    &:=
    \sum_{\substack{i\in U\\a_i\notin X,\ b_i\in X}}p_i,\\
    \mu^+(X)
    &:=
    \sum_{\substack{i\in U\\a_i\in X,\ b_i\notin X}}p_i.
\end{align*}
Also put
\[
    y^+(X):=\sum_{\substack{a\in X\\b\notin X}}y_{ab},
    \qquad
    y^-(X):=\sum_{\substack{a\notin X\\b\in X}}y_{ab}.
\]

Let $C_R$ denote the backbone cycle in the tour order given by~$T_R$, and define
\[
    \iota(X):=|\delta_{C_R}^+(X)|.
\]
For a nonempty proper subset~$X$, this is the number of cyclic intervals in
the decomposition of~$X$; it also equals
$|\delta_{C_R}^-(X)|$.  Set $\iota(\emptyset)=\iota(R)=0$.

\begin{lemma}[Uniform cut concentration]\label{lem:offline-cut-concentration}
There is an absolute constant $C_0$ such that, with probability at least
$1-n^{-5}$ over the activation set~$A$, every $X\subseteq R$ satisfies
\begin{align}
    F_A^-(X)
    &\le2\mu^-(X)+C_0\iota(X)\log n,
    \label{eq:cut-concentration-in}\\
    \mu^+(X)
    &\le2F_A^+(X)+C_0\iota(X)\log n.
    \label{eq:cut-concentration-out}
\end{align}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix $X$ with $k:=\iota(X)\ge1$.  The variable $F_A^-(X)$ is a sum of independent
Bernoulli variables with mean~$\mu^-(X)$.  The upper-tail bound in
\cref{eq:chernoff-useful} gives, for every $t\ge0$,
\[
    \P\bigl(F_A^-(X)>2\mu^-(X)+t\bigr)\le\e^{-t/3}.
\]
For the other direction, if $\mu^+(X)\le t$, then
\cref{eq:cut-concentration-out} holds automatically with~$t$ in place of
its additive term.  If $\mu^+(X)>t$, a violation implies
$F_A^+(X)<\mu^+(X)/2$, whose probability is at most, by the lower-tail bound
in~\cref{eq:chernoff-useful},
\[
    \e^{-\mu^+(X)/8}\le\e^{-t/8}.
\]

There are at most $(2n)^{2k}$ subsets of a cyclic order having
$k$ interval components: it is enough to choose their $2k$ boundary
positions.  Set $t=C_0k\log n$, choose $C_0$ sufficiently large, and apply
the union bound~\cref{eq:union-bound} over all $k\ge1$ and both inequalities.
The resulting failure
probability is at most $n^{-5}$.  The two sets with $k=0$ satisfy both
inequalities trivially.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Offline bounded-multiplicity walk]
\label{thm:offline-backbone-walk}
Let $A$ be an independent activation set.  With probability at least
$1-n^{-5}$, there is an offline closed walk~$W_A$ with the following
properties:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item for every active outside vertex $i\in A\cap U$, the walk traverses
    both arcs of $a_i\to i\to b_i$ between one and four times;
    \item every backbone edge is traversed at least once and at most
    $O(\log n)$ times; and
    \item every auxiliary backbone chord $a\to b$ with $y_{ab}>0$ is traversed
    at most $O(\log n)$ times.
\end{enumerate}
Thus every active outside chord is used at least once and every edge used by
the offline walk has multiplicity $O(\log n)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix a realization satisfying
\cref{lem:offline-cut-concentration}.  By
\cref{thm:backbone-circulation-rounding}, there is an absolute constant
$C_\Delta$ such that every cyclic interval~$I$ satisfies
\[
    |\Delta_{\widehat x,y}(I)|
    \le C_\Delta\log n.
\]
If $X$ is the disjoint union of $\iota(X)$ cyclic intervals, additivity of the
imbalance gives
\[
    \bigl|
       \mu^+(X)-\mu^-(X)+y^+(X)-y^-(X)
    \bigr|
    \le C_\Delta\iota(X)\log n.
\]
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mean-cut-comparison}
    \mu^-(X)
    \le\mu^+(X)+y^+(X)+C_\Delta\iota(X)\log n.
\end{equation}
Combining
\cref{eq:cut-concentration-in,eq:mean-cut-comparison,eq:cut-concentration-out}
yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:realized-cut-comparison}
    F_A^-(X)
    \le
    4F_A^+(X)+2y^+(X)+
    (2C_\Delta+3C_0)\iota(X)\log n
    \qquad\forall X\subseteq R.
\end{equation}

We apply \cref{thm:hoffman-circulation} to a multigraph with vertex set~$R$
and three types of arcs, with the following lower and
upper bounds:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item each active outside chord~$e_i=(a_i,b_i)$ has
    $\ell_{e_i}=1$ and $u_{e_i}=4$;
    \item each auxiliary backbone chord $a\to b$ with $y_{ab}>0$ has
    $\ell_{ab}=0$ and $u_{ab}=\lceil2y_{ab}\rceil$; and
    \item each backbone edge of~$C_R$ has lower bound~$1$ and
    upper bound
    \[
        K:=
        \left\lceil
        (2C_\Delta+3C_0)\log n
        \right\rceil+1.
    \]
\end{enumerate}
Coincident arcs of different types are treated as parallel arcs.

For every $X\subseteq R$, the total lower bound on arcs entering~$X$ is
\[
    \ell(\delta^-(X))=F_A^-(X)+\iota(X).
\]
The total upper bound on arcs leaving~$X$ satisfies
\[
    u(\delta^+(X))
    \ge4F_A^+(X)+2y^+(X)+K\iota(X).
\]
\Cref{eq:realized-cut-comparison} and the definition of~$K$
therefore imply Hoffman's condition~\cref{eq:hoffman-condition}.  All bounds
are integral, so there is an integral circulation~$g$ within them.

Every auxiliary backbone chord has
\[
    g_{ab}\le\lceil2y_{ab}\rceil
    \le 1+2D_0\log n
\]
by~\cref{eq:rounded-backbone-degree}.  The multiplicity of every backbone
edge is at most~$K=O(\log n)$, while every active outside chord has multiplicity
between one and four.

Finally, expand every chord~$e_i$ carrying $g_{e_i}$ units into
$g_{e_i}$ copies of the path $a_i\to i\to b_i$.  The resulting arc
multigraph is balanced.  Its support is strongly connected: it contains the
whole backbone cycle in tour order, and every active outside vertex has a path
from and back to that cycle.  It therefore has an Euler tour, which is the
claimed closed walk~$W_A$.
\end{proof}

\section{Assigning detours to backbone chords}
\label{sec:detour-chord-assignment}

The circulation specifies how much flow enters and leaves each outside
vertex, but it does not yet pair an incoming arc with an outgoing arc.  We now
couple these marginals.  Each pair represents a fractional two-arc detour
$a\to i\to b$ and its collapsed backbone chord~$a\to b$.  The coupling
preserves both cost and global flow balance.

Fix an induced subinstance on $W\subseteq V^\circ$ and one possible
realization~$S\subseteq W$ of the independent backbone sample.  Apply the
construction of \cref{sec:low-cost-circulation} within~$W$, set
\[
    R:=S\cup\{r\},
    \qquad U:=W\setminus S,
    \qquad z:=z^S,
\]
and let~$T_R$ be an optimal tour on~$R$.

Because $z$ has no arcs between two outside vertices, its incoming and
outgoing flow at each $i\in U$ can be coupled into variables
\[
    x_{i,ab}\ge0
    \qquad(a,b\in R)
\]
such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rounding-coupling}
    \sum_{b\in R}x_{i,ab}=z_{ai},
    \qquad
    \sum_{a\in R}x_{i,ab}=z_{ib},
    \qquad
    \sum_{a,b\in R}x_{i,ab}=p_i.
\end{equation}
Let $y_{ab}:=z_{ab}$ for $a,b\in R$.  The coupling preserves cost:
\begin{align}
    &\sum_{i\in U}\sum_{a,b\in R}
       \bigl(d(a,i)+d(i,b)\bigr)x_{i,ab}
      +\sum_{a,b\in R}d(a,b)y_{ab}
      =\cost(z).
    \label{eq:rounding-coupling-cost}
\end{align}

Write $T_R=(s_0,s_1,\ldots,s_{m-1},s_0)$.  For a cyclic interval~$I$ in
this order and a pair $(a,b)\in R\times R$, define
\[
    \chi_I(a,b):=\1_{\{a\in I\}}-\1_{\{b\in I\}}.
\]
For nonnegative vectors~$\xi=(\xi_{i,ab})$ and
$\eta=(\eta_{ab})$, define the outgoing-minus-incoming imbalance of~$I$ by
\[
    \Delta_{\xi,\eta}(I)
    :=
    \sum_{i\in U}\sum_{a,b\in R}\chi_I(a,b)\xi_{i,ab}
    +\sum_{a,b\in R}\chi_I(a,b)\eta_{ab}.
\]
Summing flow conservation over the backbone vertices in~$I$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rounding-fractional-interval-balance}
    \Delta_{x,y}(I)=0
    \qquad\text{for every cyclic interval }I.
\end{equation}

Thus~$x$ assigns fractional detours to collapsed backbone chords, while~$y$
records the circulation already supported entirely on the backbone.  The
next section rounds both families together: every outside vertex receives one
ordered pair of endpoints, and the auxiliary backbone flow becomes integral.

\section{Joint randomized rounding}
\label{sec:joint-circulation-rounding}

For a sampled backbone~$R$ with tour~$T_R$, the rounded structure consists of
one ordered backbone chord for each outside vertex and an integral multiset of
auxiliary backbone chords.  Both parts are chosen jointly, independently of
the eventual activation set.  The rounding preserves their
activation-weighted combined cost in expectation and leaves only
$O(\log^2 n)$ imbalance on every cyclic interval for every outcome.

\begin{lemma}[Joint randomized rounding]
\label{lem:joint-circulation-rounding}
There is a randomized procedure, independent of the vertex activations, that
outputs an ordered pair $(a_i,b_i)\in R\times R$ for every $i\in U$ and a
nonnegative integer vector $N=(N_{ab})_{a,b\in R}$.  Every
outcome satisfies
\[
    \lfloor y_{ab}\rfloor\le N_{ab}\le\lceil y_{ab}\rceil
    \qquad\forall a,b\in R,
\]
and, for every cyclic interval~$I$,
\begin{equation}
    \left|
     \sum_{i\in U}p_i\chi_I(a_i,b_i)
       +\sum_{a,b\in R}N_{ab}\chi_I(a,b)
     \right|
      =O(\log^2 n).
    \label{eq:joint-rounding-interval}
\end{equation}
This bound is uniform over all rounding outcomes and cyclic intervals.
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
    \E_\rho\!\left[
      \sum_{i\in U}p_i
       \bigl(d(a_i,i)+d(i,b_i)\bigr)
      +\sum_{a,b\in R}d(a,b)N_{ab}
    \right]
    =\cost(z).
    \label{eq:joint-rounding-cost}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $m=1$, every chord is a loop and has zero interval imbalance.  Keep the
unique $\xi$-variable for each outside vertex and round
$y_{rr}$ to its floor or ceiling with expectation~$y_{rr}$.  Since
$d(r,r)=0$, the expected-cost identity and the discrepancy bound follow.
Assume henceforth that $m\ge2$.

Introduce variables~$\xi_{i,ab}$, initialized at~$x_{i,ab}$, with bounds
$
    0\le \xi_{i,ab}\le p_i,
$
and variables~$\eta_{ab}$, initialized at~$y_{ab}$, with bounds
$
    \lfloor y_{ab}\rfloor\le\eta_{ab}\le\lceil y_{ab}\rceil.
$
These bounds remain unchanged throughout the procedure.

Let $h:=\lceil\log_2m\rceil$ and pad the linear order
$s_0,\ldots,s_{m-1}$ with empty positions to length~$2^h$.  For
$0\le\ell\le h$ and $0\le j<2^{h-\ell}$, define dyadic intervals
\[
    D_{\ell,j}
    :=\{s_k:0\le k<m,\ j2^\ell\le k<(j+1)2^\ell\}.
\]
Let~$\mathfrak D$ be the family of all nonempty sets~$D_{\ell,j}$,
and put $L:=8(h+1)$.

The rounding procedure considers two families of equality constraints.  For each
$i\in U$, the equality
\begin{equation}\label{eq:joint-rounding-vertex-equation}
    \sum_{a,b\in R}\xi_{i,ab}=p_i
\end{equation}
is the \emph{outside-vertex constraint} for~$i$.  For each
$D\in\mathfrak D$, the equality
\[
    \Delta_{\xi,\eta}(D)=0
\]
is the \emph{interval constraint} for~$D$.

Call a variable \emph{unfixed} when it lies strictly between its two bounds
and \emph{fixed} when it is at either bound; fixed variables are not changed
thereafter.  Initially, all outside-vertex constraints hold by the last
relation in~\cref{eq:rounding-coupling}, and all interval constraints hold by
\cref{eq:rounding-fractional-interval-balance}.  Every outside-vertex
constraint is retained throughout the procedure.  An interval constraint is
retained while at least~$L$ unfixed variables have nonzero coefficients in it
and is discarded permanently once fewer than~$L$ do.

Let $F_x$ and~$F_y$ be the sets of unfixed $\xi$- and $\eta$-variables, and put
$F:=F_x\cup F_y$.  If $F=\emptyset$, terminate.  Otherwise, let~$c_1$ be the
number of outside-vertex constraints containing an unfixed variable.  Such a
constraint cannot contain exactly one unfixed variable:
all its other variables are in~$\{0,p_i\}$, so
\cref{eq:joint-rounding-vertex-equation} would force the remaining variable
to one of its bounds.  Hence
\[
    c_1\le\frac{|F_x|}{2}\le\frac{|F|}{2}.
\]
Let~$c_2$ be the number of retained interval constraints, which currently
contain at least $L$ unfixed variables.  Each position belongs to
at most~$h+1$ dyadic intervals, so every chord variable
has nonzero coefficient in at most~$2(h+1)$ such constraints.  Counting
incidences gives
\[
    c_2L\le2(h+1)|F|,
    \qquad\text{and hence}\qquad
    c_2\le\frac{|F|}{4}.
\]
Thus $c_1+c_2<|F|$.  The homogeneous system associated with the retained
equality constraints has a nonzero direction supported on~$F$.

Move maximally in both signs of this direction until an unfixed variable first
reaches a bound.  More explicitly, for maximal $\alpha,\beta>0$, let
\[
    q^+:=q+\alpha d,
    \qquad
    q^-:=q-\beta d,
\]
where~$q$ is the current vector of unfixed variables and~$d$ is the chosen
direction.  Choose $q^+$ with probability $\beta/(\alpha+\beta)$ and $q^-$
with probability $\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$.  Both points preserve all retained
constraints and fix at least one additional variable, while
\[
    \E[q^{\mathrm{next}}\mid q]=q.
\]
Repeating therefore terminates with every $\xi$-variable in~$\{0,p_i\}$ and
every $\eta$-variable integral.  Each coordinate is a martingale throughout
the procedure.

For each $i\in U$, \cref{eq:joint-rounding-vertex-equation} now implies that
exactly one variable~$\xi_{i,a_ib_i}$ equals~$p_i$.  Put
$N_{ab}:=\eta_{ab}$.  The
martingale property gives
\[
    \P_\rho\bigl((a_i,b_i)=(a,b)\bigr)=\frac{x_{i,ab}}{p_i},
    \qquad
    \E_\rho[N_{ab}]=y_{ab}.
\]
Taking expectations in the rounded objective and using
\cref{eq:rounding-coupling-cost} proves~\cref{eq:joint-rounding-cost}.

Consider a dyadic interval constraint when it is discarded.  Its imbalance
is still zero, and fewer than~$L$ unfixed variables touch it.  Only those
variables can subsequently change.  A $\xi$-variable changes by at
most~$p_i\le1$, and an $\eta$-variable changes by at most one because its
bounds are consecutive integers.  Therefore its final imbalance has absolute
value at most~$L$.
Every linear interval has a canonical decomposition into $O(h+1)$ disjoint
dyadic intervals.  Summing the imbalance up therefore
gives final imbalance $O((h+1)L)=O(\log^2 n)$ for every cyclic interval,
which proves~\cref{eq:joint-rounding-interval}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Rounded backbone-and-chord structure]
\label{thm:rounded-backbone-chord-structure}
For an induced subinstance on~$W\subseteq V^\circ$, sample~$S\subseteq W$
with the activation probabilities, construct~$z^S$ as in
\cref{sec:low-cost-circulation}, and apply the joint rounding.  For a rounding
outcome~$\rho$, define the activation-weighted cost of the resulting structure
by
\begin{align*}
    C_{\mathrm{aug}}(S,\rho)
    &:={}
      \cost(T_R)
      +\sum_{i\in U}p_i\bigl(d(a_i,i)+d(i,b_i)\bigr)
      +\sum_{a,b\in R}d(a,b)N_{ab}.
\end{align*}
Every outcome has interval imbalance $O(\log^2 n)$ as in
\cref{eq:joint-rounding-interval}, and
\[
    \E_{S,\rho}\bigl[C_{\mathrm{aug}}(S,\rho)\bigr]
    =O(\log n)\optpost(W).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The interval-imbalance statement is part of
\cref{lem:joint-circulation-rounding}.  Conditional on~$S$,
\cref{eq:joint-rounding-cost} gives
\[
    \E_\rho\bigl[C_{\mathrm{aug}}(S,\rho)\bigr]
    =\opt(S)+\cost(z^S).
\]
Averaging over~$S$ and applying
\cref{thm:average-circulation-lower-bound} proves the cost bound.
\end{proof}

Thus the construction gives a distribution over integral augmented
backbone-and-chord structures.  Its expected activation-weighted cost is
within an $O(\log n)$ factor of~$\optpost$, while every outcome has
$O(\log^2 n)$ imbalance on every cyclic interval.  The distribution is
independent of the eventual activation realization.  The next section uses
this structure to define an adaptive policy.

\section{Adaptive probing of rare vertices}
\label{sec:adaptive-probing-rare-vertices}

We now use the augmented backbone-and-chord structure to construct a policy
for the vertices below a probability threshold.
The high-probability vertices are handled in
\cref{sec:asymmetric-approximate-adaptive-policy}.

Fix a threshold~$\tau\in(0,1]$ and let
\[
    W_\tau:=\{v\in V^\circ:p_v<\tau\},
    \qquad
    \mu:=\sum_{v\in W_\tau}p_v\le n\tau.
\]
Apply the circulation construction of \cref{sec:low-cost-circulation} and the
joint rounding of \cref{sec:joint-circulation-rounding} to the subinstance
induced by~$W_\tau$.  Fix a backbone sample
$S\subseteq W_\tau$ and an outcome~$\rho$ of the joint rounding, and retain
the notation $R=S\cup\{r\}$, $U=W_\tau\setminus S$, and~$T_R$.

For the analysis, it is harmless to declare every sampled backbone vertex
always active.  More precisely, couple an original realization~$A$ with the
auxiliary realization
\[
    \widehat A:=S\cup(A\cap U).
\]
The rule described below is run in the original instance while treating the
vertices of~$S$ as active in its internal state.  Because the outside outcomes
are coupled identically and the rule ignores the true outcomes on~$S$, the
realized tour for~$A$ is obtained from the auxiliary tour for~$\widehat A$ by
shortcutting the sampled backbone vertices that are actually inactive.  The
asymmetric triangle inequality therefore shows that forcing~$S$ to be active
can only increase the realized-tour length under this coupling.

We describe the rule as a virtual walk on the backbone-and-chord structure.
Fix an order on the outside vertices assigned to each possible tail.  A
virtual pointer starts at~$r$.  Whenever it first encounters a sampled
backbone vertex, the policy probes that vertex.  At a virtual backbone
vertex~$a$, the policy first probes the next unprobed outside vertex~$i$ with
$a_i=a$.  If~$i$ is active, the virtual walk traverses
$a\to i\to b_i$ and the pointer continues from~$b_i$; if~$i$ is inactive,
the pointer remains at~$a$.  Once the list at~$a$ is exhausted, the walk takes
an unused auxiliary chord leaving~$a$, if one exists, and otherwise takes the
next backbone edge.  It continues until every non-root vertex has been probed
and every auxiliary chord has been used, and then follows backbone edges until
the pointer reaches~$r$.
The procedure terminates because every outside probe consumes one vertex and
every auxiliary diversion consumes one unused chord; once none remain,
backbone edges visit every remaining tail.

The actual salesperson moves only when a probe is active.  Its realized tour
is a shortcut of this virtual closed walk.  For each realization the next
lemma bounds the backbone-edge multiplicity by~$\Lambda(F(A))$; afterward we
show that its expectation is $O(\log^2 n+\sqrt\mu)$.  Define the multiset of
chords used under the original realization~$A$ by
\[
    F(A):=N\uplus\biguplus_{i\in A\cap U}\{a_i\to b_i\}.
\]

\begin{lemma}[Edge-multiplicity bound for the virtual walk]
\label{lem:active-first-stack-walk}
For every realization~$A$, put $F:=F(A)$ and define its maximum
cyclic interval imbalance by
\[
    \Lambda(F)
    :=1+\max_{I\text{ cyclic interval of }T_R}
      \left|\sum_{(a,b)\in F}\chi_I(a,b)\right|.
\]
The virtual closed walk described above uses every chord in~$F$ exactly once
and every
backbone edge at most~$\Lambda(F)$ times.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $R=\{r\}$, all its chords are loops at~$r$;
the rule exhausts the outside list and all auxiliary loops at~$r$, so the
claim follows.
Suppose henceforth that~$R$ has at least two vertices.  

Let $c_e$ be the number of uses of backbone edge~$e$.  Flow balance of
the completed virtual closed walk over any cyclic interval implies that the
difference between the multiplicities of its two boundary backbone edges is
exactly the negative of the chord imbalance.  Hence
$|c_e-c_{e'}|\le\Lambda(F)-1$ for any two backbone edges.

If some backbone edge~$e'$ is unused, then
$c_e=|c_e-c_{e'}|\le\Lambda(F)-1$ for every~$e$.  Otherwise consider the
backbone edge whose first use occurs last.  Before a backbone edge is first
used, its tail list and all auxiliary chords at its tail have been exhausted.
Hence, by this last first use, every chord has been used.  The walk then
follows the backbone to~$r$ without using this same edge again, so this edge
is used exactly once.  Every other backbone edge therefore is used 
at most~$\Lambda(F)$ times.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Cost of the rare-vertex policy]
\label{lem:adaptive-circulation-cost}
The induced subinstance on~$W_\tau$ admits an adaptive policy~$\pi_\tau$
satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{eq:low-probability-policy}
    \cost(\pi_\tau)
    =O\!\left(
       \bigl(\log^2 n+\sqrt\mu\bigr)\optpost(W_\tau)
     \right).
\end{equation}
Since $\mu\le n\tau$, the same policy also has cost
$O((\log^2 n+\sqrt{n\tau})\optpost(W_\tau))$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We first bound the imbalance of the active chords.  Let~$A$ be the
original activation set, independent of the backbone sample and the
rounding.  In the auxiliary analysis all vertices of~$S$ are forced active,
so only the independent Bernoulli activations on~$U$ enter~$F(A)$.
For every fixed~$(S,\rho)$ and every cyclic interval~$I$, the realized
imbalance decomposes as
\begin{align*}
    \sum_{e\in F(A)}\chi_I(e)
    &=
      \sum_{a,b\in R}N_{ab}\chi_I(a,b)
      +\sum_{i\in U}\1_{\{i\in A\}}\chi_I(a_i,b_i)\\
    &=
      \left(
        \sum_{a,b\in R}N_{ab}\chi_I(a,b)
        +\sum_{i\in U}p_i\chi_I(a_i,b_i)
      \right)\\
    &\qquad
      +\sum_{i\in U}
       \bigl(\1_{\{i\in A\}}-p_i\bigr)\chi_I(a_i,b_i).
\end{align*}
The absolute value of the parenthesized term is $O(\log^2 n)$ by
\cref{eq:joint-rounding-interval}.

It remains to control the centered activation term.  Put
$X_i:=\1_{\{i\in A\}}-p_i$ for $i\in U$, so the variables~$X_i$ are
independent, centered, and satisfy
\[
    \sum_{i\in U}\E_A[X_i^2]
    =\sum_{i\in U}p_i(1-p_i)
    \le\mu.
\]
To express the imbalance of prefix intervals as the difference of
two partial-sum processes, group the variables~$X_i$ according to the tails
and heads of their assigned chords.  For $0\le j<m$, define
\[
    Y_j^{\mathrm{tail}}
    :=\sum_{i\in U:a_i=s_j}X_i,
    \qquad
    Y_j^{\mathrm{head}}
    :=\sum_{i\in U:b_i=s_j}X_i.
\]
The two families are not necessarily independent of each other, since the
same variable~$X_i$ appears in one tail sum and one head sum.  Within each
family, however, the random variables are independent and centered.  Moreover,
\[
    \sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\E_A[(Y_j^{\mathrm{tail}})^2]
    =\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\E_A[(Y_j^{\mathrm{head}})^2]
    =\sum_{i\in U}\E_A[X_i^2]
    \le\mu.
\]
Thus \cref{lem:maximal-independent-sums} gives
\[
    \E_A\max_{0\le k<m}
      \left|\sum_{j=0}^kY_j^{\mathrm{tail}}\right|
    \le2\sqrt\mu,
    \qquad
    \E_A\max_{0\le k<m}
      \left|\sum_{j=0}^kY_j^{\mathrm{head}}\right|
    \le2\sqrt\mu.
\]
Set $Z_{-1}:=0$.  For the backbone prefix
$J_k:=\{s_0,\ldots,s_k\}$, its imbalance is
\[
    Z_k:=\sum_{i\in U}X_i\cdot\chi_{J_k}(a_i,b_i)
    =\sum_{j=0}^k
      \bigl(Y_j^{\mathrm{tail}}-Y_j^{\mathrm{head}}\bigr).
\]
The preceding two bounds and the triangle inequality imply
\[
    \E_A\max_{-1\le k<m}|Z_k|\le4\sqrt\mu.
\]
Since every chord has one tail and one head, $Z_{m-1}=0$.  For any
cyclic interval~$I$, its imbalance can therefore be expressed as
$Z_k-Z_\ell$ for some $k,\ell\in\{-1,0,\ldots,m-1\}$ corresponding to the
two boundaries of~$I$.  Consequently,
\[
    \E_A\max_I
      \left|\sum_{i\in U}X_i\cdot\chi_I(a_i,b_i)\right|
    \le8\sqrt\mu.
\]
Combining this estimate with the $O(\log^2 n)$ interval-imbalance
bound from~\cref{eq:joint-rounding-interval} yields, for every fixed
preprocessing outcome~$(S,\rho)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:adaptive-active-imbalance}
    \E_A[\Lambda(F(A))]
    =O\!\left(\log^2 n+\sqrt\mu\right).
\end{equation}

By \cref{lem:active-first-stack-walk}, for fixed preprocessing~$(S,\rho)$,
the length of the virtual closed walk for realization~$A$ is at most
\begin{align*}
    &\sum_{a,b\in R}N_{ab}d(a,b)
      +\sum_{i\in A\cap U}\bigl(d(a_i,i)+d(i,b_i)\bigr)\\
    &\hspace{40mm}
      +\Lambda(F(A))\cost(T_R).
\end{align*}
The auxiliary realized tour is a shortcut of this walk.  Under the preceding
coupling, the original realized tour is a further shortcut, so the same
expression also upper-bounds the length of the original realized tour
pointwise.  Taking
expectation over~$A$, the expected cost is therefore at most
\begin{align*}
    &\sum_{a,b\in R}N_{ab}d(a,b)
      +\sum_{i\in U}p_i\bigl(d(a_i,i)+d(i,b_i)\bigr)\\
    &\qquad
      +\E_A[\Lambda(F(A))]\cost(T_R).
\end{align*}
The virtual pointer, the used-chord set, and the unfinished lists are internal
history.  They define a valid history-dependent probing strategy whose
physical tour is the shortcut just described.  By the Markov-policy
observation in \cref{sec:stoch-tsp-benchmarks}, the original instance admits
an adaptive policy~$\pi_{S,\rho}$ with no larger expected cost.  Hence
\begin{align*}
    \cost(\pi_{S,\rho})
    &\le
      \sum_{a,b\in R}N_{ab}d(a,b)
      +\sum_{i\in U}p_i\bigl(d(a_i,i)+d(i,b_i)\bigr)\\
    &\qquad
      +\E_A[\Lambda(F(A))]\cost(T_R).
\end{align*}
Averaging this inequality over~$\rho$ and applying
\cref{eq:joint-rounding-cost} yields, for each fixed~$S$,
\[
    \E_\rho[\cost(\pi_{S,\rho})]
    \le \cost(z^S)
       +\E_\rho\!\left[\E_A[\Lambda(F(A))]\right]\opt(S).
\]
Averaging over~$S$ gives
\begin{align*}
    \E_{S,\rho}[\cost(\pi_{S,\rho})]
    &\le \E_S[\cost(z^S)]
      +\E_{S,\rho}\!\left[
         \E_A[\Lambda(F(A))]\opt(S)
       \right].
\end{align*}
By \cref{eq:adaptive-active-imbalance,thm:average-circulation-lower-bound}, the
right-hand side is
\[
    O\!\left(
      \bigl(\log^2 n+\sqrt\mu\bigr)\optpost(W_\tau)
    \right).
\]
Hence some fixed pair~$(S,\rho)$ has policy
cost no larger than this average.  Fixing that pair before any activation is
probed produces the claimed adaptive policy~$\pi_\tau$.  The final statement
of the lemma follows from~$\mu\le n\tau$.
\end{proof}

\section{An \texorpdfstring{$O(n^{1/3})$}{O(n to the 1/3)}-approximate adaptive policy}
\label{sec:asymmetric-approximate-adaptive-policy}

For a probability threshold~$\tau$, vertices below the threshold have total
activation mass at most $n\tau$, so the preceding policy costs
$O(\log^2 n+\sqrt{n\tau})\optpost$.  Vertices above the threshold admit a
fixed tour with factor $O(1/\tau)$.  The following two lemmas justify
combining the subinstances and establish the fixed-tour bound; we then
choose~$\tau$ to balance the two factors.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:adaptive-policy-concatenation}
Let $W_1,\ldots,W_k\subseteq V^\circ$ be pairwise disjoint vertex sets, and
put
\[
    W:=\bigcup_{j=1}^k W_j.
\]
Let $A\subseteq W$ denote the random set of active vertices.  Suppose the induced subinstance on each~$W_j$ admits an
adaptive policy~$\pi_j$ of expected cost at most
$\alpha_j\optpost(W_j)$.  Then executing
$\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_k$ successively, each from and back to~$r$, gives an
adaptive policy~$\pi$ for~$W$ with
\[
    \cost(\pi)
    \le
    \left(\sum_{j=1}^k\alpha_j\right)\optpost(W).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $A\cap W_j\subseteq A$, shortcutting gives us $\opt(A\cap W_j)\le\opt(A)$.
Taking expectations yields
$\optpost(W_j)\le\optpost(W)$.  Since the policies are executed successively,
their expected costs sum up, and hence
\[
    \cost(\pi)
    \le\sum_{j=1}^k\alpha_j\optpost(W_j)
    \le
    \left(\sum_{j=1}^k\alpha_j\right)\optpost(W).
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:high-probability-fixed-tour}
Let $H\subseteq V^\circ$ be a vertex set, and let $A_H\subseteq H$ denote the
random set of active vertices in the induced subinstance on~$H$.  If
$0<\tau\le1$ and $p_v\ge\tau$ for every $v\in H$, then this subinstance
admits an adaptive policy~$\pi_H$ satisfying
\[
    \cost(\pi_H)
    \le
    \left\lceil\frac1\tau\right\rceil\optpost(H).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $k:=\lceil1/\tau\rceil$.  Independently assign every vertex of~$H$
uniformly to one of $k$ bins $B_1,\ldots,B_k$, and denote the resulting random
partition by~$\mathcal B:=(B_1,\ldots,B_k)$.
For fixed~$j$, the random set~$B_j$ is a product sample with marginal
$1/k\le p_v$ at vertex~$v$.  Couple it with a posterior sample~$A_H$ as
follows: first sample~$A_H$, and then define~$B_j$ by retaining each
$v\in A_H$ independently with probability $(1/k)/p_v$.  For every~$v\in H$,
we have $\P(v\in B_j)=p_v\frac{1/k}{p_v}=\frac1k$, so
the coupled set~$B_j$ has its original product distribution.  Moreover, by
construction, every vertex retained in~$B_j$ belongs to~$A_H$, so
$B_j\subseteq A_H$ for every outcome.  Consequently, shortcutting gives the
pointwise inequality
\[
    \opt(B_j)\le\opt(A_H).
\]
Since the coupled set~$B_j$ has the same law as its corresponding bin under
$\mathcal B$, taking expectation under the joint coupling yields
\[
    \E_{\mathcal B}[\opt(B_j)]
    \le \E[\opt(A_H)]
    =\optpost(H).
\]
Summing over all~$j$ and using linearity of expectation gives
\[
    \E_{\mathcal B}\!\left[\sum_{j=1}^k\opt(B_j)\right]
    =\sum_{j=1}^k\E_{\mathcal B}[\opt(B_j)]
    \le k\optpost(H).
\]
For every realization of~$\mathcal B$, the sets $B_1,\ldots,B_k$ form a
partition of~$H$.  Concatenating an optimal tour through each~$B_j$ therefore
gives a closed walk that visits every vertex of~$H$ and has cost
$\sum_{j=1}^k\opt(B_j)$.  Shortcutting this walk gives the pointwise inequality
\[
    \opt(H)\le\sum_{j=1}^k\opt(B_j).
\]
Since $\opt(H)$ is deterministic, taking expectation in this inequality and
combining it with the preceding upper bound gives
\[
    \opt(H)
    \le
    \E_{\mathcal B}\!\left[\sum_{j=1}^k\opt(B_j)\right]
    \le k\optpost(H).
\]
The policy~$\pi_H$ probes the vertices in the order of an optimal tour through
all of~$H$; each realized route is a shortcut of that fixed tour.
\end{proof}

We now have all the ingredients needed for \cref{thm:asymmetric-clairvoyance-upper},
which asks for an adaptive policy of cost
$O(n^{1/3})\optpost$ for every asymmetric \StochTSP{} instance.
\Cref{lem:adaptive-circulation-cost} provides a policy for the low-probability
vertices, \cref{lem:high-probability-fixed-tour} provides one for the
high-probability vertices, and \cref{lem:adaptive-policy-concatenation} allows
us to combine them.  It remains only to choose the probability threshold so
that the two cost bounds balance.

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:asymmetric-clairvoyance-upper}]
Set $\tau:=n^{-1/3}$ and split the vertex set into
\[
    H:=\{v\in V^\circ:p_v\ge\tau\},
    \qquad
    W_\tau:=\{v\in V^\circ:p_v<\tau\}.
\]
By \cref{lem:high-probability-fixed-tour}, the high-probability vertices have
an adaptive policy~$\pi_H$ with
\[
    \cost(\pi_H)
    \le \left\lceil\frac1\tau\right\rceil\optpost(H).
\]
Since $\tau=n^{-1/3}$, this is $O(n^{1/3})\optpost(H)$.
For the low-probability set, \cref{lem:adaptive-circulation-cost} gives
the policy~$\pi_\tau$ with
\[
    \cost(\pi_\tau)
    =O\!\left(n^{1/3}\optpost(W_\tau)\right).
\]
Here we used $\log^2 n=O(n^{1/3})$.
Let~$\pi$ execute~$\pi_H$ and~$\pi_\tau$ successively, returning to~$r$
between them.  By \cref{lem:adaptive-policy-concatenation},
\[
    \cost(\pi)=O(n^{1/3})\optpost.
\]
\end{proof}

\chapter{Adaptivity Gap for Asymmetric \StochTSP{}}\label{ch:asym-adapt}

This chapter proves an adaptivity-gap lower bound of
$\Omega(n^{1/3}/\log n)$ for asymmetric metrics.  We use a rectangular
variant of the asymmetric grid instances introduced by Christalla, Puhlmann,
and Traub~\cite[Section~3.1]{Christalla2025}: there are $k^2$ cyclic columns,
each containing $k$ vertices, and every non-root vertex is active with
probability~$1/k$.  Thus the number of non-root vertices is $n=k^3$.

The proof separates into an a~priori lower bound and an adaptive upper bound.
For an a~priori tour, divide its order into packets of $k$ consecutive
vertices.  If a packet meets many columns, its vertices determine many
distinct forward distances; if it meets few columns, it contains many pairs
in the same column, each separated by one full revolution of the grid.  Either
case forces a large expected contribution.  An adaptive policy instead
sweeps through the columns and visits at most one active vertex per column in
each sweep.  Its number of sweeps is the maximum active load of a column,
whose expectation is $O(\log k)$.

\begin{theorem}[Asymmetric adaptivity-gap lower bound]
\label{thm:asymmetric-adaptivity-lower}
The size-restricted adaptivity gap for asymmetric \StochTSP{} satisfies
\[
    \operatorname{AG}(n)=\Omega(n^{1/3}/\log n).
\]
\end{theorem}

We first prove the result when the number of non-root vertices is a perfect
cube.  At the end of the chapter we extend the construction to every
sufficiently large~$n$ while keeping all activation probabilities strictly
positive.

\section{The rectangular asymmetric grid}
\label{sec:asym-adapt-grid}

\begin{construction}\label{con:rectangular-asymmetric-grid}
Fix an integer $k\ge4$ and put $m\coloneqq k^2$.  Introduce a root~$r$ and, for every
$j\in[m]$, a column
\[
    C_j\coloneqq\{v_{i,j}:i\in[k]\}.
\]
Thus
\[
    V_k^\circ\coloneqq\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}C_j
\]
contains $k^3$ non-root vertices.  Regard the root as a singleton column
$C_0\coloneqq\{r\}$.  For every $j\in\{0,1,\ldots,m\}$, add in an auxiliary
graph a unit-length arc from every vertex of~$C_j$ to every vertex
of~$C_{j+1}$, where $C_{m+1}\coloneqq C_0$.  Let $d_k$ be the shortest-path
metric of this graph.
Every non-root vertex is active independently with probability
\[
    p\coloneqq\frac1k,
\]
while the root is always active.  Denote the resulting instance by
$\mathcal I_k^{\mathrm{RG}}$.
\end{construction}

For $a,b\in\{0,1,\ldots,m\}$, let $\Delta(a,b)$ be the unique integer in
$[m+1]$ congruent to $b-a$ modulo~$m+1$.  The metric has the following
explicit form.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:rectangular-grid-distances}
If $x\in C_a$ and $y\in C_b$ are distinct vertices, then
\[
    d_k(x,y)=\Delta(a,b).
\]
In particular, two distinct vertices in the same column have distance
$m+1=k^2+1$ in either order.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Every arc advances by one column in the cyclic order.  Hence every path from
$C_a$ to~$C_b$ uses at least $\Delta(a,b)$ arcs.  Using $x$ and~$y$ as
endpoints and choosing an arbitrary vertex in every intermediate column gives
a path of exactly that length.
\end{proof}

Since $d_k$ is a shortest-path metric, it is strictly positive on distinct
vertices and satisfies the triangle inequality.  It is asymmetric: for
example, a vertex in~$C_1$ has distance~$1$ to every vertex in~$C_2$, whereas
the reverse distance is~$m$.

\section{A packet lower bound for a priori tours}
\label{sec:asym-adapt-prior}

The next lemma is the main combinatorial observation.  It applies to every
possible ordering of the vertices in one packet.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:rectangular-grid-packet}
Let $z_1,\ldots,z_k$ be distinct non-root vertices in an arbitrary order.
Then
\[
    \sum_{1\le a<b\le k}d_k(z_a,z_b)
    \ge
    \frac{k^3}{96}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $s$ be the number of columns represented by $z_1,\ldots,z_k$.

Suppose first that $s\ge k/2$.  Choose one representative from every
represented column, retain their order in the sequence, and call them
$y_1,\ldots,y_s$.  For a fixed $a\in[s-1]$, the $s-a$ distances
\[
    d_k(y_a,y_{a+1}),\ldots,d_k(y_a,y_s)
\]
are distinct positive integers.  Indeed, the later representatives lie in
distinct columns, and the forward distance from the column of~$y_a$
determines the destination column uniquely.  Therefore
\[
    \sum_{b=a+1}^{s}d_k(y_a,y_b)
    \ge 1+2+\cdots+(s-a).
\]
Summing over~$a$ gives
\[
    \sum_{1\le a<b\le k}d_k(z_a,z_b)
    \ge
    \binom{s+1}{3}
    \ge
    \frac{k^3}{96},
\]
where the last inequality uses $s\ge k/2$ and $k\ge4$.

Now suppose that $s<k/2$.  For every $j\in[m]$, let
$a_j\coloneqq|C_j\cap\{z_1,\ldots,z_k\}|$.  Since
$\binom{a_j}{2}\ge a_j-1$,
\[
    \sum_{j=1}^{m}\binom{a_j}{2}
    \ge
    \sum_{j:a_j>0}(a_j-1)
    =k-s
    >\frac{k}{2}.
\]
Every pair counted on the left lies in one column and has distance
$m+1\ge k^2$ by \cref{lem:rectangular-grid-distances}.  Its contribution to
the claimed sum is taken in whichever order the two vertices occur in the
packet.  Hence
\[
    \sum_{1\le a<b\le k}d_k(z_a,z_b)
    >\frac{k}{2}\,k^2,
\]
which proves the lemma in the second case as well.
\end{proof}

We now apply the packet lemma to the expected cost of an arbitrary a~priori
tour.  Restricting attention to pairs whose endpoints lie in the same packet
is useful because the probability that all intermediate vertices are inactive
then remains bounded away from zero.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:rectangular-grid-prior}
Every a~priori tour for $\mathcal I_k^{\mathrm{RG}}$ has expected cost at
least
\[
    \frac{k^3}{384}.
\]
Consequently, $\optprior(\mathcal I_k^{\mathrm{RG}})\ge k^3/384$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix an arbitrary a~priori tour
$T=(r,x_1,\ldots,x_{k^3},r)$.
Partition its non-root order into the $k^2$ consecutive packets
\[
    P_t
    \coloneqq(x_{(t-1)k+1},\ldots,x_{tk}),
    \qquad t\in[k^2].
\]
By the definition of shortcutting in \cref{sec:stoch-tsp-benchmarks}, for
$i<j$ the shortcut $T|_A$ uses the ordered pair $(x_i,x_j)$ precisely
when both endpoints are active and all vertices strictly between them in~$T$
are inactive.  Recalling that $p=1/k$, this event has probability
\[
    p^2(1-p)^{j-i-1}.
\]
If $x_i$ and~$x_j$ belong to the same packet, then $j-i-1\le k-2$, so
\[
    p^2(1-p)^{j-i-1}
    \ge
    \frac1{k^2}\left(1-\frac1k\right)^k
    \ge
    \frac1{4k^2}.
\]
Here we used $(1-1/k)^k\ge1/4$ for $k\ge2$.

All terms in the expected cost are nonnegative.  We may therefore retain
only the ordered pairs whose endpoints lie in the same packet.  By
\cref{lem:rectangular-grid-packet}, every packet contributes at least
\[
    \frac1{4k^2}
    \sum_{\substack{x_i,x_j\in P_t\\i<j}}d_k(x_i,x_j)
    \ge
    \frac1{4k^2}\cdot\frac{k^3}{96}
    =\frac{k}{384}.
\]
Summing over the $k^2$ packets gives $\cost(T)\ge k^3/384$.  Since $T$ was
arbitrary, the same lower bound holds for the a~priori optimum.
\end{proof}

\section{An adaptive column-sweep policy}
\label{sec:asym-adapt-policy}

Fix an arbitrary ordering of the vertices within each stochastic column.  The
\emph{column-sweep policy} works in sweeps.  During one sweep it processes
$C_1,C_2,\ldots,C_m$ in this order.  In column~$C_j$, it probes the remaining
vertices according to the fixed local order until it either finds the first
active vertex, which it visits immediately, or exhausts the column.  After an
active vertex is found, all remaining vertices of that column are deferred to
the next sweep.  Once every non-root vertex has been probed, the policy
returns to~$r$.

This description uses a sweep counter only as bookkeeping.  It defines a
feasible causal strategy, and the Markovization observation in
\cref{sec:stoch-tsp-benchmarks} gives a policy of no greater expected cost
that depends only on the current vertex and the set of unprobed vertices.

For a realization~$A$, define
\[
    X_j\coloneqq|A\cap C_j|,
    \qquad
    M\coloneqq\max_{j\in[m]}X_j.
\]
Each $X_j$ has distribution $\operatorname{Binomial}(k,1/k)$, and these
variables are independent.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:rectangular-grid-sweep}
For every realization~$A$, the column-sweep policy has cost at most
$(m+1)M$.  Consequently,
\[
    \optadapt(\mathcal I_k^{\mathrm{RG}})
    \le
    (m+1)\E[M].
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $M=0$, the policy never moves and its cost is~$0$.  Assume henceforth that
$M\ge1$.

The policy discovers at most one active vertex from a column during one
sweep.  If column~$C_j$ contains $X_j$ active vertices, the policy discovers
one in each of the first $X_j$ sweeps.  Hence all active vertices have been
visited after at most~$M$ sweeps.  Any vertices probed later are inactive and
cause no movement.

Assign the root lifted coordinate~$0$.  If the policy visits a vertex in
column~$C_j$ during sweep~$h$, assign that visit lifted coordinate
\[
    j+(h-1)(m+1).
\]
These coordinates increase along the realized route.  Within one sweep, the
difference between consecutive lifted coordinates equals the corresponding
metric distance.  Between consecutive sweeps, the metric distance is at most
the difference of the lifted coordinates.  Finally, assign the return to the
root lifted coordinate~$M(m+1)$; its metric distance from the last visited
vertex is again at most the difference of the two coordinates.  The sum of
the distances therefore telescopes to at most~$(m+1)M$.
\end{proof}

It remains to bound the expected maximum column load.  We use only an
elementary exponential-moment estimate.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:rectangular-grid-load}
For $k\ge4$,
\[
    \E[M]=O(\log k).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For each $j\in[m]$,
\[
    \E[2^{X_j}]
    =\left(1-\frac1k+\frac2k\right)^k
    =\left(1+\frac1k\right)^k
    \le \e.
\]
Markov's inequality and a union bound over the $m=k^2$ columns give, for
every integer $t\ge0$,
\[
    \P[M\ge t]
    \le
    \e k^2 2^{-t}.
\]
Set $t_0\coloneqq\lceil3\log_2 k\rceil$.  Since $M$ is nonnegative and
integer-valued,
\begin{align*}
    \E[M]
    &=\sum_{t\ge1}\P[M\ge t]\\
    &\le t_0+\sum_{t>t_0}\e k^2 2^{-t}\\
    &\le 3\log_2 k+2+\frac{\e}{k}.
\end{align*}
Thus, $\E[M]=O(\log k)$.
\end{proof}

\section{Proof of the lower bound}
\label{sec:asym-adapt-gap}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:asymmetric-adaptivity-lower}]
We first combine the preceding bounds for the base instance.  By
\cref{lem:rectangular-grid-prior,lem:rectangular-grid-sweep,lem:rectangular-grid-load},
\[
    \optprior(\mathcal I_k^{\mathrm{RG}})
    \ge \frac{k^3}{384},
    \qquad
    \optadapt(\mathcal I_k^{\mathrm{RG}})
    =O(k^2\log k).
\]
It follows that
\[
    \frac{\optprior(\mathcal I_k^{\mathrm{RG}})}
         {\optadapt(\mathcal I_k^{\mathrm{RG}})}
    =\Omega\!\left(\frac{k}{\log k}\right).
\]

Let $n$ be sufficiently large and set $k\coloneqq\lfloor n^{1/3}\rfloor$.  Start
with $\mathcal I_k^{\mathrm{RG}}$, which has $k^3$ non-root vertices, and put
$s\coloneqq n-k^3$.  Since $n<(k+1)^3$, we have $s=O(k^2)$.

Add $s$ new vertices.  Retain all old distances and set the distance between
any new vertex and any other distinct vertex, in either order, to
$D\coloneqq k^2+1$.  This extension is a metric: the old metric has diameter at
most~$D$, and every triangle containing a new vertex satisfies the triangle
inequality immediately.  It remains asymmetric because its restriction to
the old vertices is asymmetric.  Give each new vertex activation probability
$\varepsilon\coloneqq k^{-10}$.

Consider an arbitrary a~priori tour of the extended instance.  With
probability $(1-\varepsilon)^s$, all new vertices are inactive.  Conditioned
on this event, the original activations retain their original distribution,
and shortcutting the new vertices leaves an a~priori order for
$\mathcal I_k^{\mathrm{RG}}$.  Its conditional expected cost is therefore at
least $k^3/384$, so
\[
    \optprior
    \ge
    (1-\varepsilon)^s\frac{k^3}{384}
    =\Omega(k^3),
\]
where the last equality uses $s=O(k^2)$ and $\varepsilon=k^{-10}$.

For an adaptive upper bound, run the column-sweep procedure on the original
vertices without its final return to the root, then probe the new vertices in
any order, and return to the root after every vertex has been probed.  The
triangle inequality bounds the realized cost by the cost of the closed
column-sweep route plus that of a closed route through the active new
vertices: it replaces the direct move from the last original active vertex to
the first new active vertex by a detour through the root.  If $Y$ new vertices
are active, the latter cost is at most $2DY$; hence its expectation is at most
\[
    2D\E[Y]
    =2Ds\varepsilon
    =o(1).
\]
Together with \cref{lem:rectangular-grid-sweep,lem:rectangular-grid-load},
this gives $\optadapt=O(k^2\log k)$.  Finally,
$k=\Theta(n^{1/3})$ and $\log k=\Theta(\log n)$, proving the theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:asymmetric-obliviousness-lower}
The size-restricted obliviousness gap for asymmetric \StochTSP{} satisfies
$\operatorname{OG}(n)=\Omega(n^{1/3}/\log n)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
This follows immediately from
\cref{eq:optineq,thm:asymmetric-adaptivity-lower}.
\end{proof}

The standard sharper estimate
$\E[M]=O(\log k/\log\log k)$ would strengthen both lower bounds for this
family to $\Omega(n^{1/3}\log\log n/\log n)$; we use only the elementary
$O(\log k)$ bound above.

\chapter{Almost-Optimal A Priori Tours via Dynamic Programming}\label{ch:dp}

Our main focus in this chapter is to compute almost-optimal a~priori tours for
\StochTSP{}.  A polynomial-time approximation scheme is impossible unless
$\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$: when every non-root vertex is active with
probability~$1$, the a~priori and a~posteriori optima both coincide with the
optimum of classical metric TSP, which is APX-hard~\cite{Karpinski2015}.

We instead allow exponential running time.  The main result is a deterministic
$(1+\varepsilon)$-approximation algorithm for a~priori TSP on both symmetric
and asymmetric metrics.  For every fixed $\varepsilon>0$, the algorithm runs
in time $c_\varepsilon^n\poly(n)$, where $c_\varepsilon$ is a constant depending
only on $\varepsilon$.

An a~priori solution fixes a tour before the active vertices are known, and
its realization shortcuts the inactive vertices.  The main difficulty is
that vertices far apart in the fixed order may therefore become consecutive.
In \Cref{sec:dp-pairwise-objective}, we
decompose the expected cost into pairwise interactions and show that the
rarest of them can be ignored with only a small loss.  After grouping the
vertices into blocks, the remaining interactions are local enough to be
handled by dynamic programming; this is carried out in
\Cref{sec:dp-block-objective,sec:dp-dynamic-program}.  The parameter choices and
the final approximation bound are given in \Cref{sec:dp-approximation}.

\section{Pairwise decomposition and truncation}
\label{sec:dp-pairwise-objective}
\label{sec:dp-truncation}

We begin by rewriting the expected shortcut cost as a sum over pairs of
vertices in the a~priori order.  A pair contributes precisely when its two
endpoints are active and every vertex lying strictly between them is inactive.  This exact
decomposition identifies the low-probability terms that can later be
discarded.

Let $T=(v_0=r,v_1,\dots,v_n,v_{n+1}=r)$ be an a~priori tour,
where $V^\circ=\{v_1,\dots,v_n\}$.  For $0\le i<j\le n+1$, use the
shorthands $p_i:=p_{v_i}$ and $d_{ij}:=d(v_i,v_j)$, and define
$Q(i,j):=\prod_{i<k<j}(1-p_k)$, with the convention that an empty product
equals~$1$.  In particular, $p_0=p_{n+1}=p_r=1$.
Thus, $Q(i,j)$ is the probability that every vertex strictly between $v_i$ and
$v_j$ is inactive.  The expected cost of $T$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:apriori-pairwise}
    \cost(T)
    =
    \sum_{0\le i<j\le n+1}p_i p_j Q(i,j)d_{ij}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, the term indexed by $(i,j)$ is paid exactly when $v_i$ and $v_j$ are
consecutive active vertices in the shortcut of~$T$.

For a threshold parameter $\eta\in(0,1)$, define the truncated cost of an
a~priori tour~$T$ to be the right-hand side of
\cref{eq:apriori-pairwise} without the pairs $(i,j)$ for which
$Q(i,j)\le\eta$.  That is,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:local-pair-cost}
    \cost^{\loc}_{\eta}(T)
    :=
    \sum_{\substack{0\le i<j\le n+1\\Q(i,j)>\eta}}
    p_i p_j Q(i,j)d_{ij}.
\end{equation}

The following lemma shows that a polynomially small threshold suffices to
control the approximation loss between the full and truncated costs.

\begin{lemma}[Pairwise truncation bound]
\label{lem:long-gap-truncation}
Let $\rho\in(0,1]$ be an error parameter and suppose that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:long-gap-truncation}
    0<\eta\le\frac{\rho^2}{16}.
\end{equation}
For any a~priori tour~$T$,
\[
    \cost^{\loc}_{\eta}(T)
    \le \cost(T)
    \le(1+\rho)\cost^{\loc}_{\eta}(T).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The first inequality follows immediately because the truncated cost omits
only nonnegative terms.  For the second inequality, put
\[
    \beta(\eta):=
    \frac{\eta\bigl(3+\ln(1/\eta)\bigr)}{1-\eta}.
\]
The discarded-pair charging bound proved in
\cref{lem:discarded-pair-charging} gives
\[
    \cost(T)-\cost^{\loc}_{\eta}(T)
    \le\beta(\eta)\cost(T).
\]
The function $\beta$ is increasing on $(0,1)$ because
\[
    \beta'(\eta)
    =\frac{2+\eta-\ln\eta}{(1-\eta)^2}>0.
\]
Since
$\eta\le\rho^2/16\le1/16$, we have
\begin{align*}
    \beta(\eta)
    &\le\frac{\rho^2}{15}
        \left(3+\ln16+2\ln\frac1\rho\right)\\
    &\le\frac{\rho^2}{15}\left(4+\frac2\rho\right)
    \le\frac{2\rho}{5}
    \le\frac{\rho}{1+\rho}.
\end{align*}
Here we used $\ln(1/\rho)\le1/\rho-1$, $\ln16<3$, and
$0<\rho\le1$.  Therefore
\[
    \cost^{\loc}_{\eta}(T)
    \ge\bigl(1-\beta(\eta)\bigr)\cost(T)
    \ge\frac{\cost(T)}{1+\rho},
\]
which proves the second inequality.
\end{proof}

\section{The block objective}\label{sec:dp-block-objective}

The truncated objective still depends on the relative order of individual
vertices.  Our goal is to compress most of these order decisions into an
order of blocks, while summarizing the internal order of each block by a
single precomputed value.  The remaining problem is that the exact coefficient of a
pair depends on the inactive probabilities of all vertices between its
endpoints, including vertices in the two endpoint blocks. And enumerating the set
of vertices in between is inefficient.

We solve this problem by placing every vertex of large activation
probability in a singleton block and requiring every remaining block to have
small total activation probability.  Within such a small block, every
inactive product $Q$ is close to~$1$, so we can approximate it by~$1$ and optimize a
simpler internal objective.  For a pair in different blocks, we retain the
inactivity product over the complete blocks strictly between the endpoint
blocks but omit the two partial products inside the endpoint blocks.  These
omissions give an upper bound, and the small block masses control the resulting
overestimate.  The
surrogate obtained from these two approximations is the \emph{block
objective}.

We first specify what blocks are allowed.  Fix $0<\delta<1/4$ and recall
that $q_v=1-p_v$.  A non-root vertex is \emph{$\delta$-heavy} if
$p_v>\delta/2$ and \emph{light} otherwise.  For a set of non-root
vertices~$B$, write
\[
    \mu(B):=\sum_{v\in B}p_v,
    \qquad
    q(B):=\prod_{v\in B}q_v.
\]
For the root block $\{r\}$, set $q(\{r\})=1$.

$\delta$-heavy vertices must remain singletons.  Light vertices may be grouped as long
as their total mass is at most~$\delta$; this upper bound makes the two
approximations above accurate.  We also prevent a run of light blocks from
being split into arbitrarily many blocks of tiny mass.  Except for the first
block in such a run, every light block must have mass at least~$\delta/2$.
This lower bound will later ensure that the dynamic program needs to remember
only a bounded number of recent blocks.

\begin{samepage}
\begin{definition}[$\delta$-admissible blocks and partitions]\label{def:admissible-partition}
A block is \emph{$\delta$-admissible} if it is either a singleton containing one $\delta$-heavy
vertex or a nonempty set of light vertices with total mass at most~$\delta$.
An ordered partition $(B_1,\dots,B_m)$ is \emph{$\delta$-admissible} if all its blocks are
$\delta$-admissible and, whenever $B_{t-1}$ and $B_t$ are both light blocks,
\[
    \mu(B_t)\ge\delta/2.
\]
Thus, in every consecutive run of light blocks, only the first block may have
mass below~$\delta/2$.
\end{definition}
\end{samepage}

Fix a $\delta$-admissible ordered partition
$\mathcal B=(B_1,\dots,B_m)$ and introduce the two root blocks
\[
    B_0=B_{m+1}:=\{r\}.
\]
We now formalize the two parts of the block objective.

First consider pairs whose endpoints lie in the same block.
Let $B$ be a light block and let $\tau$ be an order of its vertices.  For
$x<_\tau y$, the inactivity factor contributed by the vertices of~$B$
strictly between $x$ and~$y$ satisfies
\[
    \prod_{x<_\tau z<_\tau y}q_z
    \ge
    1-\sum_{z\in B}p_z
    \ge 1-\delta.
\]
Thus this factor is uniformly close to~$1$, regardless of the precise
separation of $x$ and~$y$ inside the block.  We replace it by~$1$ but still
choose the internal order that gives the smallest resulting contribution.
This gives the definition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:internal-block-cost}
    I(B)
    :=
    \min_{\tau}
    \sum_{x<_\tau y}p_xp_y d(x,y),
\end{equation}
where the minimum is over all orders~$\tau$ of~$B$.  For $\delta$-heavy singletons and
root blocks, set $I(B)=0$.

The value $I(B)$ and a minimizing order can be computed by choosing the last
vertex of the order:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:internal-block-recurrence}
    I(B)
    =
    \min_{y\in B}
    \left(
        I(B\setminus\{y\})
        +\sum_{x\in B\setminus\{y\}}p_xp_y d(x,y)
    \right),
    \qquad I(\emptyset)=0.
\end{equation}
Indeed, the second term is exactly the contribution of the pairs whose last
vertex is~$y$.

Next fix endpoints $x\in B_i$ and $y\in B_j$ in two different blocks, where
$i<j$.  Their exact inactivity factor contains the product of three parts:
the inactive probabilities of the vertices in~$B_i$ after~$x$, the inactive
probabilities of all vertices in the complete blocks strictly between $B_i$
and~$B_j$, and the inactive probabilities of the vertices in~$B_j$ before~$y$.
The first and third parts depend on the internal endpoint positions.  We drop
these two partial-block factors and retain only the product over the complete
blocks strictly between the endpoint blocks.  To aggregate the remaining base
costs between two blocks, define
\[
    C(B_i,B_j)
    :=
    \sum_{x\in B_i}\sum_{y\in B_j}p_xp_y d(x,y).
\]
As in the pairwise truncation from \cref{sec:dp-pairwise-objective}, we keep a block pair only when the retained
inactivity product exceeds~$\eta$.  Combining the internal and cross-block
contributions gives the block objective
\begin{equation}\label{eq:block-objective}
    F_\eta(\mathcal B)
    :=
    \sum_{t=1}^m I(B_t)
    +
    \sum_{\substack{0\le i<j\le m+1\\
                     \prod_{i<t<j}q(B_t)>\eta}}
    \left(\prod_{i<t<j}q(B_t)\right)C(B_i,B_j).
\end{equation}
The first sum summarizes pairs within the same block.  The second sum
summarizes pairs in different blocks using only the product of~$q(B_t)$ over
the complete blocks strictly between the endpoint blocks.

Finally, order every light block according to a minimizer in
\cref{eq:internal-block-cost} and concatenate the block orders, with the root
at the beginning and end.  Denote the resulting a~priori tour by
$T_{\mathcal B}$.  To justify minimizing the block objective, we need two
comparisons.  First, $F_\eta(\mathcal B)$ should be close to the cost of its
corresponding tour $T_{\mathcal B}$; otherwise, a small block value could hide
a tour with large true cost.  Second, every a~priori tour~$T$ should admit a
$\delta$-admissible consecutive partition~$\mathcal B_T$ whose block value is not much
larger than $\cost(T)$.  Applied to an optimal tour, this ensures that the
minimum block value is not much larger than~$\optprior$.  The next lemma makes
both comparisons precise.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:block-objective-bounds}
Fix $\delta\in(0,1/4)$ and $\eta\in(0,1)$.
The block objective satisfies the following inequalities.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item For every $\delta$-admissible ordered partition~$\mathcal B$,
    \begin{align}
        \cost^{\loc}_{\eta}(T_{\mathcal B})
        &\le F_\eta(\mathcal B),
        \label{eq:block-objective-dominates-local}\\
        F_\eta(\mathcal B)
        &\le(1-\delta)^{-2}\cost(T_{\mathcal B}).
        \label{eq:block-objective-faithful-upper}
    \end{align}
    Consequently, if $\rho\in(0,1]$ and
    $0<\eta\le\rho^2/16$, then
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:block-objective-two-sided}
        \frac{1}{1+\rho}\cost(T_{\mathcal B})
        \le F_\eta(\mathcal B)
        \le \frac{1}{(1-\delta)^2}\cost(T_{\mathcal B}).
    \end{equation}

    \item For every a~priori tour~$T$, there is a $\delta$-admissible ordered
    partition $\mathcal B_T$, consecutive in~$T$, such that
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:block-objective-from-order}
        F_\eta(\mathcal B_T)
        \le(1-\delta)^{-2}\cost(T).
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We first record the estimate that controls every omitted partial-block
factor.  If~$B$ is a light block and $B'\subseteq B$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:partial-block-factor}
    q(B')
    \ge 1-\sum_{v\in B'}p_v
    \ge 1-\delta.
\end{equation}
The first inequality is the left-hand bound in
\cref{eq:bernoulli-product-bounds}.
For a $\delta$-heavy singleton or a root block, the corresponding factor equals~$1$.

For the first inequality in~(i), fix a $\delta$-admissible ordered
partition~$\mathcal B$ and consider
endpoints $x\in B_i$ and $y\in B_j$ with $i<j$.  In the tour
$T_{\mathcal B}$, their exact coefficient is
\[
    p_xp_y\,
    q(B_i^{>x})\,
    \left(\prod_{i<t<j}q(B_t)\right)\,
    q(B_j^{<y}),
\]
where $B_i^{>x}$ denotes the vertices of $B_i$ after~$x$, and
$B_j^{<y}$ the vertices of $B_j$ before~$y$.  If $(x,y)$ is retained in
$\cost^{\loc}_{\eta}(T_{\mathcal B})$, then its exact inactivity probability
exceeds~$\eta$.  Removing the two endpoint-block factors can only increase
that probability, so the product over the complete blocks strictly between
the endpoint blocks also exceeds~$\eta$.  Hence the corresponding block pair occurs in
\cref{eq:block-objective}, with a coefficient at least as large as the exact
one.

If both endpoints lie in the same light block~$B$, their exact inactivity
factor is at most~$1$.  Their total retained contribution is therefore at
most the corresponding sum of $p_xp_y d(x,y)$ in the chosen internal order,
which is exactly~$I(B)$.  Summing these bounds over all retained pairs proves
\cref{eq:block-objective-dominates-local}.

For the second inequality in~(i), first consider a cross-block term of
$F_\eta(\mathcal B)$.  Relative to the exact coefficient of the same pair in
$T_{\mathcal B}$, its coefficient omits only the two partial-block factors
$q(B_i^{>x})$ and $q(B_j^{<y})$.  Each is either~$1$ or at least $1-\delta$
by~\cref{eq:partial-block-factor}.  Hence the block coefficient is at most
$(1-\delta)^{-2}$ times the exact coefficient.  Now let~$B$ be a light block
and let $W_B(T_{\mathcal B})$ denote the exact contribution of the pairs
internal to~$B$.  The internal order of $T_{\mathcal B}$ realizes~$I(B)$,
and every inactivity factor between two vertices of~$B$ is at least
$1-\delta$.  Therefore
\[
    I(B)\le \frac{1}{1-\delta}W_B(T_{\mathcal B}).
\]
The cross-block sum in $F_\eta$ contains only a subset of all cross-block
pairs, so summing these comparisons gives
\cref{eq:block-objective-faithful-upper}.
Combining~\cref{eq:block-objective-dominates-local} with
\cref{lem:long-gap-truncation} proves
\cref{eq:block-objective-two-sided}.

For~(ii), start from an arbitrary a~priori tour~$T$ and keep every $\delta$-heavy vertex
as a singleton.  In each maximal consecutive run of light vertices, scan from
right to left.  Close a block as soon as its mass reaches~$\delta/2$, and then
continue with the remaining prefix of the run.  Since every light vertex has
probability at most~$\delta/2$, each closed block has mass in
$[\delta/2,\delta]$.  Only the leftmost block of a run may have mass below
$\delta/2$.  The resulting consecutive partition~$\mathcal B_T$ is therefore
$\delta$-admissible.

Consider a cross-block term of $F_\eta(\mathcal B_T)$ with endpoints
$x\in B_i$ and $y\in B_j$, where $i<j$.  Relative to the exact coefficient of
this pair in~$T$, the corresponding term of $F_\eta(\mathcal B_T)$ omits
only the factors corresponding to the vertices after~$x$ in~$B_i$ and
before~$y$ in~$B_j$.  By~\cref{eq:partial-block-factor}, every cross-block
coefficient in $F_\eta(\mathcal B_T)$ is at most
$(1-\delta)^{-2}$ times the corresponding exact coefficient in~$T$.

It remains to compare the internal terms.  Let $B$ be a light block and retain
the order induced by~$T$ on~$B$.  If $W_B(T)$ denotes the exact contribution
of pairs with both endpoints in~$B$, then the definition of~$I(B)$ in
\cref{eq:internal-block-cost} and the order induced by~$T$ give
\[
    I(B)
    \le
    \sum_{\substack{x,y\in B\\x<_T y}}p_xp_y d(x,y)
    \le
    \frac{1}{1-\delta}W_B(T).
\]
The second inequality holds because every inactivity factor between two
vertices of~$B$ is at least $q(B)\ge1-\delta$.  Summing the cross-block and
internal comparisons proves~\cref{eq:block-objective-from-order}.
\end{proof}

Part~(i) says that the block objective is a two-sided approximation to the
cost of every tour that it represents.  Part~(ii) says that every a~priori
tour, and in particular an optimal one, has a $\delta$-admissible consecutive
partition whose block value is within a factor $(1-\delta)^{-2}$ of its cost.
Together, these comparisons explain why minimizing $F_\eta$ is a valid
surrogate for minimizing the exact tour cost.

\section{The dynamic program}\label{sec:dp-dynamic-program}

We now minimize $F_\eta$ exactly over all $\delta$-admissible ordered partitions.
Consider a partial partition
\[
    (B_0,B_1,\dots,B_t),
    \qquad B_0=\{r\}.
\]
If
\[
    \prod_{s=1}^t q(B_s)>\eta,
\]
its \emph{relevant suffix} is the entire prefix
$(B_0,B_1,\dots,B_t)$.  Otherwise, let $a$ be the largest index such that
\[
    \prod_{s=a}^t q(B_s)\le\eta.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
    \prod_{s=a}^t q(B_s)\le\eta
    \quad\text{and}\quad
    \prod_{s=a+1}^t q(B_s)>\eta.
\]
The relevant suffix is then $(B_a,B_{a+1},\dots,B_t)$.

A state consists of the set $U\subseteq V^\circ$ of already placed vertices and the
relevant suffix~$\Omega$ of their partial partition.  Its value is the minimum,
over all represented prefixes, of the internal terms of the placed blocks plus
all cross-block terms whose right endpoint has already been placed.  The
initial state is $(\emptyset,(B_0))$ with value~$0$.

A feasible next block~$B$ is either a singleton containing an unplaced $\delta$-heavy
vertex or a nonempty set of unplaced light vertices with
$\mu(B)\le\delta$.  If the last block of the current prefix and~$B$ are both
light, we additionally require $\mu(B)\ge\delta/2$.  Suppose
$\Omega=(B_a,\dots,B_t)$.  Appending~$B$ adds
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dp-increment}
    I(B)
    +
    \sum_{s=a}^t
    \left(\prod_{s<u\le t}q(B_u)\right)C(B_s,B).
\end{equation}
Every block in the relevant suffix occurs in this sum, because the complete
blocks between it and~$B$ have inactivity product greater than~$\eta$.
After the transition, the suffix is trimmed according to the same rule.  Once
every non-root vertex has been placed, the terminal root block~$\{r\}$ is appended
using the cross-block part of~\cref{eq:dp-increment} and internal cost zero.

\begin{lemma}[Correctness and running time of the DP]\label{lem:local-dp}
For fixed $\delta$ and~$\eta$, the dynamic program minimizes
$F_\eta$ over all $\delta$-admissible ordered partitions and runs in time
$c_{\delta,\eta}^n\poly(n)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
When a block~$B$ is appended, the only new terms of~$F_\eta$ are its internal
term $I(B)$ and the cross-block terms whose right endpoint is~$B$.  These are
exactly the terms in~\cref{eq:dp-increment}.

If a previous block is not in the relevant suffix, then the complete blocks
between it and the current end have inactivity product at most~$\eta$.
Appending more blocks only multiplies this product by numbers in~$[0,1]$.
Hence a forgotten block can never occur in a future term of~$F_\eta$.  The
state therefore contains precisely the information needed to evaluate every
continuation, and the recurrence is an exact shortest-path computation over all
$\delta$-admissible partial partitions.

It remains to bound the number of states.  The upper bound in
\cref{eq:bernoulli-product-bounds} gives
\[
    \prod_{v\in S}(1-p_v)
    \le \exp\left(-\sum_{v\in S}p_v\right).
\]
If the whole prefix is retained, then its inactivity product exceeds
$\eta$, so its total activation mass is less than
$\log(1/\eta)$.  Otherwise, deleting the first retained block leaves an
inactivity product greater than~$\eta$, so the remaining blocks have total
activation mass less than $\log(1/\eta)$.  Every $\delta$-admissible block has mass at
most~$1$: a $\delta$-heavy block is a singleton of mass $p_v\le1$, while a light block
has mass at most~$\delta<1$.  Thus the total activation mass of a relevant
suffix is less than
\[
    M_\eta:=\log(1/\eta)+1.
\]

Every retained $\delta$-heavy singleton has mass greater than~$\delta/2$, and every
retained light block except the first one in a light run has mass at least
$\delta/2$.  Let $\mathcal G$ be the collection of these blocks.  Since their
total mass is less than $M_\eta$,
\[
    |\mathcal G|<2\frac{M_\eta}{\delta}.
\]
Every remaining retained block is the first light block of a light run.  The
number of light runs is at most one more than the number of retained $\delta$-heavy
blocks, and hence at most $|\mathcal G|+1$.  Therefore the number of retained
non-root-vertex blocks is at most
\[
    2|\mathcal G|+1
    <4\frac{M_\eta}{\delta}+1.
\]
Including the root block, the relevant suffix has at most
\[
    K:=\left\lceil4\frac{M_\eta}{\delta}\right\rceil+2
    =O\!\left(\frac{\log(1/\eta)+1}{\delta}\right)
\]
blocks.  A state can be encoded by assigning each non-root vertex one of the labels
``unplaced'', ``placed and forgotten'', or ``position $1,\dots,K$ in the
relevant suffix''.  Thus there are at most $(K+2)^n\poly(n)$ states.  A
transition may enumerate a subset of the unplaced light vertices, contributing
at most another factor~$2^n$, and all numerical terms can be evaluated in
polynomial time.  This gives a running time
$c_{\delta,\eta}^n\poly(n)$.  The recurrence
\cref{eq:internal-block-recurrence} for the values $I(B)$ takes
$O(n2^n)$ time and is absorbed in the same bound.
\end{proof}

\section{Approximation guarantee}\label{sec:dp-approximation}

The dynamic program minimizes $F_\eta$ exactly over $\delta$-admissible partitions.
It remains to choose the parameters so that the tour represented by this
minimum has cost at most $(1+\varepsilon)\optprior$.  Since the parameters
depend only on~$\varepsilon$, this choice will keep the running-time base
dependent only on~$\varepsilon$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:apriori-ptas}
For every fixed $0<\varepsilon\le1$, a~priori TSP on symmetric or asymmetric
metrics has a deterministic algorithm running in time
$c_\varepsilon^n\poly(n)$ and returning an a~priori tour~$\widehat T$ such that
\[
    \cost(\widehat T)\le(1+\varepsilon)\optprior.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Set
\[
    \gamma:=\frac{\varepsilon}{10},
    \qquad
    \delta:=\rho:=\gamma,
    \qquad
    \eta:=\frac{\gamma^2}{16}.
\]

Let $T^*$ be an optimal a~priori tour.  By
\cref{lem:block-objective-bounds}(ii), there is a $\delta$-admissible ordered partition
$\mathcal B^*$ into consecutive sets of~$T^*$ such that
\[
    F_\eta(\mathcal B^*)
    \le(1-\gamma)^{-2}\optprior.
\]
Let $\widehat{\mathcal B}$ be a partition minimizing $F_\eta$, as computed by
\cref{lem:local-dp}, and put
$\widehat T:=T_{\widehat{\mathcal B}}$.  Since $0<\gamma\le1/10$,
\cref{lem:block-objective-bounds}(i), the optimality of
$\widehat{\mathcal B}$, and the preceding bound,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:final-approximation-chain}
    \begin{aligned}
        \cost(\widehat T)
        &\le (1+\gamma)F_\eta(\widehat{\mathcal B})\\
        &\le (1+\gamma)F_\eta(\mathcal B^*)\\
        &\le (1+\gamma)(1-\gamma)^{-2}\optprior.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Using $(1-x)^{-1}\le1+2x$ for $0\le x\le1/2$, we obtain
\[
    (1+\gamma)(1-\gamma)^{-2}
    \le(1+\gamma)(1+2\gamma)^2
    \le1+6\gamma
    \le1+\varepsilon.
\]
Together with~\cref{eq:final-approximation-chain}, this proves the claimed
approximation guarantee.

Finally,
\[
    \log(1/\eta)
    =2\log\frac{4}{\gamma},
\]
which depends only on~$\varepsilon$.  Since $\delta$ also depends only on
$\varepsilon$, \cref{lem:local-dp} shows that
the running time remains $c_\varepsilon^n\poly(n)$.
\end{proof}

\section{Directions for future research}\label{sec:future-research}

The results of this thesis suggest several possible directions for subsequent
work.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Symmetric clairvoyance gap.}
    What is the correct supremum of the clairvoyance gap for symmetric \StochTSP{}?
    The three-chain construction gives a lower bound
    of~$10/9$.  The upper bound in \cref{thm:general-upper} applies when
    there is exactly one semi-active vertex, but a matching upper bound for
    unrestricted symmetric instances remains open.

    \item \textbf{Symmetric adaptivity gap.}
    Our probabilistic construction gives a lower bound of~$2$, while the
    obliviousness-gap upper bound of~$3$ also bounds the adaptivity gap.
    Is the adaptivity gap equal to~$2$, or can a different instance produce
    a larger separation?

    \item \textbf{Asymmetric adaptivity gap.}
    The rectangular grid construction gives a lower bound of
    $\Omega(n^{1/3}/\log n)$.  The $O(\sqrt n)$ upper bound on the
    asymmetric obliviousness gap from Christalla, Puhlmann, and
    Traub~\cite{Christalla2025} also bounds the adaptivity gap.  Can either
    bound be improved?

    \item \textbf{Asymmetric clairvoyance gap.}
    Our results place the clairvoyance gap between~$3/2$ and
    $O(n^{1/3})$.  The lower bound comes from the layered-column construction,

\chapter{Technical Proof for Pairwise Truncation}
\label{app:pairwise-truncation}

This appendix proves the charging estimate used in
\cref{lem:long-gap-truncation}.  We use the notation for the a~priori tour,
the probabilities~$p_i$, the distances~$d_{ij}$, and the products~$Q(i,j)$
from \cref{sec:dp-pairwise-objective}.

\begin{lemma}[Discarded-pair charging]
\label{lem:discarded-pair-charging}
For every a~priori tour~$T$ and every $\eta\in(0,1)$,
\[
    \cost(T)-\cost^{\loc}_{\eta}(T)
    \le
    \frac{\eta\bigl(3+\ln(1/\eta)\bigr)}{1-\eta}\cost(T).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write
\[
    w_{ij}:=p_ip_jQ(i,j),
    \qquad
    D_\eta:=\cost(T)-\cost^{\loc}_{\eta}(T).
\]
Thus $D_\eta$ is the total contribution of the discarded pairs, namely those
pairs $(i,j)$ for which $Q(i,j)\le\eta$.

Fix a discarded pair $(i,j)$.  Draw the activation states of the vertices
strictly between $v_i$ and~$v_j$ according to their product distribution,
conditioned on at least one of them being active.  The conditioning event has
probability
\[
    1-Q(i,j)\ge1-\eta.
\]
Together with $v_i$ and~$v_j$, the sampled active vertices form a path in
their order in~$T$; denote its edge set by~$P_{ij}$.  The triangle inequality
gives
\[
    d_{ij}\le\sum_{(a,b)\in P_{ij}}d_{ab}.
\]
We charge $w_{ij}d_{ij}$ to the edges of this random path.

Fix a target pair $(a,b)$ with $w_{ab}>0$, and let $\Gamma_{ab}$ be the
expected coefficient charged to~$d_{ab}$.  A source pair $(i,j)$ can charge
$(a,b)$ only if $i\le a<b\le j$.  The source $(a,b)$ itself contributes
nothing because the conditioning ensures that its sampled path contains an
active vertex strictly between its endpoints.  For any other containing
source, the unconditioned probability that $(a,b)$ is an edge of the sampled
path is
\[
    Q(a,b)\,
    p_a^{\1_{\{i<a\}}}
    p_b^{\1_{\{b<j\}}}.
\]
The occurrence of this edge already implies that the conditioning event holds,
so its conditional probability is the displayed quantity divided by
$1-Q(i,j)$.  Multiplying by~$w_{ij}$ and dividing by~$w_{ab}$ gives,
respectively, the three ratios
\[
    \frac{p_jQ(a,j)}{1-Q(a,j)},
    \qquad
    \frac{p_iQ(i,b)}{1-Q(i,b)},
    \qquad
    \frac{p_ip_jQ(i,j)}{1-Q(i,j)}
\]
when $(i,j)=(a,j)$ with $j>b$, when $(i,j)=(i,b)$ with $i<a$, and when
$i<a<b<j$.  Every source under consideration is discarded, so each
denominator is at least $1-\eta$.  Summing the three containment patterns
gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:appendix-truncation-target-congestion}
\frac{\Gamma_{ab}}{w_{ab}}
\le\frac{1}{1-\eta}
\left(
    \sum_{\substack{j>b\\Q(a,j)\le\eta}}p_jQ(a,j)
    +\sum_{\substack{i<a\\Q(i,b)\le\eta}}p_iQ(i,b)
    +\sum_{\substack{i<a,\,j>b\\Q(i,j)\le\eta}}p_ip_jQ(i,j)
\right).
\end{equation}
The first two sums are each at most~$\eta$.  Indeed, $Q(a,j)$ is
nonincreasing in~$j$, so the indices satisfying $Q(a,j)\le\eta$ form a
suffix.  If this suffix is nonempty, let $j_0$ be its first index.  For
$j\le n$,
\[
    p_jQ(a,j)=Q(a,j)-Q(a,j+1),
\]
while the final root satisfies
$p_{n+1}Q(a,n+1)=Q(a,n+1)$.  Therefore
\[
    \sum_{\substack{j>b\\Q(a,j)\le\eta}}p_jQ(a,j)
    =\sum_{j=j_0}^{n+1}p_jQ(a,j)
    =Q(a,j_0)
    \le\eta.
\]
Similarly, $Q(i,b)$ is nondecreasing in~$i$, so the indices in the second
sum form a prefix.  If $i_0$ is its last index, then
\[
    \sum_{\substack{i<a\\Q(i,b)\le\eta}}p_iQ(i,b)
    =\sum_{i=0}^{i_0}p_iQ(i,b)
    =Q(i_0,b)
    \le\eta,
\]
where the telescoping uses
$p_iQ(i,b)=Q(i,b)-Q(i-1,b)$ for $i\ge1$ and
$p_0Q(0,b)=Q(0,b)$.  An empty suffix or prefix contributes zero.

It remains to bound the third sum in
\cref{eq:appendix-truncation-target-congestion}.  Put
\[
    H:=(1-p_a)Q(a,b)(1-p_b),
    \qquad
    x_i:=Q(i,a),
    \qquad
    y_j:=Q(b,j).
\]
For $i<a<b<j$, we have $Q(i,j)=Hx_iy_j$.  If either outside index set is
empty, the third sum vanishes.  Otherwise, telescoping gives
\[
    \sum_{i<a}p_ix_i=1,
    \qquad
    \sum_{j>b}p_jy_j=1.
\]
For example, the first identity follows from
\[
    p_0Q(0,a)+\sum_{i=1}^{a-1}
       \bigl(Q(i,a)-Q(i-1,a)\bigr)
    =Q(a-1,a)=1,
\]
and the second is its right-hand analogue, including the terminal root.
If $H\le\eta$, the third sum is therefore at most $H\le\eta$.  Suppose
instead that $H>\eta$, and set $t:=\eta/H\in(0,1)$.  For every $z>0$,
the indices satisfying $y_j\le z$ form a suffix, so the same right-hand
telescoping gives
\[
    \sum_{\substack{j>b\\y_j\le z}}p_jy_j\le\min\{1,z\}.
\]
Consequently,
\begin{align*}
    \sum_{\substack{i<a,\,j>b\\Q(i,j)\le\eta}}p_ip_jQ(i,j)
    &=H\sum_{\substack{i<a\\x_i>0}}p_ix_i
        \sum_{\substack{j>b\\y_j\le t/x_i}}p_jy_j\\
    &\le H\sum_{i<a}p_i\min\{x_i,t\}.
\end{align*}

We use the following elementary survival-product estimate:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:appendix-survival-product-estimate}
    \sum_{i<a}p_i\min\{Q(i,a),t\}
    \le t\left(1+\ln\frac1t\right)
    \qquad(0<t<1).
\end{equation}
To verify it, recall that $x_i=Q(i,a)$ is nondecreasing in~$i$ and
$x_{i-1}=(1-p_i)x_i$.  If $x_0>t$, then
\[
    \sum_{i<a}p_i\min\{x_i,t\}
    =t\sum_{i<a}p_i
    \le t\left(1+\ln\frac1{x_0}\right)
    \le t\left(1+\ln\frac1t\right).
\]
Here
$\sum_{i<a}p_i\le1+\sum_{i=1}^{a-1}-\ln(1-p_i)
=1+\ln(1/x_0)$.
Otherwise, let~$\ell$ be the largest index for which $x_\ell\le t$, and put
$x:=x_{\ell+1}$ and $z:=x_\ell$.  Since
$z=(1-p_{\ell+1})x$, we have
\[
    \sum_{i=0}^{\ell}p_ix_i=z,
    \qquad
    \sum_{i=\ell+1}^{a-1}p_i
    \le1-\frac zx+\ln\frac1x.
\]
The first identity telescopes, while the second follows from
$p_{\ell+1}=1-z/x$ and
$p_i\le-\ln(1-p_i)$ for $i\ge\ell+2$.  Consequently,
\begin{align*}
    \sum_{i<a}p_i\min\{x_i,t\}
    &\le z+t\left(1-\frac{z}{x}+\ln\frac1x\right)\\
    &\le t\left(2-\frac{t}{x}+\ln\frac1x\right)\\
    &\le t\left(1+\ln\frac1t\right).
\end{align*}
The last inequality is equivalent to
$1-t/x+\ln(t/x)\le0$, which follows from $\ln u\le u-1$.  This proves
\cref{eq:appendix-survival-product-estimate}.

Applying the estimate, the third sum in
\cref{eq:appendix-truncation-target-congestion} is at most
\[
    Ht\left(1+\ln\frac1t\right)
    =\eta\left(1+\ln\frac{H}{\eta}\right)
    \le\eta\left(1+\ln\frac1\eta\right).
\]
It follows that every target pair has congestion at most
\[
    \beta(\eta):=\frac{\eta\bigl(3+\ln(1/\eta)\bigr)}{1-\eta}.
\]
Pairs with $w_{ab}=0$ receive no charge.  Indeed, if $p_a=0$ or $p_b=0$,
the target cannot appear as an active internal vertex, and a source having it
as an endpoint has zero weight.  If $Q(a,b)=0$, some vertex strictly between
$a$ and~$b$ is active with probability one, so $(a,b)$ cannot be an edge of
any sampled path.  We may therefore sum the congestion bound over all target
pairs to obtain
\[
    D_\eta\le\beta(\eta)\cost(T),
\]
which is the claimed inequality.
\end{proof}
